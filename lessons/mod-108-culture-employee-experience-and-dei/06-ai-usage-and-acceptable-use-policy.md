# 6. AI usage and acceptable-use policy

> The employee-facing rules that govern which AI tools the workforce may use, which data classes may be pasted into them, when a human must review the output, and when the use must be disclosed — drafted so the corporation neither strangles productivity with a blanket ban nor absorbs the confidentiality, IP, and regulator-disclosure exposure that unregulated shadow-AI use inevitably produces.

## Motivation

Generative-AI tools have moved from novelty to standard-issue productivity infrastructure inside eighteen months. The employees who used to Google their way through a task now paste it into a chat window. That change is a productivity multiplier in almost every function; it is also a category of confidentiality-and-IP exposure the corporation has not previously had to manage at the employee-behaviour layer.

The concrete failure modes recur:

- An account executive pastes a signed customer contract into a personal-tier chatbot to draft a summary email; the contract text — including customer identifiers, pricing, and a non-standard indemnity — is now training-corpus-adjacent under the personal-tier terms of service. The customer's security team asks a routine data-processing question in the next renewal cycle and the corporation cannot honestly answer.
- An engineer autocompletes a proprietary algorithm implementation using a personal-tier code assistant and ships it. The trained-on-public-code provenance of the completion is not documented; three quarters later, plaintiff's counsel in an unrelated litigation alleges the shipped code reproduces a copyrighted implementation and the corporation has no defensible provenance trail.
- A recruiting-operations analyst uses a personal-tier chatbot to score inbound candidate resumes against the job description. No one has audited the scoring for disparate impact; no one has told candidates about the automated decision; the corporation has walked into New York City Local Law 144 and EEOC exposure without a paper trail.
- A senior engineer at a corporation with active IPO preparation uses AI tools to draft internal memos discussing the S-1 process and material non-public information. The tool's retention policy is not enterprise-tier; the corporation now has an MNPI-in-third-party-hands problem it did not know it had, and the audit committee has a fresh disclosure question.

Each failure mode is avoidable through an operating discipline that this chapter builds: a **tiered framework** for which tools the workforce may use, a **data-class matrix** for which data classes may be pasted into which tier, a **human-in-the-loop requirement** for outputs that ship, a **disclosure architecture** for external and regulator settings, and a **discipline path** that escalates from coaching to termination through the standard progressive-discipline mechanics.

> **Legal-not-legal-advice caveat.** AI-usage policy sits at the intersection of confidentiality and IP-assignment obligations (mod-102, mod-109), data-classification and privacy regimes (mod-110), sector-specific regulator expectations (SEC, DOJ, EEOC, sector regulators), and a moving landscape of federal executive orders, state statutes (Colorado AI Act, California AB 2013, others in flight), and NIST / OMB guidance. This chapter names the frameworks and the operating posture; every corporation should have its AI-usage policy reviewed by counsel with current-cycle knowledge of the regulator landscape in its operating jurisdictions and its industry. See [resources.md](./resources.md) and [chapter 02](./02-employee-handbook-nlrb-compliant-post-stericycle.md) for the handbook-drafting pattern that houses this policy.

This chapter is the employee-behaviour policy. It is not the model-risk-management framework, the algorithmic-governance stack, or the red-teaming and evaluation methodology — those live in the AI-specific roles enumerated in the ownership-boundary section.

## 1. The four-tier tool framework

The core structural decision is a **tiered classification of AI tools** that maps every tool the workforce might reach for into one of four buckets, each carrying its own permission and data-handling posture. The framework is enumerated up-front in the policy so employees do not have to interpret it in the moment.

| Tier | Meaning | Example categories (illustrative — do not name vendors' current pricing tiers) | Permission posture |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Tier 1 — Approved, enterprise-licensed, general-purpose** | Tools the corporation has purchased under an enterprise or business agreement with (a) contractual no-training-on-inputs, (b) an executed DPA covering employee use, (c) SSO / audit-logging integration, (d) admin-level data-retention control | Enterprise chatbots; enterprise coding assistants; workspace-embedded AI features; enterprise transcription and meeting-notes tools | Available to all employees for the data classes enumerated in the data-class matrix below |
| **Tier 2 — Approved with data restrictions** | Tools approved for a specified subset of use cases, or with data-class restrictions tighter than Tier 1 (e.g., approved for public-data brainstorming but not for customer data), or approved under a departmental licence rather than a company-wide agreement | Sector-specific research tools; design-generation tools; audio / video generation tools; analytics-assist tools; certain search-augmented tools | Available under the restrictions listed on the internal approved-tools registry |
| **Tier 3 — Restricted to specific use cases and approvers** | Tools that are permitted only for a specifically-scoped experimental, evaluation, or vendor-diligence use case; require named-approver sign-off; time-boxed; often subject to an evaluation report at the end | New tool the security team is evaluating; a Tier-4-adjacent tool needed for a one-off vendor-diligence exercise; a research spike that requires model-fine-tuning access | Available only to the named approver's team, only for the sanctioned use case, only for the time-boxed evaluation window |
| **Tier 4 — Forbidden** | Tools the corporation has determined it will not permit — either because of unresolved security concerns, regulator-jurisdiction concerns, vendor-country concerns, terms-of-service that assert broad rights over inputs, or an unacceptable retention posture | Consumer-tier accounts of tools where an enterprise tier exists; tools with training-on-inputs by default; tools with data-residency incompatible with regulator regime; tools flagged by the security team | Not permitted for any work purpose; use is a material-breach event under the discipline path |

### Movement criteria between tiers

The tier assignment is not permanent. The security team, in coordination with legal and the head of AI (or the general counsel where an AI executive is not yet in role), reviews the approved-tools registry on a documented cadence — quarterly at minimum, ad-hoc on trigger events. The registry itself is not an artefact of one person's memory; it is a versioned document with an owner, a review-due date on every entry, and a change log that captures every promotion or demotion between tiers.

The criteria that move a tool between tiers:

- **Enterprise-licence execution.** A tool that has been Tier 4 or Tier 3 during evaluation moves to Tier 1 or Tier 2 once (a) the enterprise agreement is signed, (b) the DPA covers the workforce's use case, (c) the tenant has been provisioned with SSO and audit logging, (d) admin data-retention controls are configured. See [mod-112](../mod-112-enterprise-risk-insurance-and-compliance/) for the vendor-security-review and DPA-execution mechanics.
- **Terms-of-service change.** A tool whose ToS materially changes — new training-on-inputs default, changed retention posture, new data-residency terms, changed sub-processor list — is re-reviewed. Movement can be in either direction.
- **Regulator-jurisdiction change.** A tool subject to a new export-control designation, a new state statute (Colorado AI Act, California AB 2013 provisions, others), or a new sector-regulator posture (SEC, HHS OCR, FTC, sector regulators) is re-reviewed. <!-- needs-research: catalogue current-cycle state AI statutes and their private-employer scope; the state landscape is moving quarter-to-quarter. -->
- **Security incident.** A tool involved in a security incident — data leak, prompt-injection compromise, credential exposure — is moved to Tier 3 or Tier 4 pending investigation and remediation.
- **Vendor viability.** A tool whose vendor has been acquired, sanctioned, or is subject to material regulatory action is re-reviewed.

The registry lists, for each tool: the current tier, the assignment date, the approving reviewers, the permitted data classes, the permitted use cases, the retention posture, the sub-processor list at the vendor, and the review-due date. It is published on the internal wiki and linked from the AI-usage policy chapter of the handbook. Employees who cannot find a tool on the registry — because it is new, because they encountered it in a customer-supplied workflow, because a colleague forwarded it — treat the tool as Tier 4 pending a security review. "Not on the registry" is the same operating posture as "forbidden" until a reviewer has made a call.

## 2. Data-class constraints on model inputs

The tier framework governs *which tool* the employee may reach for. The **data-class matrix** governs *what may be pasted into the tool*. The two must be read together.

The data-classification scheme itself — public, internal, confidential, restricted, regulated — is maintained in [mod-110 (Privacy, data governance and sector compliance)](../mod-110-privacy-data-governance-and-sector-compliance/). The AI-usage policy adopts that scheme as its authoritative reference and maps each class to a permitted-tier posture.

| Data class | Description (see mod-110 for canonical definition) | Tier 1 (enterprise) | Tier 2 (approved with restriction) | Tier 3 (restricted use case) | Tier 4 (forbidden) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Public** | Marketing-cleared content, public docs, published blog posts | Permitted | Permitted | Permitted only for the sanctioned use case | Not permitted (no tool use at all) |
| **Internal** | Non-sensitive internal docs (org charts published internally, general roadmap statements, non-restricted OKRs) | Permitted | Permitted subject to per-tool restriction on the registry | Permitted only for the sanctioned use case | Not permitted |
| **Confidential** | Non-public product plans, unreleased code, financial data not yet reported, internal strategy documents | Permitted with logging; do not paste MNPI (see below) | Not permitted unless the tool is specifically approved for confidential data | Permitted only if the sanctioned use case specifically covers confidential data | Not permitted |
| **Customer data** | Any data belonging to a customer or received under a customer NDA; customer contract terms; customer support tickets | Permitted only in the enterprise tenant configured for that customer's data-processing obligations; per-customer DPA restrictions apply | Not permitted | Not permitted absent explicit customer consent captured in the sanctioning approval | Not permitted |
| **PII (personal information)** | Employee data, candidate data, customer end-user PII; see mod-110 for the CCPA / GDPR / state-privacy scope | Permitted only where the enterprise agreement's DPA covers the processing purpose; PII-of-employees restricted to HRIS-integrated tools where possible | Not permitted | Not permitted absent an explicit privacy review | Not permitted |
| **PHI (protected health information)** | Health data covered by HIPAA (if the corporation is a covered entity or business associate) or state health-data statutes | Permitted only in a tool covered by a Business Associate Agreement; see mod-110 and sector-compliance chapters | Not permitted | Not permitted | Not permitted |
| **MNPI (material non-public information)** | For public companies or corporations in registration: information that would be material to an investor and has not been publicly disclosed | Not permitted in any tool absent explicit legal / disclosure-committee approval; the safer default is not to paste MNPI into any AI tool | Not permitted | Not permitted | Not permitted |
| **Regulated / export-controlled** | Data covered by ITAR, EAR, OFAC sanctions, controlled-unclassified-information regimes, sector-specific data (CJIS, IRS 1075, others) | Not permitted absent an explicit sector-compliance review; most enterprise general-purpose tools are not certified for these regimes | Not permitted | Not permitted | Not permitted |
| **Attorney-client privileged** | Communications with counsel, work product prepared at counsel's direction | Not permitted absent GC sign-off; pasting privileged material into a third-party tool risks privilege waiver | Not permitted | Not permitted | Not permitted |

The matrix is the operating heart of the policy. Employees who cannot immediately classify the data they are working with should default to the more restrictive posture and consult the security or legal team. The registry entry for each tool restates the data-class posture for that specific tool in plain language.

Two operating notes on the matrix:

- **Inference is data.** A prompt that does not literally contain a customer name but that would let a reasonable reader infer the customer (a description of a distinctive integration, a paraphrased pricing structure that appears in only one deal, a quoted product-roadmap item) is still customer data for the purposes of the matrix. The rule of thumb: if the output would be embarrassing to publish on the corporation's blog, the input is not "public" for AI-tool purposes.
- **Aggregation raises the class.** Fifty records that are each low-sensitivity in isolation can, in aggregate, constitute a confidential or PII dataset. Bulk paste of many records is treated at the highest class present in the aggregation, not at the class of the individual record.

## 3. Human-in-the-loop review requirements

AI output is a draft, not a decision. The policy enumerates the categories of AI-assisted work that require human review before the output is shipped, lands in a system of record, or is acted upon. The reviewer is the accountable human — the employee whose name is on the artifact — not a delegated approver.

- **AI-generated code.** Every AI-generated code contribution goes through the standard code-review process (see the engineering handbook and, where applicable, the SDLC governance in `chief-ai-officer-learning` and `ai-risk-engineer-learning`). The author reads the generated code, understands what it does, and takes responsibility for the semantics. Auto-committed AI output without human read-through is not permitted. Where AI-generated code interacts with a security-sensitive surface (auth, crypto, data handling, third-party API keys), a second-reviewer sign-off is required.
- **AI-generated customer-facing content.** Marketing copy, sales emails, customer-support responses, help-centre articles, and documentation that will be seen by a customer or a prospect go through the ordinary editorial or content-review cadence. The author is responsible for accuracy, tone, and any product claims. AI-generated content that makes a factual claim about the product or a numerical claim about performance must be independently verified against the underlying source.
- **AI-generated hiring and employment decisions.** Any AI-assisted screening, ranking, or evaluation of candidates or employees — resume screening, interview scoring, promotion recommendation, RIF-selection input — is subject to the algorithmic-governance stack owned by `chief-ai-officer-learning` and the specific compliance regimes (New York City **Local Law 144** bias-audit requirement; EEOC guidance on AI in employment decisions; state analogues). This chapter does not adjudicate those requirements. Employee-facing rule: do not use an AI tool to score, rank, or reject a candidate or an employee absent an explicit sign-off from the head of people and the algorithmic-governance owner. <!-- needs-research: confirm current-cycle NYC Local Law 144 audit-scope guidance and the state-level AI-in-hiring landscape (California, Illinois AI Video Interview Act, others). -->
- **AI-generated regulated decisions.** Credit decisions, medical decisions, insurance-underwriting decisions, and other decisions subject to sector-specific regulator regimes require review under the applicable regulator's expectations. Where the corporation is not itself a covered entity, the policy still requires review of any AI-generated output that will be relied upon in a regulated downstream decision.
- **AI-generated legal and financial work-product.** AI drafts of legal documents, financial models, disclosures, or filings are reviewed by the accountable licensed professional (counsel, controller, CFO, external auditor) before the document is used, filed, or relied upon. AI drafts touching MNPI or disclosure-committee-scope material follow the additional MNPI rule in the data-class matrix above.
- **AI-generated internal decisions with material consequence.** Any AI-assisted output that will be used to make an internal decision with material consequence — a compensation change, a performance-management determination, a supplier selection — is subject to the same human-in-the-loop discipline as customer-facing content.

The pattern to internalise: **the human is accountable for the output, not the tool**. The policy does not require the employee to justify every use of AI; it requires the employee to stand behind the output as if the employee had produced it unaided. "The model said so" is not a defence in a code-review escalation, a customer complaint, a regulator inquiry, or an internal-decision post-mortem.

The interaction with the PIIA (proprietary-information and inventions agreement) executed at hire — see [mod-102 (Founding-team legal architecture)](../mod-102-founding-team-legal-architecture/) — is worth calling out. The PIIA assigns the corporation ownership of employee-created work; AI-assisted work created in the course of employment is employee-created work for these purposes. The employee's use of an AI tool does not transfer IP to the tool vendor (Tier 1 enterprise agreements are specifically negotiated to prevent this), and does not free the employee from the assignment obligation. The employee's obligation to disclose prior inventions, third-party rights, and open-source dependencies runs through AI-assisted work the same as through any other work-product.

## 4. Disclosure requirements

Disclosure of AI use is context-dependent. The policy enumerates the disclosure requirements at three altitudes.

### 4a. External disclosure — customers and prospects

- **Customer contract compliance.** Where the customer's contract with the corporation prohibits or restricts the use of AI tools in producing deliverables (a growing pattern in enterprise-customer procurement), that restriction is authoritative. Employees working on a customer engagement consult the customer-specific contract terms — captured in the CRM per [mod-109](../mod-109-commercial-contracts-ip-and-legal-ops/) — before using AI tools in producing customer deliverables.
- **"Material AI use" disclosure norm.** For customer-facing deliverables where AI has materially shaped the output (a strategy memo, a code deliverable, a design), the default posture is transparent disclosure of the AI-assisted nature of the work when asked, and proactive disclosure when the deliverable's form would lead a reasonable customer to assume it was produced unaided.
- **Sales and marketing claims.** Statements to prospects about the corporation's own use of AI, or about its product's AI capabilities, are subject to the ordinary marketing-review process; over-claiming AI capability is now a live FTC and litigation-plaintiff surface. <!-- needs-research: confirm current-cycle FTC posture on AI claims (Operation AI Comply, endorsement-guide interaction) and adjust the marketing-review guidance accordingly. -->
- **RFP and security-questionnaire responses.** Customer and prospect security questionnaires increasingly ask specific questions about the corporation's AI use — which vendors, which models, which retention posture, which sub-processors, which countries. The security team maintains a canonical response repository; individual account teams do not answer these questions freehand.

### 4b. Internal disclosure — code review, doc review, decision review

- **Code review.** Pull-request descriptions include a note when AI-generated code is a material portion of the change. The reviewer knows to look for the specific failure modes that AI-generated code exhibits (hallucinated APIs, subtle security issues, inconsistent style, plausible-but-wrong references).
- **Doc review.** Internal documents with material AI-drafted content are marked as such in the review cadence. The reviewer knows to apply the same accuracy-and-source discipline as with customer-facing content.
- **Decision documentation.** Where an AI tool was used as an input to an internal decision (a hiring memo, a comp change, a supplier evaluation), the decision documentation notes the tool used and the human review performed. The paper trail matters if the decision is later contested.

### 4c. Regulator disclosure

- **SEC disclosure (public companies and companies in registration).** AI use may now appear in the risk-factors section of the 10-K, in the MD&A discussion of operational risk, and in specific disclosures where AI is material to the business or to disclosure controls. The 2024 SEC enforcement wave on "AI-washing" — over-stating AI capability in filings and investor communications — is the visible enforcement posture. Corporations in registration or in periodic-reporting cadence should coordinate any AI-related disclosure with the disclosure committee, external counsel, and the auditor. <!-- needs-research: confirm current SEC C&DI (Compliance and Disclosure Interpretations) on AI-related disclosure, current AI-washing enforcement postures, and any new SEC rulemaking on AI disclosure before quoting current-year specifics. -->
- **DOJ Evaluation of Corporate Compliance Programs (ECCP).** The DOJ's ECCP guidance — the framework prosecutors use to evaluate the effectiveness of a compliance programme when making charging and resolution decisions — has been updated to specifically address the corporation's use of AI, including the corporation's controls over AI misuse, its assessment of AI-related risk, and its ability to detect and respond to AI-related incidents. <!-- needs-research: confirm current-cycle ECCP guidance language on AI (2024 update and any subsequent revisions); the DOJ posture is being actively refined. -->
- **Sector regulator expectations.** Sector regulators — banking regulators, HHS OCR, HHS OIG, state insurance regulators, FDA (for health-tech), FINRA (for broker-dealers), FTC (for consumer-facing products and unfair-and-deceptive-practice reach) — have published AI-specific guidance at varying levels of formality. Corporations in a regulated sector should confirm their sector's current AI-guidance posture with the sector-compliance owner. See [mod-110](../mod-110-privacy-data-governance-and-sector-compliance/) and [mod-112](../mod-112-enterprise-risk-insurance-and-compliance/).
- **State AI statutes.** Colorado's AI Act, California AB 2013 and related provisions, New York City Local Law 144, and other state-and-local AI statutes carry their own disclosure obligations — to consumers, to candidates, to regulators. The applicable-statute inventory is maintained with the sector-compliance owner. <!-- needs-research: catalogue current-cycle state AI statutes with disclosure obligations; the state layer is moving quarterly. -->

Disclosure is not optional. An employee who is uncertain whether a use of AI triggers a disclosure obligation escalates to the compliance or legal team before proceeding.

## 5. Enterprise-licence versus personal-licence

The default operating decision for a corporation handling any confidential, customer, PII, or regulated data is: **AI tools are used under an enterprise licence, provisioned by the corporation, integrated with SSO, and covered by an executed DPA**. Personal-tier accounts are, in almost every category, not appropriate for corporate work.

The reasoning is not about the underlying model quality — the enterprise and personal tiers of the same vendor's product often expose the same model. The reasoning is about the operating envelope around the model:

- **Training on inputs.** Personal-tier terms of service commonly permit the vendor to use inputs to train future models. Enterprise-tier terms commonly do not. Confidential and customer data pasted into a personal tier is now training-corpus-adjacent under the vendor's ToS; the corporation cannot honestly represent to a customer that their data was not used to train a third-party model.
- **Retention.** Enterprise tiers offer admin-controlled retention (30-day, zero-retention, or configurable). Personal tiers commonly retain inputs and outputs on the vendor's timeline, not the corporation's.
- **DPA execution.** Enterprise tiers come with a data-processing agreement that the corporation's legal team has reviewed and executed. Personal-tier use is governed by consumer-tier ToS with no DPA overlay.
- **SSO and audit logging.** Enterprise tiers integrate with the corporation's IdP (Okta, Entra, Google Workspace) and produce audit logs the security team can query. Personal-tier use is invisible to corporate telemetry.
- **Tenant isolation.** Enterprise tiers isolate the corporation's tenant from other customers' tenants and from the vendor's training pipeline. Personal tiers do not offer this guarantee.
- **Discovery posture.** In a dispute or investigation, the corporation can produce the interaction history from an enterprise-tenant tool. It cannot produce (or defensibly attest to) the interaction history from a personal-tier tool an employee used on their own account.

### Authorising a personal-tier use

There are narrow, legitimate reasons for personal-tier use — a public-data research task, a hobby project the employee is doing on personal time and equipment that touches no corporate data. The policy enumerates the mechanism for the exceptional case:

- The employee submits a request to the security team naming the tool, the intended use, the data classes involved (public only), and the duration.
- The security team reviews against the tier framework and either (a) approves as a Tier 3 sanctioned use, (b) declines and points the employee to a Tier 1 or Tier 2 equivalent, or (c) escalates to legal for a novel-use call.
- Approval is documented in the registry; the employee is on record as operating within an authorised exception.

### The shadow-AI problem

The policy is honest about the operating reality: **absent an approved, easy-to-use Tier 1 stack, employees will use personal-tier tools whether or not the policy permits it**. This is not primarily a discipline problem; it is a product problem. The countermeasures:

- **Provide a good Tier 1 default.** The security and IT teams provision enterprise-tier general-purpose tools that cover 80%+ of employee use cases. Onboarding (see [mod-104](../mod-104-hiring-onboarding-and-hr-operations/)) includes AI-tools setup and training.
- **Publish the approved list.** The registry is easy to find, easy to search, and includes the "why this tool, why not that one" reasoning.
- **Detection.** The security team runs periodic detection sweeps — SaaS-discovery tooling, DNS logs, browser-extension inventories, expense-report analysis for AI-tool subscriptions — to surface personal-tier or unapproved-tool use. Detected use triggers a conversation, not an immediate discipline event, on first occurrence.
- **Amnesty and reporting.** An employee who has been using a personal-tier tool and wants to move to an approved posture can self-report to the security team without discipline consequence on first occurrence, provided no confidential data was involved. This is a deliberate choice to prioritise migration over enforcement in the initial policy-adoption period.
- **Manager visibility.** Managers are trained to notice the signal — an employee who describes an unusual workflow, an artefact that looks unlike the employee's ordinary work-product, a subscription showing up on an expense report. The manager's role is not to be the tool police; the manager's role is to route the conversation to the security team where the workflow raises a policy question the manager cannot answer.
- **Corporate-card and expense controls.** Finance flags AI-tool subscription charges on personal or corporate cards and cross-references against the approved-tools registry. Reimbursement for a personal-tier AI subscription is declined; reimbursement for an approved-tier tool the employee purchased on their own initiative is routed to the security team for tenant consolidation.
- **Endpoint and network telemetry.** The security team's endpoint-detection tooling and DNS-level telemetry produce a periodic report of AI-tool traffic. The report is not employee-surveillance content; it is aggregated tool-usage data used to size the enterprise-tier stack and to detect Tier 4 use. Employee-monitoring disclosures in the handbook privacy chapter cover the collection and use of this telemetry.

## 6. Interaction with SEC, DOJ, and regulator disclosure regimes

The regulator-disclosure landscape for AI use is genuinely moving. The policy does not attempt to encode the current-cycle rules; it names the interfaces and points at the owners.

- **SEC.** For a public company or a company in registration, the disclosure committee owns the question of whether and how AI use appears in filings. Material AI use in the corporation's operations, material AI capability in the product, material AI-related risks — each may trigger a 10-K risk-factor disclosure, an MD&A discussion, or a specific filing-response. The AI-washing enforcement wave has raised the stakes on over-claiming; the corresponding under-disclosure of material AI dependency is also actionable. <!-- needs-research: confirm current SEC posture on AI-related disclosure — C&DI updates, enforcement postures, any new rulemaking — before this section is used as an operating reference. -->
- **DOJ ECCP.** The Evaluation of Corporate Compliance Programs guidance is the framework prosecutors use to evaluate compliance-programme effectiveness in charging and resolution decisions. Recent ECCP updates specifically address AI-related risk, controls, and detection. A corporation subject to a DOJ investigation or in a self-disclosure posture is expected to be able to describe its AI-usage policy, its detection mechanisms, its incident-response history, and its training cadence. <!-- needs-research: confirm current-cycle ECCP guidance on AI (2024 update and any subsequent revisions). -->
- **Sector regulators.** Banking (OCC, FDIC, Federal Reserve interagency AI guidance), health (HHS OCR HIPAA-and-AI guidance, FDA on AI-enabled medical devices), consumer (FTC on AI and unfair-and-deceptive practices, state AG postures on AI in consumer products), insurance (NAIC AI model bulletin, state insurance-department guidance) — each maintains sector-specific AI expectations. Corporations in a regulated sector maintain a sector-guidance inventory with the sector-compliance owner. See [mod-110](../mod-110-privacy-data-governance-and-sector-compliance/).
- **State AI statutes.** Colorado's AI Act (effective 2026, private-employer application), California AB 2013 (training-data disclosure), New York City Local Law 144 (automated employment-decision-tool bias audits), Illinois AI Video Interview Act, and a growing list of state-and-local statutes. Applicable-statute tracking is a rolling exercise. <!-- needs-research: catalogue current-cycle state AI statutes with private-employer application, effective dates, and disclosure obligations. -->
- **Federal executive orders and NIST / OMB guidance.** The federal executive-order layer on AI has moved multiple times in the current cycle; NIST's AI Risk Management Framework and OMB's federal-agency AI guidance shape the expected posture for federal contractors and, indirectly, the compliance-benchmark for private employers. <!-- needs-research: confirm current federal EO status on AI, current NIST AI RMF version, current OMB AI guidance for federal agencies. -->
- **Cross-border and EU AI Act.** For a corporation with EU-based employees, EU-based customers, or products offered in the EU, the EU AI Act adds its own overlay — risk-tier classification of AI systems, prohibited-use categories, general-purpose-model obligations, and workplace-AI-specific transparency requirements. The EU AI Act's phased applicability schedule and its interaction with GDPR live in [mod-110](../mod-110-privacy-data-governance-and-sector-compliance/). <!-- needs-research: confirm current-cycle EU AI Act applicability schedule and the specific workplace-AI transparency provisions applicable to non-EU corporations with EU-based workforce. -->

The operating discipline: the AI-usage policy is version-controlled and reviewed on the same cadence as the handbook (see [chapter 02](./02-employee-handbook-nlrb-compliant-post-stericycle.md)); regulator-cycle updates trigger an ad-hoc policy update; material policy updates trigger a workforce re-acknowledgment.

## 7. Training, onboarding and refresh cadence

A policy that lives only on the wiki is not the same as a policy the workforce operates. The AI-usage policy is embedded in the corporation's training cadence at three points.

- **Onboarding.** Every new hire completes an AI-usage-policy module during their first week, delivered through the corporation's LMS (see [mod-104](../mod-104-hiring-onboarding-and-hr-operations/)). The module walks through the tier framework, the data-class matrix, the human-in-the-loop rules, the disclosure obligations, the enterprise-tier tools the employee already has access to, and the amnesty-and-reporting posture. Completion is a Day-1 handbook-acknowledgment checklist item.
- **Annual refresh.** Every employee completes an annual refresh in the same cadence as the handbook re-acknowledgment (see [chapter 02](./02-employee-handbook-nlrb-compliant-post-stericycle.md)). The refresh includes the delta from the prior version and any new worked examples that came out of incidents in the trailing twelve months.
- **Role-specific supplements.** Employees in roles with elevated AI exposure — engineering, product, sales, recruiting, legal, finance, communications — receive a role-specific supplement to the general training. The engineering supplement covers the code-review integration, the security-sensitive-surface second-reviewer rule, and provenance-tracking for AI-generated code. The recruiting supplement covers the NYC Local Law 144 posture and the head-of-people sign-off requirement for any AI-assisted candidate scoring. The legal and finance supplements cover the privilege and MNPI rules.

Training completion is tracked in the LMS and is one of the operational metrics reviewed in the compliance dashboard the general counsel maintains for the audit committee (see [mod-112](../mod-112-enterprise-risk-insurance-and-compliance/)).

The training itself is written in the same voice as this chapter — plain language, worked examples, an honest acknowledgement of the tension between the productivity value of AI tools and the operating envelope the corporation has to maintain. Employees who feel the policy is written to enable their work — with clear defaults, a discoverable registry, and an amnesty posture on honest mistakes — comply with it in a materially different mode than employees who feel the policy is written to punish them.

## 8. The discipline path

Enforcement of the AI-usage policy runs through the standard progressive-discipline framework in the handbook's discipline chapter (see [chapter 02](./02-employee-handbook-nlrb-compliant-post-stericycle.md), Chapter 14 of the reference table-of-contents, and [mod-107 chapter 05](../mod-107-performance-promotion-and-offboarding/05-termination-playbook.md) for the termination-playbook mechanics that pick up at the end of the escalation).

The tiers of severity:

- **First-occurrence, low-severity.** Personal-tier use of an approved-category tool for public data; use of a Tier 2 tool for a use case the employee should have checked against the registry; a documentation gap on a code contribution. Response: a coaching conversation with the manager and the security team; the employee is directed to the approved-tools registry and, where appropriate, offered a walkthrough of the enterprise-tier equivalent. No formal discipline record. The amnesty-and-reporting posture in Section 5 above applies.
- **Repeat-occurrence.** A second occurrence of the same pattern, or a first occurrence involving confidential data (customer data, PII, unreleased product plans) into a personal-tier or Tier 4 tool. Response: a documented warning under the handbook's progressive-discipline framework; mandatory re-training on the AI-usage policy; the manager and the HRBP document the conversation.
- **Material breach.** A single incident involving MNPI, regulated data, PHI, attorney-client-privileged material, or a large volume of customer data pasted into a personal-tier or Tier 4 tool; use of AI in a hiring, employment, or regulated decision without the required sign-off; deliberate circumvention of the tiered framework (e.g., using a personal device to bypass corporate telemetry). Response: an investigation under the handbook's investigations process (see [chapter 04](./04-engagement-measurement-and-action-planning.md) sibling on the harassment-investigations pattern for the general process posture); depending on findings, discipline up to and including termination for cause per [mod-107 chapter 05](../mod-107-performance-promotion-and-offboarding/05-termination-playbook.md); parallel workstreams on incident response, customer notification, and regulator disclosure as applicable.
- **Repeat material breach or wilful violation.** Termination for cause is the default; parallel referral to counsel on any restrictive-covenant or downstream-remedy questions.

The discipline path is not a scorecard the employee will read line-by-line. It is the framework the manager and the HRBP apply consistently across incidents, so that discipline is not arbitrary and the corporation's response to a given incident is defensible on review.

Two operating principles hold across the tiers:

- **Retaliation prohibition.** An employee who reports an AI-usage incident — their own or a colleague's — in good faith is protected from retaliation under the corporation's anti-retaliation policy in the handbook. The self-reporting posture only works if employees believe they will not be punished for the report itself.
- **NLRA Section 7 interaction.** The AI-usage policy, like every workplace rule in the handbook, is drafted with the Stericycle framework in mind (see [chapter 02](./02-employee-handbook-nlrb-compliant-post-stericycle.md)). Nothing in the policy is intended to restrict employees' Section 7 rights, including concerted discussion of working conditions. The policy narrowly restricts what may be pasted into third-party tools; it does not restrict employees' discussion of the policy itself, the corporation's AI strategy, or the effect of AI on employees' work.

## Concrete example: the customer-contract-into-personal-ChatGPT walkthrough

A hypothetical 150-person Series-B B2B SaaS corporation, headquartered in California, with employees across CA, NY, CO, and WA. The corporation's AI-usage policy has been in effect for six months; the Tier 1 stack includes an enterprise-licensed general-purpose chatbot, an enterprise-licensed coding assistant, and workspace-embedded AI features in the corporation's productivity suite.

**Day 0 — the incident.** An account executive is preparing a renewal-strategy memo for a Tier-1 enterprise customer. The customer's redlined MSA came back with a non-standard indemnity, a pricing rebate structure, and a termination-for-convenience carve-out. To draft an internal summary email for the account team quickly, the AE pastes the redlined MSA text — approximately 40 pages, including the customer's legal-entity name, deal-specific pricing, and the non-standard indemnity language — into a personal-tier chatbot the AE has been using for personal tasks. The chatbot returns a competent summary; the AE forwards it to the account team.

**Day 3 — detection.** The security team's SaaS-discovery tooling flags the domain of the personal-tier chatbot in the AE's browser telemetry. The security engineer investigating the flag pulls the corporation's approved-tools registry (the personal-tier is not on it), correlates the browsing session to a large-paste event, and confirms with the AE in a Slack DM that the AE did use the tool for a work task.

**Day 3–5 — incident triage.**

- The AE, the AE's manager, the head of security, the general counsel, and the HRBP are convened.
- The AE describes the incident candidly (per the amnesty-and-reporting posture, the corporation's operating stance is to prioritise honest disclosure and remediation over adversarial posture on first-occurrence use).
- The security team confirms which content was pasted (from the AE's browser history and the AE's account-side chat history where retrievable), the retention posture of the personal-tier tool (the tool retains inputs for 30 days by default), and whether the vendor's ToS permits training on inputs (it does, absent an opted-out setting the AE did not enable).
- Legal reviews the customer's MSA for data-handling obligations, notification requirements, and the corporation's contractual posture on sub-processor use. The MSA includes a data-processing addendum that requires notification for any incident involving customer data in a non-approved sub-processor.

**Day 5–10 — response.**

- **Vendor-side remediation.** The corporation contacts the personal-tier vendor's support, requests deletion of the AE's account and all associated conversation history, and receives written confirmation of deletion within the tool's operating SLA.
- **Customer notification.** The corporation's account team, with legal in the loop, notifies the customer per the MSA's data-processing-addendum obligation. The notification is factual, describes the incident, describes the remediation, describes the corporation's AI-usage policy and how this incident was outside the approved posture, and offers a call with the customer's security team.
- **Internal remediation.** The AE completes mandatory re-training on the AI-usage policy. The AE's discipline record includes a documented warning per the progressive-discipline framework (first occurrence involving confidential customer data — this falls into the "repeat-occurrence" severity tier because the data class involved was customer data, notwithstanding the AE's first-occurrence status).
- **Policy adjustment.** The security team reviews whether the Tier 1 stack was in fact sufficient for the AE's use case — a fast summary of a long contract is a legitimate need. The team confirms the enterprise-tier chatbot supports the use case and adds a specific "summarise a contract" workflow to the AI-onboarding curriculum. The team also adjusts the SaaS-discovery detection cadence to catch similar patterns faster.
- **Regulator posture.** Legal assesses whether the incident triggers any regulator-disclosure obligation. In this hypothetical, the customer's data is not PHI, PII beyond the customer's legal-entity name and the AE's own name, or MNPI; no regulator-notification obligation attaches, but the incident is logged in the corporation's compliance-incident register per [mod-112](../mod-112-enterprise-risk-insurance-and-compliance/).

**Day 10–15 — incident classification.**

- The head of security and the general counsel formally classify the incident under the discipline path in Section 8: first-occurrence-with-customer-data, escalating from the low-severity coaching tier to the repeat-occurrence documented-warning tier because the data class involved was customer data.
- The AE's manager, coached by the HRBP, delivers the documented warning in a private one-on-one, describes the specific behaviour, describes the specific policy provision, and describes what the expected behaviour looks like going forward. The AE signs an acknowledgement of the conversation.
- The AE's PIP is not triggered — the incident is a policy-violation event, not a performance-management event, and conflating the two would both muddy the discipline record and undermine the AE's downstream managerial coaching (see [mod-107 chapter 04](../mod-107-performance-promotion-and-offboarding/) on the PIP-versus-discipline distinction).

**Day 30 — post-mortem.**

- The head of security, the head of people, the general counsel, and the customer's account owner debrief the incident against the AI-usage policy, the training curriculum, the Tier 1 stack, and the detection posture.
- The debrief produces two action items: (a) a communications refresher to the workforce reminding employees of the enterprise-tier tools available for common use cases and of the amnesty-and-reporting posture, (b) an update to the AI-usage policy adding a specific worked example on customer-contract handling.
- The customer, satisfied by the notification and the remediation, does not escalate. The renewal proceeds on the previously-agreed terms.

**What went right.** Detection was fast. The response was structured, not panicked. The employee was treated per the progressive-discipline framework — not made an example of, not let off entirely. The customer was told the truth. The policy was updated with the lessons.

**What could have gone wrong.** If the pasted content had been MNPI (say, the AE was preparing for an IPO-adjacent conversation), the incident would have escalated to a disclosure-committee event with 10-K risk-factor consequences. If the AE had lied about the incident when confronted, the discipline posture would have escalated to termination for wilful violation under the "material breach" tier. If the personal-tier tool's ToS had asserted broader rights over inputs, the corporation's customer-notification would have been more difficult to script defensibly. If the customer's MSA had required regulator notification (a common pattern in health, financial-services, and government-adjacent customer contracts), the incident would have triggered a parallel regulator-notification workstream with its own clock. If the corporation's cyber-insurance carrier had a specific notification obligation for third-party-tool-related incidents, that clock would have run in parallel too (see [mod-112](../mod-112-enterprise-risk-insurance-and-compliance/) for the insurance-notification mechanics).

The lesson: the tier framework, the data-class matrix, the amnesty-and-reporting posture, and the discipline path all did the work they were designed to do. The policy was not a document that lived on a wiki page unread; it was the operating framework the corporation actually ran the incident through.

## Ownership boundary

This chapter owns:

- The **employee-behaviour rules** for which AI tools the workforce may use, which data classes may be pasted into which tools, what human review is required before output ships, and what disclosure obligations attach at the individual-employee level.
- The **tier framework** and the **data-class matrix** as the employee-facing operating structures.
- The **discipline path** for policy violations, integrated with the handbook's progressive-discipline framework and the termination-playbook mechanics.
- The **amnesty-and-reporting posture** and the **shadow-AI countermeasure discipline**.

This chapter defers to:

- **AI-technical / model-risk / red-teaming / evaluation methodology** — the substantive AI-governance stack (model-risk management, red-teaming, evaluation methodology, algorithmic-accountability governance) is owned by the `chief-ai-officer-learning`, `head-of-ai-governance-learning`, and `ai-risk-engineer-learning` roles. This chapter sets employee-behaviour rules; it does not adjudicate what makes a model safe, how it should be evaluated, or what its risk profile is.
- **Vendor security review, DPA execution, vendor risk register** — the vendor-side security review, the DPA negotiation and execution, and the vendor-risk-register maintenance live in [mod-112 (Enterprise risk, insurance and compliance)](../mod-112-enterprise-risk-insurance-and-compliance/). This chapter cross-references the outputs of that process (the tier assignment on the approved-tools registry) without owning the vendor-side workflow.
- **Customer-facing AI contract terms** — the AI-specific clauses in customer contracts (customer restrictions on the corporation's use of AI, customer obligations on the corporation's product's use of AI, indemnities and liability allocations for AI outputs) live in [mod-109 (Commercial contracts, IP and legal ops)](../mod-109-commercial-contracts-ip-and-legal-ops/). This chapter cross-references those terms via the CRM-recorded customer-specific restrictions.
- **Data-classification scheme and privacy notices** — the authoritative data-classification scheme (public / internal / confidential / customer / PII / PHI / MNPI / regulated / privileged), the privacy-notice architecture for employees and candidates, and the privacy-programme governance live in [mod-110 (Privacy, data governance and sector compliance)](../mod-110-privacy-data-governance-and-sector-compliance/). This chapter adopts that classification scheme; it does not maintain it.
- **AI-in-hiring bias governance** — the substantive governance of AI in employment decisions (NYC Local Law 144 audit programme, EEOC AI-in-employment guidance, state AI-in-hiring statutes) is owned by the `chief-ai-officer-learning` role for the algorithmic-governance stack. This chapter's rule is the behavioural one — do not use AI to score, rank, or reject a candidate or employee absent an explicit sign-off from the head of people and the algorithmic-governance owner.
- **Handbook chapter housing** — this policy lives inside the employee handbook; the handbook's structural composition, Section 7 drafting posture, and signature-and-update workflow are in [chapter 02](./02-employee-handbook-nlrb-compliant-post-stericycle.md).
- **Values interaction** — the culture-OS values (transparency, human-in-the-loop, employee agency) that animate the policy's tone are in [chapter 01](./01-values-behaviours-and-anti-values.md).
- **PIIA and employee-created work** — the IP-assignment agreement (PIIA) governing employee-created work, including AI-assisted work, lives in [mod-102 (Founding-team legal architecture)](../mod-102-founding-team-legal-architecture/). This chapter cross-references the PIIA framing; it does not restate it.

## Summary

- The AI-usage policy is a **four-tier tool framework** (Tier 1 enterprise-licensed general-purpose, Tier 2 approved with restrictions, Tier 3 restricted-to-specific-use-cases, Tier 4 forbidden) crossed with a **data-class matrix** (public, internal, confidential, customer, PII, PHI, MNPI, regulated, privileged) that governs what may be pasted into which tool.
- **Tier movement criteria** — enterprise-licence execution, terms-of-service change, regulator-jurisdiction change, security incident, vendor viability — keep the approved-tools registry current on a documented cadence.
- **Human-in-the-loop review** is required for AI-generated code, customer-facing content, hiring / employment decisions, regulated decisions, and legal or financial work-product. The human is accountable for the output, not the tool.
- **Disclosure** operates at three altitudes — external (customers and prospects, including the "material AI use" norm), internal (code review, doc review, decision documentation), and regulator (SEC, DOJ ECCP, sector regulators, state AI statutes). The regulator-cycle content is moving and is captured with `<!-- needs-research -->` markers rather than encoded as fixed rules.
- **Enterprise licences are the norm** for corporations handling any confidential, customer, PII, or regulated data — the reasoning is the operating envelope (no training on inputs, admin retention control, DPA, SSO, tenant isolation, audit logging, discovery posture), not the underlying model quality.
- **The shadow-AI problem** is a product problem, not primarily a discipline problem — the countermeasures are a good Tier 1 default, a discoverable approved-tools registry, active detection, and an amnesty-and-reporting posture on first-occurrence non-material use.
- **The discipline path** integrates with the handbook's progressive-discipline framework and [mod-107 chapter 05](../mod-107-performance-promotion-and-offboarding/05-termination-playbook.md): first-occurrence low-severity coaching, repeat-occurrence documented warning, material-breach investigation and termination-for-cause where warranted, wilful violation defaulting to termination.
- **Training and refresh cadence** — onboarding module in Week 1, annual refresh alongside handbook re-acknowledgment, role-specific supplements for engineering, product, sales, recruiting, legal, and finance. Completion tracked in the LMS and surfaced in the compliance dashboard.
- **Ownership** — this chapter owns employee-behaviour rules. Model-risk methodology, vendor-security workflow, customer-facing AI contract terms, data-classification scheme, and algorithmic-governance stack are owned in the sibling roles and modules enumerated in the boundary section.
