# 3. DEI programme design in the post-SFFA legal landscape

> Redesign the corporation's DEI portfolio — fellowships, sponsorship, supplier programmes, hiring-panel norms, benefits, pay-equity audit, ERGs — so that every programme survives Title VII and section 1981 disparate-treatment scrutiny, still moves the demographic and inclusion metrics the corporation cares about, and does not embarrass the founders in three years.

## Motivation

Diversity, equity, and inclusion programmes at technology startups were, through roughly 2020–2022, an area of relative freedom. Programmes were designed against the diversity-metric goal (representation targets, pipeline metrics, retention gaps) and against the internal-culture goal (belonging, sponsorship, career progression). The legal-risk assessment was thin because the enforcement environment was tolerant and the plaintiff bar was not organised to challenge private-employer programmes. Race-restricted fellowships, race-based grant programmes, race-conscious hiring targets, and race-based supplier programmes were common and were rarely sued.

That environment changed materially with **SFFA v. Harvard** (2023), the political shift that followed, and the wave of section-1981 and Title-VII challenges that has followed since. The failure modes are no longer hypothetical:

- The corporation runs a Black-only or Latine-only fellowship programme; an unsuccessful applicant of another race sues under **42 U.S.C. § 1981** (racial discrimination in contracting); the programme is enjoined and the corporation pays plaintiff attorneys' fees.
- The corporation's venture-arm grant programme with race-restricted eligibility is challenged in the same posture as the **American Alliance for Equal Rights v. Fearless Fund** litigation and is invalidated at the preliminary-injunction stage.
- The corporation, spooked by an activist-shareholder letter, retires the entire DEI programme overnight. Retention among under-represented senior employees drops within two quarters, the employer-brand damage attracts recruiter attention, and the incoming CHRO inherits a hole.
- The corporation ignores the shift entirely, keeps the race-restricted programmes running unchanged, loses a preliminary-injunction motion, and the lead investor — whose LPs have been reading the same headlines — quietly withdraws support in the next round.

Each of these failure modes is avoidable. The operating discipline this chapter builds is: **redesign the portfolio so every programme is defensible under current disparate-treatment law, keep moving the outcomes the corporation cares about, and be honest with employees and investors about what changed and why.**

> **Legal-not-legal-advice caveat.** The private-employer application of the SFFA reasoning, the scope of section 1981 for private employment programmes, the reach of Muldrow v. City of St. Louis to DEI-adjacent HR decisions, and the state-level anti-DEI and pro-DEI statutes are all under active litigation and active legislative movement as of the current cycle. Nothing in this chapter is legal advice. Every DEI programme design decision should be run past employment counsel with current-cycle knowledge of the litigation posture in the corporation's operating jurisdictions and, where the programme has an external-facing element (fellowship, grant, supplier), with litigation counsel in the room. See [resources.md](./resources.md) for primary sources.

## The post-SFFA legal landscape

**Students for Fair Admissions, Inc. v. President and Fellows of Harvard College**, 600 U.S. 181 (2023), consolidated with SFFA v. University of North Carolina, held that Harvard's and UNC's race-conscious admissions programmes violated the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment (as applied to UNC) and Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 (as applied to Harvard). The holding was directly about race-conscious higher-education admissions, not private employment. But the reasoning — the narrow interpretation of "compelling interest," the rejection of "stereotype-reinforcing" racial classifications, the insistence on a logical endpoint — has been picked up by lower courts and by the plaintiff bar and applied to a widening set of private-sector programmes.

The relevant follow-on authority the operator should track:

| Case / authority | Year | What it addressed | Operating implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **SFFA v. Harvard / UNC**, 600 U.S. 181 | 2023 | Race-conscious admissions under 14th Amendment and Title VI | Direct holding is admissions-only; reasoning is the basis for the post-SFFA challenge wave. |
| **Muldrow v. City of St. Louis**, 601 U.S. 346 | 2024 | Title VII "adverse employment action" standard | Lowered the threshold for what counts as an actionable Title VII injury from "significant" harm to "some harm" — expands the surface for reverse-discrimination and DEI-related Title VII challenges. |
| **American Alliance for Equal Rights v. Fearless Fund**, 103 F.4th 765 (11th Cir. 2024) | 2024 | Section 1981 challenge to a race-restricted grant programme | Confirmed that section 1981 reaches race-restricted private grant contracts; race-restricted funding programmes carry live litigation risk. |
| **Ames v. Ohio Department of Youth Services** | 2025 | Title VII majority-plaintiff standard | Removed the "background circumstances" heightened prima-facie test that some circuits had applied to majority-group plaintiffs; equalises the Title VII prima-facie bar. <!-- needs-research: confirm citation and holding of Ames v. Ohio DYS current-term Supreme Court decision and the specific circuit-split it resolved. --> |
| **Section 1981 challenge wave** | 2023– | Fellowship / mentorship / grant programmes with race-based eligibility | A growing docket of preliminary-injunction motions and settlements against explicit race-based eligibility in private-sector programmes. |
| **State anti-DEI statutes** | 2023– | Public-employer DEI restrictions in several states | Currently applies primarily to state universities and public employers; private-employer reach is being tested. <!-- needs-research: enumerate current state anti-DEI statutes and identify which have private-employer application; the list is moving. --> |
| **2025 federal executive orders on DEI** | 2025 | Federal-contractor DEI programme restrictions; OFCCP jurisdiction changes | Materially altered the federal-contractor operating environment and OFCCP posture. <!-- needs-research: confirm current EO 11246 status, DOL / OFCCP enforcement posture, and federal-contractor certification requirements after the 2025 executive orders before quoting current-year specifics. --> |

**The operating takeaway.** The reasoning of SFFA has been extended, by the plaintiff bar and by receptive courts, well beyond the admissions context in which it was decided. The rules that are becoming clear for private employers:

1. **Explicitly race-restricted eligibility** in a programme that involves a contract or contract-adjacent benefit (fellowship stipend, grant, supplier engagement, employment offer) is exposed under **42 U.S.C. § 1981**.
2. **Explicitly race-conscious decision inputs** in employment decisions (hiring, promotion, RIF selection, compensation adjustments) are exposed under **Title VII** and, per **Muldrow**, the "adverse action" bar is lower than it used to be.
3. **Post-Ames**, a majority-group Title VII plaintiff does not carry a heightened prima-facie burden.
4. **Outcome-focused programmes** that measure representation and inclusion outcomes without gating individual decisions on race are, on the current authorities, in a materially better legal position than **input-focused programmes** that condition eligibility or selection on race.

Not legal advice. The direction of travel is the point.

## Alternative programme designs that survive scrutiny

This is the core operating content of the chapter. For each traditional DEI programme category, the question is: **can the intent (support under-represented populations, close representation gaps, improve inclusion) be achieved by a programme design that does not rest on race-based or gender-based eligibility?** The answer, for almost every category, is yes — with some redesign work.

### Mentorship and sponsorship programmes

The traditional design was: a race-restricted or gender-restricted cohort of mentees paired with senior leaders. The redesign:

- **Eligibility is defined on a demographic-blind criterion that correlates with the under-served population** without being that population. Examples: "any IC without a director-level or above sponsor," "any employee who has been in role for 3+ years without a promotion," "any employee joining from a non-traditional background (career switcher, community college, military, self-taught)."
- **Sponsorship is systemised as a manager-and-director responsibility**, not an opt-in extracurricular. Every director owes N sponsorship relationships; the sponsorship map is reviewed in the talent-review cadence from [mod-107](../mod-107-performance-promotion-and-offboarding/).
- **The outcome metric** is representation among sponsored ICs and progression rates for the sponsored cohort. Measuring the demographic distribution of who ends up sponsored is legally fine; conditioning sponsorship *on* demographic identity is not.
- **The mentorship curriculum** is open — leadership pathways, career navigation, technical growth — and the cohort composition tends toward the under-sponsored population by design, not by exclusion.

The rule of thumb: if a programme's eligibility rule reads "you may apply if you are [race / gender]," it is exposed. If it reads "you may apply if you [neutral criterion that reaches the intended population]," it is materially safer.

### Development programmes and leadership pipelines

Traditional design: a leadership-development cohort restricted to Black / Latine / women / URM employees. The redesign:

- **The programme is open to any employee at the target level** who meets the neutral eligibility criteria (level, tenure, performance rating, function).
- **Outreach and recruitment for the programme is intentional** — the corporation invests in surfacing the programme to under-represented pools through ERGs (see below), targeted internal communications, and manager-nomination processes with a diverse-slate expectation. Outreach to under-represented populations is legally distinct from restricted eligibility.
- **Selection into the cohort is based on documented, demographic-blind criteria** (application quality, manager endorsement, performance history) and the selection outcome is measured for representation, not driven by demographic quota.
- **External partnerships** — historically-Black colleges and universities (HBCUs), Hispanic-serving institutions (HSIs), women-in-tech organisations, community organisations serving under-represented populations — remain fully appropriate as recruiting and outreach channels. Building a candidate pipeline through outreach is not the same as restricting who is eligible to apply.

### Supplier diversity

Traditional design: a supplier programme with an explicit race-, gender-, or veteran-owned eligibility requirement (with contracting preference or dedicated budget allocation). The redesign:

- **The supplier programme is open to any supplier** meeting the corporation's standard qualification criteria.
- **The corporation collects supplier-diversity dimension data as a reporting metric** — self-reported certifications (minority business enterprise, women-owned business, LGBTQ-owned, service-disabled veteran-owned, disability-owned) are recorded and rolled up into the annual supplier-diversity report.
- **Contracting decisions are made on the standard evaluation criteria** (price, quality, capability, terms), not on demographic identity of the supplier.
- **Programmes that build supplier pipelines** — supplier-development programmes, small-business outreach, capability-building programmes — remain appropriate provided eligibility for the *contract award* is demographic-blind.
- **Federal-contract supplier-diversity requirements** are a separate question and depend on the corporation's federal-contractor status and the current federal-contractor rules. <!-- needs-research: confirm current federal-contractor supplier-diversity requirements after 2025 EO changes; the picture has moved. -->

### Hiring-panel diversity norms

Traditional design (largely still fine, and worth reinforcing): the corporation's hiring process itself is designed to reach a diverse candidate pool and to interview those candidates with a diverse panel.

- **Diverse interview slates.** The corporation adopts a norm — every senior-role interview loop presents a slate of candidates that includes at least N candidates from under-represented backgrounds where the pool supports it. This is a **process norm on the company side** (about who the corporation interviews), not a candidate-side eligibility restriction (about who is allowed to apply).
- **Diverse interview panels.** Every interview loop is staffed with a panel that includes interviewers from under-represented backgrounds, and the panel composition is reviewed by the recruiting operations team.
- **Structured interviews with scoring rubrics.** The most legally-defensible hiring process is a highly-structured one — every candidate answers the same core questions, is scored against the same rubric, and hiring decisions are traceable to those scores. Structured interviews reduce disparate-impact risk substantially.
- **Cross-reference [mod-104](../mod-104-hiring-onboarding-and-hr-operations/)** for the full hiring-loop mechanics, structured-interview design, and offer-decision governance.

The distinction is: **candidate-side eligibility restrictions** (race-restricted job postings, race-based advancement gates) are exposed. **Company-side process norms** about how the corporation sources, interviews, and evaluates candidates are, on current authorities, defensible.

### Inclusive benefits design

Benefits design is one of the most under-appreciated DEI levers because it is materially insulated from the SFFA / section 1981 posture: benefits are offered to all employees on neutral eligibility criteria (employed here, at this level, in this jurisdiction), and the *design of the benefit* accommodates a wider set of family structures, health needs, and life circumstances.

- **Gender-neutral parental leave.** Same paid-leave entitlement for the birthing parent, the non-birthing parent, adoptive parents, and foster parents, decoupled from gender. Distinct from short-term-disability leave for the medical component of childbirth, which is legitimately birthing-parent-specific.
- **Gender-affirming care coverage.** The health plan covers gender-affirming care — hormone therapy, gender-affirming surgery, mental-health support — under standard medical-necessity criteria. Preferred-provider access and travel benefits where local access is restricted.
- **Fertility coverage.** IVF, IUI, egg / sperm freezing, and adoption cost-sharing — offered to all employees regardless of marital status, gender identity, sexual orientation, or family structure.
- **Adoption and surrogacy support.** Financial support for adoption fees, surrogacy arrangements, and associated legal fees.
- **Mental-health coverage.** Comprehensive mental-health benefits with low copays, adequate network coverage, and destigmatised access; often paired with an EAP (employee assistance programme).
- **Caregiver leave and elder-care support.** Paid caregiver leave for employees with elder-care or dependent-care responsibilities.

Cross-reference [mod-106](../mod-106-compensation-architecture-and-total-rewards/) for the total-rewards architecture that houses these benefit designs and the vendor / carrier selection mechanics.

### Compensation-equity audit

An annual regression-adjusted pay-equity audit is the highest-leverage DEI intervention the corporation can run, and it is on solid legal footing — the audit itself is a diagnostic exercise conducted under attorney-client privilege, and any remediation happens through the standard merit-cycle mechanics, not through explicit race- or gender-based comp adjustments.

- **Cadence.** Annual, timed to feed the compensation cycle.
- **Privilege.** Conducted under attorney-client privilege — engagement letter with employment counsel, counsel-engaged compensation consultant, work-product marked accordingly. This preserves the corporation's ability to conduct the audit honestly without creating a discoverable document that adverse counsel could weaponise.
- **Methodology.** Regression analysis on total-cash compensation (base + target bonus) and separately on equity, controlling for level, function, tenure, geography / cost-of-labour zone, and any other legitimate business factor.
- **Remediation trigger.** Unexplained-variance thresholds (commonly 3–5% adjusted gap) trigger targeted remediation in the next comp cycle. Remediation is delivered through the standard merit-cycle mechanism, not as a race-labelled or gender-labelled adjustment.
- **Cross-reference [mod-106](../mod-106-compensation-architecture-and-total-rewards/)** for the audit methodology, the regression specification, the privilege architecture, and the integration with the compensation cycle.

## EEO-1 reporting compliance

The **EEO-1 Component 1** report is the federal demographic workforce report filed annually with the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) by covered private employers.

**Coverage.** Private employers with **100 or more employees**, and federal contractors with **50 or more employees** and a federal contract of $50,000 or more. The corporation reports:

- Headcount by job category and by race / ethnicity and gender combination
- Data collected as of a "workforce snapshot period" (a pay period the employer selects during the specified snapshot window)
- Filed through the EEOC's online portal within the annual submission window

**EEO-1 job categories.** The report classifies every employee into one of ten job categories:

| # | Category |
|---|---|
| 1.1 | Executive / Senior Level Officials and Managers |
| 1.2 | First / Mid Level Officials and Managers |
| 2 | Professionals |
| 3 | Technicians |
| 4 | Sales Workers |
| 5 | Administrative Support Workers |
| 6 | Craft Workers |
| 7 | Operatives |
| 8 | Laborers and Helpers |
| 9 | Service Workers |

Most technology-company employees map to categories 1.1, 1.2, and 2. Category assignment is on the basis of the employee's actual job duties, not job title. HR-operations discipline: every employee has a documented EEO-1 category assignment, and the assignment is reviewed when roles materially change.

**Race / ethnicity categories.** The EEO-1 uses seven race / ethnicity categories, all self-identified: Hispanic or Latino; White; Black or African American; Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander; Asian; American Indian or Alaska Native; and Two or More Races.

**Submission window.** The EEOC opens the reporting window annually and publishes the deadline. <!-- needs-research: confirm current-year EEO-1 submission window dates before publishing internal deadlines. -->

**State-level equivalents.** Several states have independent pay-data and workforce-demographic reporting regimes that layer on top of the federal EEO-1:

| State | Statute / regulation | What it requires | Threshold |
|---|---|---|---|
| **California** | Gov. Code § 12999 (SB 973 / SB 1162) | Annual pay data report to the California Civil Rights Department (CRD) — median and mean hourly rate by job category, race / ethnicity, and sex, for both employees and labour contractors | 100+ employees (any employee in California) |
| **Illinois** | Equal Pay Act (Equal Pay Registration Certificate) | Biennial Equal Pay Registration Certificate application to the Illinois Department of Labor including EEO-1-style data and a pay-equity compliance statement | 100+ employees in Illinois |
| **Massachusetts** | Chapter 141 of the Acts of 2024 (wage data reporting) | Annual wage data report to the Massachusetts Executive Office of Labor and Workforce Development | 100+ employees in Massachusetts |
| **New York City** | Local Law 32 of 2022 (pay transparency) | Pay-range posting in job advertisements; separate from workforce-demographic reporting | 4+ employees |

<!-- needs-research: confirm current-year filing deadlines, thresholds, and covered-employer definitions for each state pay-data / wage-data statute; the state landscape moves annually. -->

**Retention discipline.**

- The EEO-1 workforce snapshot data (as of the snapshot date) is retained for **at least 3 years**, and typically indefinitely as part of the corporation's HRIS record.
- The California pay-data report file, the Illinois EPRC application, and the Massachusetts wage-data submission are retained on the same cadence.
- The underlying compensation and demographic data that feeds the reports lives in the HRIS and comp-planning tool per [mod-104](../mod-104-hiring-onboarding-and-hr-operations/) and [mod-106](../mod-106-compensation-architecture-and-total-rewards/) — the annual EEO-1 exercise is a derivation from that data, not a parallel data collection.

## OFCCP Affirmative Action Plan compliance

The **Office of Federal Contract Compliance Programs** (OFCCP), within the U.S. Department of Labor, historically administered the affirmative-action-plan (AAP) obligations that attached to federal contractors under Executive Order 11246 and two related statutes.

The 2025 executive orders materially altered OFCCP jurisdiction — in particular the revocation or modification of EO 11246, the wind-down of race- and sex-based affirmative-action-plan requirements for federal contractors, and the continuation of the Section 503 (disability) and VEVRAA (veterans) AAP requirements. The specifics as of the current cycle should be confirmed against OFCCP guidance and DOL rulemaking before this section is used as an operating reference. -->

The historical framework, which the operator should still understand because portions remain and because the operating cadence is instructive:

**Coverage.** Federal contractors and subcontractors with **50 or more employees** and a covered federal contract of **$50,000 or more**.

**The three plan documents.**

| Plan | Governing authority | Covers | Threshold |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Executive Order 11246 AAP** | EO 11246 (historically) | Gender and race / ethnicity affirmative action | 50 employees, $50k contract |
| **Section 503 AAP** | Section 503 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 | Individuals with disabilities | 50 employees, $50k contract; utilisation goal of 7% for individuals with disabilities |
| **VEVRAA AAP** | Vietnam Era Veterans' Readjustment Assistance Act (38 U.S.C. § 4212) | Protected veterans | 50 employees, $150k contract; hiring benchmark published annually |

**Annual cadence.** Historically, AAPs were prepared annually on a "plan year" basis chosen by the contractor. The plan included a workforce analysis, availability analysis, utilisation analysis (with goals set where under-utilisation exists), and narrative action-oriented programmes.

**OFCCP audit / compliance evaluation.** The OFCCP historically conducted scheduled compliance evaluations, opened with a scheduling letter and Itemised Listing. The evaluation reviewed the AAP, personnel activity data (applicant-to-hire, promotion, termination), compensation data, and outreach and recruitment efforts.

**Operating implication for the current cycle.** For any corporation that holds — or is considering holding — a covered federal contract, the OFCCP-jurisdiction question, the AAP-preparation question, and the certification question should be run past employment counsel with current federal-contractor guidance in hand. The 2025 EO changes have moved the picture; do not rely on pre-2025 AAP-preparation vendors or templates without a current-cycle refresh.

## Pay-equity audit cadence

The pay-equity audit is discussed in detail in [mod-106](../mod-106-compensation-architecture-and-total-rewards/); the DEI-programme lens on it is:

| Element | Design |
|---|---|
| **Cadence** | Annual, timed 60–90 days before the compensation cycle so remediation can flow through the merit cycle |
| **Privilege** | Attorney-client privileged; counsel-engaged compensation consultant; work-product marked and access-controlled |
| **Data set** | All benchmarked employees; total cash compensation (base + target bonus) and separately vested-plus-unvested equity value at target |
| **Regression controls** | Level, function, sub-function, tenure at company, prior relevant experience, geographic zone / cost-of-labour band |
| **Population slices** | Gender (binary and non-binary where sample supports), race / ethnicity, age (40+ vs. under-40), veteran status, disability status where self-identified |
| **Threshold for remediation** | Unexplained-variance flag typically at 3–5% adjusted gap or at statistical-significance threshold; corporation defines threshold in advance of running the audit |
| **Remediation mechanism** | Individual comp adjustments delivered through the standard merit cycle; not labelled as race- or gender-based adjustments in the individual comp-letter |
| **Governance** | Compensation-committee brief on the audit results and the remediation cost; head-of-people / GC / CFO review of the methodology |
| **External disclosure** | The corporation may voluntarily disclose that it conducts an annual pay-equity audit and remediates; the audit results themselves stay privileged |

The audit is one of the two or three programmes in the DEI portfolio that has both real impact on the metric the corporation cares about (pay equity by demographic slice) and a legally-solid design (privileged diagnostic + neutral-mechanism remediation).

## ERG (Employee Resource Group) framework

Employee Resource Groups — sometimes called Business Resource Groups, Affinity Groups, or Colleague Networks — are voluntary, employee-organised groups that build community, provide peer support, contribute to corporate diversity efforts, and sometimes function as a talent-and-culture consulting resource to the executive team.

The ERG framework the corporation should adopt:

- **Chartered ERGs.** Every ERG operates under a written charter approved by the head of people and the general counsel. The charter includes: mission, membership criteria, activities, budget, executive sponsor, chair and vice-chair roles, and a term for the chair.
- **Open membership.** The single most important design decision: **ERGs are open to all employees who share the interest, not gated on the demographic identity the ERG serves.** The Women's ERG is open to any employee interested in gender equity, not restricted to women. The Black ERG is open to any employee interested in supporting Black colleagues, not restricted to Black employees. The LGBTQ+ ERG is open to any employee who is a member or an ally. This is both a legal-defensibility norm (avoids race-restricted or gender-restricted membership as a section 1981 / Title VII exposure) and a cultural norm (allyship is welcome and encouraged).
- **Budget allocation.** Each chartered ERG receives an annual budget from the people-team budget. Typical ranges: $2,000–$25,000 depending on ERG size and activity scope. Budget is used for community events, professional development, conference attendance, guest speakers, and internal programming.
- **Executive sponsor.** Every ERG has a named executive sponsor at the VP+ level who advocates for the ERG in leadership settings, secures resources, and signals the executive team's commitment. Executive sponsors are typically assigned regardless of the sponsor's own demographic identity.
- **Chair compensation / recognition.** ERG chair work is real work and is either compensated (a stipend or an allocated portion of paid work-time) or recognised in performance and career-progression conversations.
- **Reporting.** Each ERG delivers an annual report to the head of people covering membership, activities, budget expenditure, and outcomes.
- **Corporate-activity boundaries.** ERGs are not corporate-lobbying vehicles. Chartered activities are internally focused (community, career, culture); external political or lobbying activity is out of scope per the charter and the corporation's political-activity policy.

The drafting-around-eligibility norm is the load-bearing element. An ERG whose charter says "membership is open to Black employees only" is exposed. An ERG whose charter says "membership is open to any employee who supports the mission of building community and career advancement for Black colleagues" is not.

## Anti-DEI-backlash operating norms

The DEI programme now operates under a level of external scrutiny — from activist investors, from short-sellers with an anti-DEI thesis, from litigious plaintiffs, from media outlets on both sides — that did not exist five years ago. The operating discipline:

**Focus on process and outcome, not demographic inputs to decisions.**

- **Measuring representation outcomes is fine.** "What percentage of our senior engineers are women?" is a legitimate diagnostic question that the corporation can and should ask.
- **Using demographic identity as an input to an individual selection decision** — who gets hired, who gets promoted, who gets a comp adjustment, who gets into the leadership programme — is where the legal exposure sits. Every selection decision should be traceable to documented, demographic-blind criteria.

**Programme framing.**

- **Programme names and public communications** avoid framings that invite challenge. "Leadership development for high-potential ICs" travels better than "Diverse leaders programme." "Sponsorship for under-sponsored ICs" travels better than "Sponsorship for women and URM employees."
- **Public disclosures** — the corporation's DEI page, the annual impact report, the S-1 disclosures if applicable — describe the programme in outcome-and-process terms, not in race-and-gender-restricted terms.
- **Board and executive messaging** stays honest — the corporation has redesigned its DEI portfolio to reflect the current legal landscape; the corporation continues to care about representation and inclusion; the corporation is not abandoning the work.

**Handling activist and media pressure.**

- **Activist-shareholder letters and proposals** — coordinated response through the legal, IR, and communications teams. The corporation's substantive answer is that the programme is legally-defensible and outcome-focused; the corporation does not capitulate to demands to retire programmes that are legitimate.
- **Media inquiries** — routed to communications; on-the-record responses are pre-approved; the CEO and head of people are prepared with the corporation's DEI narrative.
- **Employee questions** — town-hall and manager cadence includes a candid explanation of what changed, why, and what the corporation is still doing. Employees are better served by an honest explanation of the redesign than by silence.

**Documentation discipline.**

- **Every DEI programme has a legal-review record** — the general counsel or outside employment counsel has reviewed and approved the programme design.
- **Selection decisions are documented** against the neutral criteria — for a leadership-development programme, the selection rubric, the individual scores, the selection rationale, all preserved.
- **Compensation adjustments** flowing from the pay-equity audit are documented as merit-cycle adjustments in the standard compensation record — not as demographic-based adjustments in a separately-labelled document.

**Retention over performance.** The temptation, under external pressure, is to swing between over-commitment (public DEI declarations, high-profile targets) and abandonment (retire the programme, remove the DEI page). Neither is the right operating stance. The right operating stance is quiet, disciplined, well-documented continuation of the programmes that survive scrutiny.

## Concrete example: a 200-person Series-B company's post-SFFA redesign

A hypothetical 200-person Series-B B2B SaaS company had, in 2022, the following DEI portfolio:

| Programme | 2022 state | 2024 post-redesign state | Rationale |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Black Engineering Fellowship** — 6-month cohort with stipend for Black engineering candidates | Race-restricted eligibility; external candidates apply through Black-in-Tech partner orgs | **Retired** and replaced by an **Emerging Engineering Talent** programme with neutral eligibility (early-career engineers from non-traditional backgrounds) and continued partner-org outreach | Section 1981 exposure on race-restricted eligibility for a stipend-carrying programme |
| **Women in Leadership Cohort** — 12-month leadership development for women managers | Gender-restricted cohort | **Restructured** as **Emerging Leaders Cohort** — open to any manager at L4–L5 without a director-level sponsor, with intentional outreach to women and URM managers | Removes gender-restricted eligibility; keeps the underlying leadership-development structure and the outreach channels |
| **URM Employee Grant Programme** — $5k professional-development grant for URM employees | Race-restricted eligibility | **Retired**; replaced by a **corporation-wide learning-and-development budget** available to all employees with an internal-application process | Section 1981 exposure; underlying investment intent redirected to a neutral programme |
| **Supplier Diversity Programme** — 15% supplier-spend target with minority-owned businesses | Race-restricted contracting target | **Restructured** as an **open supplier programme** with a supplier-diversity reporting dimension; certifications self-reported and rolled up in the annual report | Removes race-based contracting eligibility; preserves the reporting dimension the corporation cares about |
| **Diverse Interview Slate norm** | Recruiting norm — every senior role has a diverse slate | **Retained unchanged** and reinforced with recruiting-operations tracking | Company-side process norm; not exposed under current authorities |
| **Diverse Interview Panel norm** | Recruiting norm — every interview loop includes URM interviewers | **Retained unchanged** with interviewer-availability tracking | Company-side process norm; not exposed under current authorities |
| **Inclusive Benefits (gender-affirming care, fertility, adoption, mental health)** | Benefit design | **Retained unchanged** — additional expansion of fertility coverage in the 2025 plan year | Neutral eligibility; open to all employees |
| **Annual Pay-Equity Audit** | Annual audit under privilege | **Retained unchanged**; methodology refresh with new comp-consultant vendor | Privileged diagnostic; neutral-mechanism remediation |
| **ERG framework (5 chartered ERGs)** | Membership open to all; executive sponsors assigned | **Retained** with charter refresh to confirm the open-membership norm in writing; budget increased | Compliant with the open-membership norm |
| **DEI hiring target (30% URM at senior levels)** | Public target in the annual impact report | **Retired as an external commitment**; internal representation-outcome tracking retained | Public hiring target creates a challenge posture; internal outcome tracking does not gate individual decisions |
| **DEI training (annual mandatory)** | Annual mandatory training on unconscious bias, inclusive behaviours | **Restructured** with counsel review to remove any language that could be characterised as stereotyping majority-group employees; retained as annual training | Post-Ames and post-Muldrow environment; training content itself has been raised in some Title VII actions |
| **Chief Diversity Officer role** | CDO reports to CEO; sits on executive team | **Retained**; scope broadened to "Head of People Experience" covering DEI, culture, ERGs, and inclusive benefits | Preserves the executive-level ownership; broadens the mandate to reduce single-issue exposure |

Net outcome of the redesign:

- **Two programmes retired outright** (race-restricted fellowship, race-restricted grant) with the underlying investment redirected to neutral-eligibility successors.
- **Three programmes restructured** (leadership cohort, supplier programme, DEI training) with the intent preserved and the demographic-gating removed.
- **Six programmes retained unchanged** (interview slate, interview panel, inclusive benefits, pay-equity audit, ERG framework, CDO role) because they were already on defensible ground.
- **One external commitment retired** (public hiring target) with the underlying internal tracking preserved.

The corporation's demographic-outcome metrics continued to move in the same direction after the redesign; the litigation exposure dropped materially; the corporation was not the subject of an activist-shareholder letter or a section-1981 challenge in the following cycle.

## Summary

- The DEI legal landscape shifted materially with **SFFA v. Harvard (2023)**, extended by **Muldrow**, **Fearless Fund**, **Ames**, the section-1981 challenge wave, and the 2025 federal executive orders on federal-contractor DEI.
- **Explicitly race- or gender-restricted eligibility** in fellowships, grants, mentorship cohorts, and supplier programmes carries live litigation risk under **42 U.S.C. § 1981** and Title VII.
- **Alternative programme designs** — mentorship / sponsorship on neutral eligibility criteria, leadership development open to all with intentional outreach, supplier programmes with a diversity-reporting dimension, hiring-panel diversity norms as company-side process, inclusive benefits, privileged pay-equity audits — preserve the intent and materially reduce the legal exposure.
- **EEO-1 reporting** applies at 100+ employees (or 50+ for covered federal contractors); state pay-data reports (California, Illinois, Massachusetts) layer on top; retention discipline is 3+ years.
- **OFCCP AAP compliance** — historically for federal contractors at 50 employees / $50k; the 2025 executive orders materially changed the picture; confirm current OFCCP jurisdiction with counsel before relying on pre-2025 AAP templates.
- **Pay-equity audit** — annual, privileged, regression-adjusted on level / function / tenure / geography; remediation via the standard merit cycle. See [mod-106](../mod-106-compensation-architecture-and-total-rewards/).
- **ERG framework** — chartered, open membership, allocated budget, executive sponsors; open-membership norm is the legal-defensibility anchor.
- **Anti-DEI-backlash operating norms** — focus on process and outcome metrics; do not gate individual selection decisions on demographic inputs; document programme designs under counsel review; retain the discipline of quiet, defensible continuation over swings between over-commitment and abandonment.
- See [exercise-03](./exercises/exercise-03-dei-programme-design-post-sffa.md) for the portfolio-redesign authoring drill.
