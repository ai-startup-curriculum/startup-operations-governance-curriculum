# 1. Values, behaviours & anti-values

> A defensible set of values is specific, behaviourally anchored, trade-off explicit, and disagreement-tolerant — paired with anti-values that name what you refuse to do — and wired into hiring, review, recognition, and offboarding so they are load-bearing rather than decorative.

## Motivation

Most startup values lists are indistinguishable from every other startup values list. "Innovation, integrity, customer obsession, teamwork, ownership" appears on so many careers pages that it has become invisible — the words no longer transmit information because they carry the same content whether you write them or your competitor writes them. Values that could belong to any company do not describe your company. They are, at best, decoration; at worst, they are actively harmful.

The concrete failure modes are recognisable. **Embarrassing wall art** — the CEO commissions a mural of the five values, the mural goes up, and within a year the values have been quietly abandoned by the operating team because nobody remembers what "Deliver excellence" was supposed to mean in a Tuesday standup. **"Value violation" as pretext for firing** — a manager wants to terminate a report they cannot build a performance case against, so the manager reaches for "not a culture fit" or "violated our value of teamwork," and the corporation absorbs the litigation risk of a termination whose real rationale is undocumented. **The values-workshop-that-goes-nowhere** — the exec team spends a weekend off-site drafting values, produces a deck, presents the deck at all-hands, and then never mentions the values again in any operating forum (no interview rubric, no review criterion, no promotion bar, no recognition programme). The values die of neglect within eighteen months.

The underlying problem is that most values-authoring efforts optimise for the wrong thing. They optimise for a list that sounds inspiring on a careers page and offends no one on the exec team. Inspiration and unanimity are the wrong success criteria. A value that inspires everyone equally is a platitude; a value that offends no one on the exec team is a value that requires no trade-off. The right success criteria are the opposite: **the value should describe a specific behaviour that a peer company would plausibly reject**, and it should be uncomfortable enough that at least one exec on the drafting team argues against it.

The purpose of this chapter is to teach you to author values that are load-bearing — that a hiring manager can score against, a review calibration can invoke, a peer-recognition programme can point to, and a termination case can (with the appropriate legal architecture from [mod-103](../mod-103-employment-law-and-contract-design/)) rest on. That means values chosen with a framework, paired with explicit anti-values, and wired into the people-ops operating stack so they show up in every consequential decision the corporation makes about a human.

## The five operating decisions

Authoring values that survive contact with the operating calendar is five separate decisions:

1. **Selection framework.** What is the bar a candidate value has to clear before it goes on the list?
2. **Authoring process.** Who drafts, who challenges, who signs off, and how do you avoid the exec-off-site-echo-chamber failure mode?
3. **Anti-values framing.** Which behaviours do you refuse, and how do you draft the "we do NOT ..." sentences without softening them into pablum?
4. **Integration into people-ops operations.** How do values show up in hiring, performance, promotion, recognition, and offboarding — and where do you defer to sibling modules for the underlying mechanics?
5. **Retrospective cadence.** When do you revisit the values, when do you refuse to revisit them, and what does a "values reset" signal about the previous set?

Each is a separate decision. Skipping any one of them produces the failure modes in the motivation section.

## Decision 1 — The value-selection framework

Every candidate value has to clear four tests. If it fails any one, it does not go on the list.

**Test 1 — Specific and non-generic.** The test is: could a peer company plausibly claim the *opposite* value and still function? If not, the value is a platitude. "Innovation" fails — no company claims "we do not innovate." "Ship weekly" passes — plenty of successful companies deliberately ship on longer cycles, and choosing weekly is a real choice with real consequences. "Integrity" fails — no company claims dishonesty. "Say the uncomfortable thing in the room, not in Slack after" passes — plenty of companies operate on Slack-back-channel norms and function fine, and choosing the opposite is a real choice.

**Test 2 — Behaviourally anchored.** Each value carries two or three concrete behaviours that a manager or peer could observe. "Own your outcomes" is a slogan; "when a release breaks in production, the on-call engineer writes the incident review within 48 hours, names the decision they would make differently, and posts it to the engineering channel" is a behaviour. The behaviour test is: could you write an interview question that scores this? Could you write a peer-recognition citation that references this? If not, the anchor is too abstract.

**Test 3 — Trade-off explicit.** Every value describes what it costs you. "Ship weekly" costs you architectural elegance and long-arc refactors. "Say the uncomfortable thing in the room" costs you the comfort of harmony-first meetings and it costs you some percentage of new-hire retention (some people leave a directness-first culture). If a value has no cost, it is not a value — it is a preference everyone agrees on, and preferences everyone agrees on do not require an operating system to enforce. Draft the cost paragraph *before* the value goes on the list. The cost paragraph is the honesty check.

**Test 4 — Disagreement-tolerant.** A value that every exec nods along to on first read is a platitude. A real value produces argument in the drafting room. If the drafting session is comfortable, you are drafting platitudes. Deliberately draft each candidate value in a form strong enough to attract disagreement, and then keep the disagreement in the room until either the value survives the disagreement (and goes on the list) or the disagreement wins (and the value comes off). A value nobody argued with is a value nobody will operate against.

### Running the values-authoring workshop

The authoring process is a two-layer read: the **exec drafting team** and the **cross-functional read group**.

**Exec drafting team.** The CEO, head of people, and two or three exec-team members who have been at the company long enough to have opinions about how it actually operates today. Size: 4–6. Larger than that and drafting collapses into consensus theatre; smaller and you get the CEO's diary rather than a corporate document. The drafting team meets for two half-day sessions two to three weeks apart. Session one produces a long-list of 15–25 candidate values against the four tests above. Session two prunes to a short-list of 5–7 (five is the mainstream target; more than seven and no one remembers them).

**Cross-functional read group.** A group of 10–15 people from across functions and levels — some senior, some mid-level, some IC, some manager. The read group receives the exec draft with the trade-off paragraph attached. They are asked three questions per value: (1) is this describing how we actually operate today, or how the exec team wishes we operated? (2) is the cost paragraph honest — do you actually accept that cost? (3) is there a behaviour the value should have that the exec draft missed? The read group is not a voting body; it is a reality check on whether the exec draft is describing the same company the rest of the org works at.

The read group's most important job is to catch **aspirational values** — values that describe the company the CEO wishes existed rather than the company that actually exists. Aspirational values are not automatically wrong (some fraction of a values list should describe where you are trying to get to), but they should be labelled as such internally and the operating team should know that aspirational values do not yet meet the bar for being invoked in a review or a termination.

The output of the workshop is a document with, for each value:

- The one-sentence value statement.
- Two or three behavioural anchors.
- The trade-off paragraph — what this value costs the company.
- (Optional but recommended) One or two "this is not" clarifications — near-neighbour misreadings the drafting team wants to head off.

## Decision 2 — The anti-values framing

Anti-values are the sentences that begin with "we do NOT ..." They name the behaviours the corporation refuses. Anti-values are frequently more useful than positive values because they are unambiguous — "we do not tolerate silent disagreement followed by back-channel Slack" leaves less room for interpretation than "we value directness."

**Why anti-values are hard to write.** Positive values are easy because they flatter. Anti-values name a behaviour, name a group of people who exhibit that behaviour, and (implicitly) name a group of people who will not thrive here. Every anti-value is an active recruitment filter and an active retention filter. The temptation, once drafted, is to soften — to turn "we do NOT run consensus-first meetings; the decision-owner decides after listening" into "we value decisive decision-making," which sounds nicer and communicates nothing. Every softening step is a step toward the platitude failure mode.

**How to draft anti-values.** Two techniques work.

The first is **inversion of the positive value**. For each positive value, ask: what is the specific behaviour we are refusing? "Ship weekly" inverts to "we do NOT let architectural perfectionism block a Friday release; we ship, then refactor in flight." The inversion frequently produces a sharper sentence than the positive value did.

The second is **the offboarding retrospective**. Ask the exec team and the head of people: over the last 12–24 months, what were the top three behaviours that led to a departure — whether termination or a mutual exit? Each of those behaviours is a candidate anti-value. Anti-values authored this way are automatically load-bearing because you have already fired against them.

**Guardrails on drafting.** Anti-values should describe *behaviours*, not *identities*. "We do NOT hire people who prioritise family over work" is not a behavioural anti-value — it is an identity filter, and it will expose the corporation to discrimination claims. "We do NOT ship work that has not been reviewed by a second engineer" is a behavioural anti-value. Draft in the second frame, always.

Anti-values should also be **enforceable**. If you write "we do NOT tolerate disrespect" and then take no action when a senior leader is disrespectful in a meeting, you have taught the org that the anti-value is decorative. Every anti-value on the list should have at least one recent example where the corporation acted on it — ideally a public example the org saw. Anti-values without enforcement history are more damaging than no anti-values, because they teach the org that written commitments are optional.

**The temptation to soften.** Between draft and publish, an anti-value passes through the CEO, the head of people, general counsel (mod-103), and often a PR or communications lens. Each of those readers has an incentive to soften. General counsel worries about the anti-value being cited in a wrongful-termination case; PR worries about it being screenshotted and mocked; the CEO worries about a founder-friend recognising themselves in it. The head-of-people's job in the authoring workshop is to protect the sharpness. A round of legal review is warranted (mod-103 covers the at-will and arbitration architecture that limits the litigation surface of values-based statements), but the reviewer's role is to spot legal exposure, not to soften the language for comfort.

## Decision 3 — Integration into people-ops operations

Values that are not wired into operating decisions decay. Wire them into five places.

### 3.1 Hiring

Values show up in the hiring loop in two places: the **values-fit interview** and the **values-based competency questions** interleaved into function-specific interviews. This chapter covers only the values side of the mechanic — the full hiring loop architecture, interviewer training, and scorecard mechanics live in [mod-104](../mod-104-hiring-onboarding-and-hr-operations/).

**Values-fit interview.** A single 45-minute interview slot dedicated to values, run by someone outside the hiring function (a peer from a different team). Structure: for each value, one behavioural question ("tell me about a time you ...") with a follow-up probe. The interviewer scores each value on a three-point scale (below / at / above bar) with a written rationale. The interview is *not* a "would I get a beer with this person" check — the classic culture-fit failure mode. It is a behavioural scoring against the anchors written into each value. The values-fit interview has veto power over the hire.

**Values-based competency questions in function interviews.** Each function-specific interview also carries one or two questions tied to a value that is particularly load-bearing for that function. An engineering interview on a "ship weekly" value asks about the candidate's most recent trade-off between shipping and refactoring. A sales interview on a "say the uncomfortable thing" value asks about the candidate's last hard customer conversation. These questions raise the base-rate signal on values and prevent the values-fit interview from being the only shot.

**Anti-values in hiring.** The anti-values inform *disqualifying signals*, not scored questions. If the "we do NOT tolerate silent disagreement" anti-value is on the list, the hiring loop watches for candidates whose stories about past conflict end with "I let it go and worked around them." That pattern is a disqualifier, not a low score.

### 3.2 Performance and promotion

Values show up in the review criteria and in the promotion criteria. The mechanics of the performance operating system are in [mod-107 chapter 01](../mod-107-performance-promotion-and-offboarding/01-performance-management-operating-system.md); the mechanics of promotion architecture are in [mod-107 chapter 02](../mod-107-performance-promotion-and-offboarding/02-promotion-architecture.md). This chapter covers what to plug into those mechanics.

**In the review criterion.** The review template carries a values section alongside the goals and competencies sections. For each value, the manager writes a paragraph on the report's behaviour against the value's anchors over the review period. The self-review carries the same section. The values section produces a values rating (below / at / above), separate from the performance rating. **Do not merge values and performance into a single rating** — it destroys signal and it makes values-based feedback impossible to give distinctly. A high performer with a values gap needs to hear that clearly; a strong culture-carrier who is under-performing on outcomes needs to hear that clearly too.

**In calibration.** The values rating is calibrated in the same session as the performance rating (see mod-107 chapter 01 on calibration mechanics). A pattern to watch for is **the high-performer values pass** — a report whose performance is strong enough that the manager softens the values rating rather than deliver hard feedback. Calibration catches this by making values ratings visible across the function.

**In promotion.** The promotion bar for every level carries a values dimension. Promotion to manager, in particular, should require an above-bar values rating for at least the two most recent review cycles, because a manager who is a values gap propagates the gap into their entire team. The promotion committee (mod-107 chapter 02) is empowered to block a promotion on values grounds even if performance is above bar.

### 3.3 Recognition

Values show up in every recognition programme the company runs — peer recognition, spot bonuses, and formal awards.

**Peer recognition.** The recognition submission form (Slack integration, Bonusly, a shared Airtable, whatever) requires the sender to tag which value the recognised behaviour exemplifies. This has two effects: it teaches the org what the values look like in practice (the recognition feed becomes a living behaviour dictionary), and it produces a signal on which values are actually load-bearing (a value that gets zero peer recognitions in a quarter is either invisible or dead).

**Spot bonuses.** A spot-bonus policy tied to values is a strong reinforcement mechanism. Any manager can nominate a report for a spot bonus (typical range: $250–$2,500 depending on stage; consult mod-106 for the compensation-architecture side) with a written citation that references the value and the specific behaviour. The head of people reviews the citation. Spot-bonus data over a quarter is a useful diagnostic on manager attention — a manager who never nominates is either failing to notice value-carrying behaviour or is holding recognition hostage.

**Formal awards.** Annual or semi-annual company-wide awards tied to each value ("the [ship-weekly] award for the person who best exemplified ship-weekly this year") make the values visible at the all-hands and produce a public reinforcement moment. Award selection should be a small committee, not a popularity vote, because popularity votes reward likability rather than the behaviour the value describes.

### 3.4 Offboarding

The termination playbook mechanics live in [mod-107 chapter 05](../mod-107-performance-promotion-and-offboarding/05-termination-playbook.md); the legal architecture for at-will termination and separation agreements lives in [mod-103](../mod-103-employment-law-and-contract-design/). This chapter covers only the distinction between a values-violation termination and a performance-based termination.

**Performance-based termination.** The employee is meeting some or none of their outcome expectations. The path is: coaching → documented performance concerns → PIP → termination if the PIP is not met. The documentation trail is quantitative (goals missed, deliverables late, quality issues). See mod-107 chapters 01–05 for the full mechanics.

**Values-violation termination.** The employee is meeting outcome expectations but is exhibiting a behaviour that the corporation has publicly named an anti-value. The path is materially different: because the behaviour is (typically) not a matter of skill or ramp, a PIP is often not the right instrument. The mainstream pattern is: documented conversation → final written warning tied to the specific anti-value → termination on the next occurrence. The documentation trail is qualitative (specific incidents, specific witnesses, specific behavioural anchor from the anti-value definition).

**Where the two collide.** A performance-based case that is *actually* a values case in disguise is the most common failure mode. A manager wants a report gone but cannot document a performance gap, so the manager reaches for "not a culture fit." This is the mode that produces litigation and morale damage. The head-of-people gate is: if a manager brings a "values-fit" termination case, the head of people asks (a) which specific value or anti-value, (b) which specific behavioural anchor, (c) what documentation exists, (d) what conversations have been held. If any of the four answers is thin, the case is not yet ripe — go back to documented conversations first.

**Enforcement signals to the rest of the org.** Values-based terminations are, by design, more visible than performance terminations. When the corporation terminates a senior leader for an anti-value violation, the rest of the org learns that the values are load-bearing. This is why the anti-values enforcement history matters so much — every enforced case teaches the org, and every unenforced case teaches the opposite.

## Decision 4 — The retrospective cadence

Values are not annual OKRs. They should not be revised every year. The retrospective cadence is:

- **Annual light-touch review.** Once a year, at the same time as the annual planning cycle, the head of people runs a 60-minute session with the exec team asking: has any value stopped describing us? Has any anti-value stopped being enforced? Is there a behaviour we consistently reward that is not on the list? The default outcome is *no change*. The point of the light-touch review is to catch drift, not to invite rewriting.
- **On-major-inflection full review.** When the company hits a genuine inflection — a new CEO, a transformational acquisition, an IPO, a pivot that changes the customer or the product materially — the values are revisited in full. This is a rare event; most companies go through zero or one such inflection in the founder-CEO era.

**Why a "values reset" every 2–3 years is a bad signal.** If the company is rewriting its values every two or three years absent a genuine inflection, the previous set was never load-bearing. Load-bearing values *survive* — the org has invested in operating against them, the workforce has been hired against them, the anti-values enforcement history rests on them. Rewriting resets all of that investment. Frequent values rewrites also teach the org that the values are marketing artefacts rather than operating commitments, which is precisely the failure mode this chapter is trying to prevent.

The exception is if the previous values were badly authored — generic, un-anchored, un-enforced. In that case a full rewrite is warranted, but the rewrite should be honest with the org about what was wrong with the previous set. "We rewrote the values because the last ones were platitudes and we never enforced them" is a defensible message; "we refreshed the values" is not.

## Ownership boundary

This chapter owns the authoring, integration, and retrospective mechanics for values, behaviours, and anti-values as a **culture** instrument. It defers to sibling modules on two questions.

- **Legal grounds for a values-violation termination.** The at-will architecture, the arbitration clauses, the separation-agreement mechanics, and the discrimination-claim exposure of behavioural anti-values are owned by [mod-103](../mod-103-employment-law-and-contract-design/). This chapter tells you how to *author* an enforceable anti-value; mod-103 tells you what the employment contract has to say for the anti-value to be safely invocable at termination.
- **The PIP / warning / termination mechanics themselves.** The documentation cadence, the HR-business-partner escalation path, the final-written-warning template, and the termination-day playbook are owned by [mod-107 chapters 04 and 05](../mod-107-performance-promotion-and-offboarding/). This chapter tells you when a values-violation case is ripe; mod-107 tells you how to execute it.

If you are unclear which module owns a question, the rule of thumb is: if the question is "what is the value and how is it wired into people-ops," this chapter owns it; if the question is "what does the paperwork say and how is it executed," mod-103 and mod-107 own it.

## Concrete example: a Series-A 80-person B2B SaaS company

A hypothetical 80-person Series-A B2B SaaS company selling workflow software into mid-market Ops teams. Founder-CEO, five-person exec team, twelve managers, three functions (Engineering + Product, Go-to-Market, G&A). Head of people is the first non-founder people hire, joined at 45 employees.

The exec team + head of people ran the two-session drafting workshop above, then took the draft to a 12-person cross-functional read group. The output was five values and four anti-values.

### The five values

**Value 1 — Ship weekly, refactor in flight.**

- *Behavioural anchors.* Every engineering team ships to production on a weekly cadence, minimum. When a design turns out to be wrong, the fix goes in as a follow-up ticket, not as a blocker on the initial ship. Every incident review names the decision that would have been made differently and the specific engineer who owns the fix.
- *What it costs.* Architectural elegance and long-arc refactors take longer here than at companies that ship monthly. Some senior engineers who value big-design-up-front will not be happy. Occasional production incidents from ships that could have been more thoroughly reviewed — the corporation accepts this as the cost of the cadence.

**Value 2 — Say the uncomfortable thing in the room, not in Slack after.**

- *Behavioural anchors.* Disagreement is raised in the meeting where the decision is made, by the person who disagrees. Post-meeting "actually, I disagreed" DMs are treated as a values gap. Managers actively invite dissent from the least-senior person in the room before closing a decision.
- *What it costs.* Meetings run longer. New hires from harmony-first cultures take 3–6 months to adjust and some do not. The corporation loses some fraction of candidates in the values-fit interview because they cannot produce a story of raising a hard thing in the room.

**Value 3 — Customer evidence over customer opinion.**

- *Behavioural anchors.* Every product decision is supported by named-customer evidence (transcripts, usage data, support tickets), not by summarised customer sentiment. Product managers publish the underlying evidence with the decision, not the summary. When evidence conflicts with a senior stakeholder's opinion, the evidence wins by default and the burden shifts to the stakeholder to produce counter-evidence.
- *What it costs.* Decisions take longer than they would if the CEO or Head of Product could just decide. Some senior stakeholders find the burden-of-proof reversal uncomfortable. The corporation over-invests in customer-research infrastructure relative to a similar-stage peer.

**Value 4 — Own the outcome, not the effort.**

- *Behavioural anchors.* Reviews and 1:1s focus on outcomes shipped, not hours worked or effort expended. When a project fails, the accountable owner is identifiable and identified. Missed commitments are named at the next standup, not buried.
- *What it costs.* People who work hard on things that do not ship do not get rewarded for the effort; some find this culturally harsh. Managers spend more energy on scoping ("what is the outcome we are committing to?") upfront, which slows the start of projects.

**Value 5 — Teach as you go.**

- *Behavioural anchors.* Every senior engineer or senior IC has at least one mentee on their team, with a named 1:1 cadence. Every non-trivial system has documentation authored by the person who built it. Onboarding buddies are a formal, rotating responsibility, not a favour.
- *What it costs.* Senior ICs spend 5–10% of their time on teaching activity that does not show up on a roadmap. The corporation accepts slightly slower senior-IC delivery in exchange for the compounding of the team's capability.

### The four anti-values

**Anti-value 1 — We do NOT run consensus-first meetings.**
The decision-owner decides after listening to the room. Meetings that end with "let's discuss more offline" without a named decision-owner are treated as failed meetings. This anti-value exists because consensus-first meetings look collegial and are, in practice, how decisions get delayed until someone else's calendar makes them.

**Anti-value 2 — We do NOT tolerate silent disagreement followed by back-channel Slack.**
The DM from Person A to Person B after a meeting saying "I actually disagreed with what X said" is the specific behaviour named. On first occurrence, the manager coaches. On repeat occurrence, this becomes a documented values-violation conversation. This is the enforcement teeth of Value 2.

**Anti-value 3 — We do NOT rate performance and cultural contribution together.**
A high-performing values-gap employee will be told clearly, in review, that their outcomes are strong and their values contribution is a gap. A strong culture-carrier who is under-performing will hear the same distinctness in the other direction. Merging the two into a single rating is prohibited at calibration.

**Anti-value 4 — We do NOT hire or promote managers who are values gaps.**
An IC who is a values gap can be coached in place. A manager who is a values gap propagates the gap into their entire team and is treated as an urgent case. Promotion to manager requires an above-bar values rating for the two most recent review cycles; hiring a manager from outside requires unanimous values-fit interview signal.

### Wiring into the operating stack

| Operating surface | How the values appear |
|---|---|
| Hiring loop (mod-104) | 45-minute values-fit interview, veto-holding; one values-anchored question in each function-specific interview |
| Review template (mod-107 ch. 01) | Separate values-rating section per value, calibrated alongside performance rating |
| Promotion criteria (mod-107 ch. 02) | Above-bar values rating required for manager promotions; values-gap block available to the promotion committee |
| Peer recognition | Slack-integrated recognition tags each nomination against one of the five values |
| Spot bonuses | Manager-nominated, head-of-people-reviewed, citation must reference a value and a behavioural anchor |
| Values-violation termination (mod-103 + mod-107 ch. 05) | Documented conversation → final written warning → termination; head-of-people gate before opening the case |
| Retrospective | 60-minute annual light-touch review; full rewrite deferred unless a genuine inflection occurs |

The list of five + four is stable across the last two annual reviews. The corporation has enforced against Anti-value 2 in one senior-leader case and against Anti-value 4 in one hiring case (rescinded offer post-values-fit). Both enforcement events were communicated at the level of principle (not personnel) at the next all-hands, so the org learned that the anti-values are load-bearing.

## Summary

- Generic values lists ("innovation, integrity, teamwork") are worse than no values — they teach the org that written commitments are decorative.
- A candidate value has to clear four tests: specific and non-generic, behaviourally anchored, trade-off explicit, disagreement-tolerant. Draft the trade-off paragraph before the value goes on the list.
- Author with a small exec drafting team (4–6) followed by a cross-functional read group (10–15). The read group's job is to catch aspirational-vs-actual drift.
- Anti-values ("we do NOT ...") are usually more useful than positive values because they are unambiguous. Resist every softening pressure between draft and publish.
- Wire values into hiring (values-fit interview + interleaved function questions), performance (separate values rating, calibrated), promotion (values bar for manager promotion), recognition (peer, spot bonus, formal awards), and offboarding (values-violation termination as a distinct path from performance-based termination).
- Defer the legal architecture of values-based termination to [mod-103](../mod-103-employment-law-and-contract-design/); defer the PIP / warning / termination mechanics to [mod-107 chapters 04 and 05](../mod-107-performance-promotion-and-offboarding/).
- Run an annual light-touch retrospective; rewrite in full only on a genuine inflection (new CEO, transformational acquisition, IPO, product pivot). A "values reset" every two or three years signals the previous set was never load-bearing.
