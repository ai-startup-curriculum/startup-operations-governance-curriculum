# 2. The employee handbook — NLRB-compliant structure post-Stericycle (2023)

> The handbook as the corporation's operating contract with every employee — structured for NLRA Section 7 safety under the Stericycle framework, layered with a state-and-local supplement matrix, and maintained on an update-and-re-acknowledgment cadence that keeps it enforceable.

## Motivation

The employee handbook is the single document that most employees will actually read (once, on day one, in a hurry) and that regulators, plaintiffs' counsel, and NLRB regional offices will read carefully. Get it wrong and the failure lands in one of two categories: (a) a facially-invalid workplace rule that draws an unfair-labor-practice charge under NLRA Section 7, or (b) a missed state-or-local supplement that leaves the corporation exposed on pay transparency, sick leave, biometric consent, or non-compete restrictions the founding team did not know applied.

The concrete failure modes recur:

- A confidentiality clause copied from a 2018 template forbids employees from "discussing the terms of their employment with any third party" — under NLRB Stericycle (2023) it is facially invalid, and the corporation receives an unfair-labor-practice charge months after a fired employee posts a screenshot on LinkedIn.
- A severance-and-release template still contains a broad non-disparagement clause covering "any statement about the corporation, its officers, or its employees." NLRB *McLaren Macomb* (2023) voids the clause for the non-supervisory pool, and the release the corporation paid for is unenforceable on the parts that mattered.
- A state passes pay-transparency legislation two years ago; the corporation's handbook and job-posting templates never absorbed the requirement; the state labor department opens an inquiry after a rejected candidate complaint, and the corporation faces per-posting penalties across the last 24 months of postings.
- The handbook was signed by the 2021 hire wave, updated three times since, and never re-acknowledged. A dispute arises over a 2024 policy the employee legitimately never saw; the handbook is now unenforceable on that policy.

This chapter builds the handbook as a living operating document: Section-7-safe at the federal layer, state-supplemented for every jurisdiction the corporation employs in, versioned and re-acknowledged on a documented cadence.

> **Legal-not-legal-advice caveat.** Employee-handbook drafting sits at the intersection of NLRA federal doctrine, EEOC guidance, and a rapidly-evolving state-and-local overlay. This chapter names the frameworks and the drafting posture; every handbook — and every material update — should be reviewed by employment counsel licensed in each state of operation before publication. See [resources.md](./resources.md) for primary-source citations and [mod-103](../mod-103-employment-law-and-contract-design/) for the underlying employment-law framework.

## The NLRB Stericycle (2023) framework

For a decade the NLRB reviewed workplace-rule facial validity under *Boeing* (2017) and its refinement in *LA Specialty Produce* (2019) — a three-category test that gave employers meaningful latitude to draft rules that had a plausible legitimate justification, even if the language was somewhat broad.

In **Stericycle, Inc.**, 372 NLRB No. 113 (2023), the Board overruled Boeing and LA Specialty Produce and installed a new, employer-unfriendly test. The core of the Stericycle framework:

- The General Counsel bears the initial burden of proving that a challenged workplace rule has a **reasonable tendency to chill employees in the exercise of their Section 7 rights** — where Section 7 covers concerted activity for mutual aid or protection, including discussing wages, hours, and terms of employment; organising; and concertedly complaining about working conditions.
- The rule is interpreted from the perspective of an **economically-dependent employee** who contemplates engaging in Section 7 activity and reads the rule; ambiguity is construed against the drafter (the employer).
- If the General Counsel meets that burden, the rule is **presumptively unlawful**.
- The employer can rebut the presumption only by proving that the rule **advances a legitimate and substantial business interest** and that the employer **could not achieve that interest with a more narrowly tailored rule**.

The operational consequences for handbook drafting are stark:

- The default posture flips. Under Boeing, "we have a plausible business reason" was often enough. Under Stericycle, "we have a legitimate business reason **and** we cannot achieve it with narrower language" is the standard, and the burden is on the employer.
- **Ambiguous rules lose.** A rule that a reasonable, cautious employee might read as covering Section 7 activity — even if the employer subjectively intended a narrower scope — is presumptively invalid.
- **Narrow tailoring becomes the drafting discipline.** Every workplace rule in the handbook should be re-read with the question: could this be written more narrowly and still serve the business purpose?

### What Stericycle means for common handbook clauses

The categories below are the clauses NLRB regional offices most frequently target under the Stericycle framework. Each is drafted with a specific narrowing pattern.

#### Confidentiality clauses

- **Do not sweep in** discussion of wages, hours, benefits, working conditions, or terms of employment. A blanket "employees must keep the terms of their employment confidential" clause is presumptively invalid under Stericycle and has been for a decade under prior Board doctrine.
- **Narrow tailoring pattern.** Restrict to specific, defined categories of business-sensitive information — customer lists, unreleased product plans, trade secrets, non-public financial information, personally-identifiable information of coworkers or customers — and expressly carve out Section 7-protected discussion.
- **Section 7 savings clause** at the end of the confidentiality section: "Nothing in this policy prohibits employees from discussing wages, hours, benefits, or other terms and conditions of employment with coworkers, or from engaging in other activity protected by Section 7 of the National Labor Relations Act."

#### Social-media policies

- **Do not prohibit** employees from discussing the workplace, coworkers, supervisors, or working conditions on social media where the discussion is protected concerted activity.
- **Do not require** employees to identify themselves as employees when posting, or to submit posts for pre-approval, on topics that touch working conditions.
- **Narrow tailoring pattern.** Restrict to (a) non-disclosure of the same defined confidential categories as above; (b) prohibition on speaking on behalf of the corporation without authorisation; (c) prohibition on unlawful harassment or discriminatory conduct.
- Include a Section 7 savings clause.

#### Non-disparagement policies

- **Non-disparagement clauses in the handbook itself** — as distinct from severance agreements — are difficult to sustain under Stericycle for the non-supervisory pool. A rule that prohibits "disparaging" statements about the corporation, its officers, or its employees will almost always be readable as chilling concerted complaint about working conditions.
- **NLRB *McLaren Macomb*, 372 NLRB No. 58 (2023)** — the parallel decision that governs non-disparagement and confidentiality clauses in **severance agreements** offered to non-supervisory employees. Overly broad clauses independently violate Section 8(a)(1). See [mod-107 chapter 05](../mod-107-performance-promotion-and-offboarding/05-termination-playbook.md) for the severance-agreement mechanics.
- **Narrow tailoring pattern for the handbook.** Restrict to (a) knowingly false statements about the corporation's products or services, (b) statements made in the course of unlawful harassment or discrimination. Do not attempt a blanket non-disparagement rule.

#### Recording policies

- **Blanket "no recording in the workplace" rules are presumptively invalid** — NLRB doctrine treats workplace recording as potentially protected Section 7 activity (documentation of unsafe conditions, discrimination, or wage-and-hour violations that could support a concerted complaint).
- **Narrow tailoring pattern.** Prohibit recording only in specific, justifiable contexts — attorney-client-privileged conversations, confidential HR investigation interviews, spaces where recording would violate customer or patient privacy (HIPAA, attorney-client, etc.), or where recording would capture trade-secret information.
- Do not draft the rule as "no recording without manager approval" — that framing puts the manager in the position of denying recording that would document a Section 7 grievance.

#### Civility and professional-conduct rules

- **Do not chill protected concerted speech.** A rule requiring employees to "treat all coworkers with respect at all times" or to avoid "negative or disparaging comments about the company" is readable, from the economically-dependent-employee perspective, as forbidding concerted complaint about working conditions.
- **Narrow tailoring pattern.** Anchor civility rules in the corporation's unlawful-harassment and non-discrimination policies (which are themselves statutory obligations under Title VII and state analogues) rather than in an open-ended civility mandate. Prohibit conduct that is unlawful, threatening, or violent; do not prohibit conduct that is merely unpleasant, critical, or negative.

#### Off-duty-conduct rules

- **Protected concerted activity off-duty remains protected.** A rule that reaches an employee's conduct outside of work — social-media posts about the corporation, participation in organising activity in a public forum, statements to the press about working conditions — is presumptively invalid where the off-duty conduct is Section 7-protected.
- **Narrow tailoring pattern.** Off-duty-conduct rules should be limited to specific, work-related nexus categories — off-duty conduct that violates the corporation's harassment policy toward coworkers, off-duty use of confidential information, off-duty criminal conduct that materially affects the employee's ability to perform the job.

### The Section 7 savings clause

The savings clause is not a magic incantation — the NLRB has repeatedly held that a general Section 7 disclaimer does not cure a facially-invalid workplace rule. But a properly-drafted savings clause is a necessary belt-and-suspenders posture:

> **Section 7 rights.** Nothing in this handbook is intended to prohibit, restrict, or interfere with employees' rights under Section 7 of the National Labor Relations Act, including the right to discuss wages, hours, benefits, and other terms and conditions of employment; to engage in concerted activity for mutual aid or protection; and to organise. If any provision of this handbook is interpreted to conflict with Section 7 rights, that provision is not enforceable to that extent.

Place the savings clause both at the top of the handbook (in the introduction) and at the end of each specific policy category (confidentiality, social media, recording, civility, off-duty conduct) where the underlying rule is most likely to be challenged.

## The state-and-local supplement matrix

The federal-law layer (Title VII, ADEA, ADA, FMLA, FLSA, OSHA, NLRA) is the same everywhere. The state-and-local layer varies dramatically. Every handbook needs a **state-supplement structure** — either a single handbook with a state-appendix section keyed to each state of operation, or separate state-specific handbooks issued to employees in each jurisdiction. The single-handbook-with-appendices structure is the more common pattern below Series-C headcount.

### Drafting posture within each chapter

- Use plain language, second-person voice ("You are entitled to…", "You must…"), and bulleted / tabular structure over dense prose.
- Every policy that touches a workplace rule (code of conduct, technology, social media, off-duty conduct, recording) closes with a Section 7 savings clause.
- Every policy that has a state overlay includes an in-chapter cross-reference to the state-supplement appendix, so the state-based employee reads both.
- Every policy that defers to a sibling module includes a link, not a restatement — restating creates a divergence risk between the handbook and the substantive policy.

## The handbook-signature workflow

The handbook is enforceable against the employee only to the extent the employee has been given the current version and has acknowledged it. The signature workflow is the operational hinge.

### At-hire signature

- Handbook is delivered electronically via the HRIS on or before day 1 (see [mod-104 chapter 07](../mod-104-hiring-onboarding-and-hr-operations/07-hris-peo-payroll-stack.md) for the HRIS architecture — Rippling, Justworks, Gusto, Deel, Workday all support handbook-acknowledgment workflows).
- Employee reviews the handbook and electronically signs an acknowledgment that includes:
  - Receipt of the current version of the handbook (versioned by date and revision number).
  - Understanding that the handbook is not a contract and does not modify the at-will nature of employment.
  - Agreement to abide by the handbook's terms.
  - Understanding that the corporation may revise the handbook at any time and that continued employment following revision constitutes acknowledgment of the revised terms.
- Acknowledgment is completed before or on day 1; the HRBP or people-ops runs a report weekly to catch missed acknowledgments.
- The signed acknowledgment is stored in the HRIS employee-file module with immutable audit metadata (timestamp, IP address, document version).

### Re-acknowledgment on material update

- **Material update trigger.** A material update is any change that (a) modifies an employee's rights or obligations (leave entitlements, PTO accrual, discipline process, code of conduct), (b) responds to a legal-compliance requirement (new state law, NLRB memorandum, new EEOC guidance), or (c) reflects a policy-level decision (RTO shift, new AI-usage policy).
- **Not a material update.** Typo fixes, formatting changes, updated office addresses, updated benefits-vendor names. These are captured in the version-history log but do not trigger re-acknowledgment.
- **Re-acknowledgment mechanics.** The HRIS pushes a re-acknowledgment task to all affected employees within 5 business days of the update effective date. Employees have 10 business days to complete. Non-completion escalates to the manager and then the HRBP.
- **Delta-only presentation.** The re-acknowledgment screen should show the specific delta from the prior version (redline or callout) rather than requiring the employee to re-read the entire handbook — the failure mode is employees clicking "acknowledged" without reading, which the delta view materially reduces.

### Retention

- Handbook acknowledgments are retained for the duration of employment plus the applicable statute-of-limitations period for employment claims — practically, at least **4 years post-termination** for federal claims, longer for some state-law claims. The HRIS retention configuration should reflect the longest applicable jurisdictional retention obligation.
- Prior versions of the handbook itself must be retained — if a dispute arises over a 2024 policy, the corporation needs to produce the exact 2024 version, not the current version.
- The version-history log (see next section) is a corporate record and should be retained indefinitely.

## The handbook-update cadence

The handbook is a living document, not a founding artifact. A defensible cadence has two rhythms.

### Annual full review

- Once per year (typically Q4, in advance of the new plan year), the head of people, general counsel or outside employment counsel, and the HRBP conduct a full handbook review.
- Every chapter is re-read against current law, current corporation policy, and NLRB / EEOC / state-agency developments over the prior 12 months.
- The annual review closes with either (a) a batch handbook update effective January 1, or (b) a documented "no changes required" note in the version-history log.
- Employees complete an annual re-acknowledgment even if no changes were made — this closes the loop on the hire-wave-that-never-re-acknowledged failure mode from the motivation section.

### Ad-hoc update on regulatory or corporate change

Ad-hoc updates are triggered by discrete events. The trigger inventory includes:

- **New state or local statute** in a jurisdiction of operation — pay transparency, sick leave, non-compete restriction, biometric consent.
- **New NLRB decision or General Counsel memorandum** materially affecting handbook doctrine (e.g., Stericycle 2023 replacing Boeing; McLaren Macomb 2023 on severance-agreement clauses).
- **New EEOC guidance** — most recent examples include guidance on AI in employment decisions, harassment guidance, pregnancy accommodation under the PWFA.
- **New state AI-in-hiring statute** — NYC Local Law 144 (2023) on automated employment-decision tools; California AB 331 developments; other state analogues emerging annually.
- **IRS or DOL rulemaking** — 401(k) contribution limits, FSA / HSA limits, overtime-exemption salary thresholds, FMLA definitional updates.
- **Corporation policy decision** — RTO change, new equity-plan mechanics, new benefits program, new AI-tool acceptable-use policy.
- **Litigation or agency-action feedback** — an NLRB regional office challenges a specific clause; the corporation loses or settles a claim that surfaces a drafting weakness; employment counsel identifies a risk in a diligence review.

Each ad-hoc update is captured in the version-history log with the trigger event, the affected clauses, and the re-acknowledgment status.

## Concrete example: a Series-B handbook's 12-month update log

A hypothetical Series-B B2B SaaS corporation, headquartered in San Francisco, with employees in California, New York, Colorado, and Washington. The update log for the trailing 12 months, showing six triggered updates:

| Date | Trigger event | Affected clauses | Re-acknowledgment mechanism |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Feb — v3.1 | Colorado FAMLI benefit payments begin; contribution mechanics finalised | Colorado state-supplement: Paid Family Leave section; PTO chapter cross-reference | CO employees re-acknowledge within 10 business days via Rippling task |
| Apr — v3.2 | EEOC harassment guidance (2024) published | Chapter 8 (Harassment / non-discrimination / retaliation); Chapter 13 (Complaint / grievance procedure) | All employees re-acknowledge; annual training rescheduled to reflect updated guidance |
| Jun — v3.3 | Washington RCW 49.58.110 pay-range enforcement clarification; internal audit of open job postings | Chapter 3 (Compensation); job-posting template (external to handbook but linked); Washington state supplement | WA employees re-acknowledge; hiring-manager training refresh |
| Aug — v3.4 | NLRB General Counsel memorandum on non-compete provisions in employment agreements | Chapter 12 (IP and confidentiality); at-hire PIIA template (external — see [mod-102](../mod-102-founding-team-legal-architecture/)) | All employees re-acknowledge; PIIA re-executed for affected employees per counsel guidance |
| Oct — v3.5 | Corporation adopts an approved-AI-tools list and updates AI-usage acceptable-use policy | Chapter 10 (Technology, acceptable use, BYOD, AI usage); cross-reference to [chapter 06](./06-ai-usage-and-acceptable-use-policy.md) | All employees re-acknowledge; delta view highlights approved-tools list and prohibited-use categories |
| Dec — v4.0 | Annual full review; batch update of state supplements for the new plan year; refresh of NLRA Section 7 savings clause in light of most recent Board decisions | Multiple chapters (all state supplements; Chapter 7 code of conduct; Chapter 10 technology) | All employees complete annual re-acknowledgment effective January 1; hire-wave employees who had never re-acknowledged now on the current version |

The pattern to notice: six triggered updates in a year is a normal cadence for a Series-B in this footprint. A corporation that updated its handbook once in 12 months is either not tracking regulatory change or is running on an out-of-date document.

## Summary

- The handbook is the corporation's operating contract with every employee; it is drafted from the federal NLRA Section 7 baseline, layered with a state-and-local supplement structure, and maintained on an update-and-re-acknowledgment cadence.
- **NLRB Stericycle (2023)** replaced Boeing / LA Specialty Produce and installed a plaintiff-friendly test: an ambiguous rule that a reasonable, economically-dependent employee could interpret as chilling Section 7 activity is presumptively unlawful, and the burden is on the employer to prove narrow tailoring to a legitimate and substantial business interest.
- Six clause categories are the most frequent Stericycle targets: **confidentiality, social media, non-disparagement, recording, civility, off-duty conduct**. Each is drafted with a specific narrowing pattern plus a Section 7 savings clause.
- **NLRB McLaren Macomb (2023)** governs non-disparagement and confidentiality clauses in severance agreements for non-supervisory employees — see [mod-107 chapter 05](../mod-107-performance-promotion-and-offboarding/05-termination-playbook.md).
- Every state of operation gets a **state-supplement appendix** covering paid sick leave, paid family leave, pay transparency, non-compete restrictions, biometric-consent (IL BIPA), CCPA employee-data (CA), and other jurisdiction-specific obligations. California, New York, Illinois, Colorado, and Washington are the most frequently-triggered supplements; the list is not exhaustive.
- The **standard chapter structure** — welcome, employment basics, compensation, benefits, time off, remote / hybrid / RTO, code of conduct, harassment, safety, technology, privacy, IP, complaints, discipline, separation, acknowledgment — with each chapter cross-referencing the sibling modules where substantive policy lives.
- The **signature workflow** — electronic acknowledgment via HRIS on day 1, re-acknowledgment on material update within 10 business days, retention for at least 4 years post-termination.
- The **update cadence** — annual full review with a batch effective January 1, plus ad-hoc updates on regulatory change (new state statute, new NLRB memorandum, new EEOC guidance, new state AI-in-hiring law like NYC Local Law 144, IRS / DOL rulemaking).
- Six triggered updates in 12 months is a normal Series-B cadence; a handbook that has not been touched in a year is a defensive-posture failure.
