# 3. The corporate-secretary function

> The keeper of the corporate record — minutes, consents, indemnification agreements, and the cadence calendar that keeps the record clean between board meetings.

## Motivation

The corporate secretary is the least glamorous seat in the boardroom and the one whose work survives longest. Every governance artefact a future acquirer, IPO underwriter, plaintiff's lawyer, or DOJ line prosecutor will read — minutes, unanimous written consents, committee charters, indemnification agreements, the share ledger — passes through the secretary's hands. Sloppy minutes cost a director the business-judgment presumption. A missing consent invalidates a stock issuance. An unsigned indemnification agreement leaves an officer personally exposed the first time a shareholder-derivative complaint names them. This chapter is the operating manual for the secretary function: who wears the hat, what artefacts they produce, and the cadence that keeps the record defensible.

## Who wears the hat

DGCL § 142(a) requires every Delaware corporation to have "such officers with such titles and duties as shall be stated in the bylaws" and specifically contemplates a secretary as the officer responsible for maintaining the corporate record. The bylaws designate the office; the board elects the individual.

At early stage, the general counsel or the chief operating officer typically wears the corporate-secretary hat as a second title — the role is real but the volume is manageable alongside a primary function. As the company scales through growth stage and toward IPO readiness, the volume of consents, committee meetings, subsidiary maintenance, and § 16 filings grows past what a fractional office can absorb, and the function usually splits out: a dedicated deputy general counsel — corporate & securities, or a chief-of-staff-to-the-general-counsel role, takes over day-to-day secretary work while the GC retains signatory authority for the most sensitive artefacts. Public companies almost always maintain a full-time corporate secretary and one or more assistant secretaries, because the SEC filings, NYSE/Nasdaq listing rules, and stockholder-meeting mechanics require dedicated bandwidth.

The bylaws should permit "assistant secretaries" so that any properly appointed deputy can countersign consents and certify resolutions when the primary secretary is unavailable — this avoids a single-person bottleneck on stock issuances and financing closings.

## Meeting minutes

Minutes are the primary evidence of board deliberation. If the corporation is later sued in a shareholder-derivative case, a Section 220 books-and-records demand, or an M&A dispute, the minutes are what a court reads to decide whether the directors were informed, engaged, and acting in good faith.

The disciplined default is the **action-and-decision style**: record who attended, what materials were pre-read, which advisors presented, what topics were discussed at a high level, what motion was made, who moved and seconded, who voted for, against, abstained, or recused, and what was resolved. Do **not** transcribe the discussion verbatim; verbatim minutes create a discoverable narrative of every director's every offhand comment, which is exactly the record a plaintiff's lawyer wants and exactly the record that undermines the business-judgment presumption when a comment is quoted out of context.

The alternative — the **narrative style** — reads more like meeting notes: it captures the give-and-take of the discussion, alternative options considered, and the concerns raised. Narrative minutes are richer institutional memory and are sometimes useful in regulated industries where the record must show that specific factors were considered. The trade-off is litigation exposure: every sentence in narrative minutes is a sentence a plaintiff will quote back at the directors.

Mature practice: action-and-decision style as the default, narrative style used sparingly and deliberately for specific agenda items where the record of factors-considered protects the directors (for example, a Revlon-mode sale-process discussion or a going-concern determination).

Minutes are drafted by the secretary during or immediately after the meeting, circulated to the CEO and general counsel for accuracy review, then presented for board approval at the next meeting. Approved minutes are entered in the minute book with the meeting's exhibits (board materials, presentations, resolutions) indexed by exhibit number.

### Minutes excerpt — action-and-decision style

> **Approval of Series C Financing.** Ms. Chen, Chief Financial Officer, and Mr. Patel of Latham & Watkins LLP presented the terms of the proposed Series C Preferred Stock financing, including the term sheet from Redwood Capital Partners as lead investor (previously distributed as Exhibit 4.2), the pro forma capitalization table (Exhibit 4.3), the form of Amended and Restated Certificate of Incorporation (Exhibit 4.4), and the form of Series C Preferred Stock Purchase Agreement, Investors' Rights Agreement, Right of First Refusal and Co-Sale Agreement, and Voting Agreement (Exhibits 4.5 through 4.8). The Board reviewed the pricing, the resulting dilution to existing stockholders, the option-pool expansion contemplated by the term sheet, and the director-nomination provisions granting Redwood Capital Partners the right to designate one director. The Board considered the fairness of the transaction to the Corporation and its stockholders, having received the presentation materials in advance of the meeting.
>
> Upon motion duly made by Mr. Ortiz and seconded by Ms. Nakamura, the Board unanimously adopted the resolutions attached as Exhibit 4.9, approving (i) the Amended and Restated Certificate of Incorporation, (ii) the Series C financing transaction and the definitive agreements substantially in the forms presented, (iii) the increase in shares reserved under the 2022 Equity Incentive Plan by 4,500,000 shares subject to stockholder approval, and (iv) the appointment of Ms. Rivera as a Class II director effective upon the closing of the financing, subject to receipt of her executed indemnification agreement and director-and-officer questionnaire. Ms. Rivera was not present and did not participate in the deliberation or vote.

Note what the excerpt does not do: it does not quote any director's remarks, does not describe the debate over the option-pool sizing, and does not attribute any concern to a named director. It records the materials, the process, and the decision.

## Board written consents

Between meetings, boards act by unanimous written consent under DGCL § 141(f). The statute makes the consent effective when the last director signs, or at a later effective time specified in the consent. Unanimity is the default and cannot be relaxed unless the bylaws or charter explicitly permit less-than-unanimous board consents (rare and generally disfavored).

The secretary's discipline: (1) circulate the draft consent and every referenced exhibit through the board portal (Diligent, BoardEffect, Nasdaq Boardvantage, or an equivalent) with a defined signature SLA — typically 48 or 72 hours for routine consents, same-day for closings; (2) collect signatures via the portal's e-signature workflow (which produces an audit trail); (3) countersign as secretary attesting to the consent's execution; (4) file the executed consent in the minute book with the version-controlled exhibit set; (5) distribute the executed consent back to the directors for their records.

### Written-consent template outline

1. **Caption.** "Action by Unanimous Written Consent of the Board of Directors of [Corporation], a Delaware corporation," with the effective date.
2. **Preamble.** Recital that the undersigned, constituting all of the directors of the Corporation, take the following actions by written consent in lieu of a meeting pursuant to DGCL § 141(f) and the Corporation's Bylaws.
3. **Recitals ("WHEREAS" clauses).** One recital per material fact the Board is being asked to rely on — e.g., that the Corporation has negotiated the attached Series C term sheet; that the Compensation Committee has reviewed and recommended the attached option grants; that management has recommended the attached commercial contract as being in the best interests of the Corporation.
4. **Resolutions ("RESOLVED" clauses).** The operative approvals, drafted as standalone resolutions so each can be extracted as a certified excerpt. Include an omnibus "further-actions" resolution authorizing the officers to take all actions and execute all documents necessary or advisable to effect the foregoing.
5. **Exhibits list.** Each material document referenced by the resolutions attached as a numbered exhibit (Exhibit A: form of Purchase Agreement; Exhibit B: form of Amended Charter; Exhibit C: option-grant schedule; etc.).
6. **Signature blocks.** One signature block per director, with a date line. E-signature blocks acceptable; DGCL § 116 recognizes electronic signatures on corporate consents.
7. **Secretary's countersignature and attestation.** The corporate secretary countersigns attesting that the consent was duly executed by all directors and is filed in the corporate records.

## Stockholder consents

Stockholders act between meetings under DGCL § 228, which — for private companies whose bylaws permit it — allows action by written consent signed by holders of shares representing at least the minimum number of votes that would be required to authorize the action at a meeting. Majority written consent is the private-company default; unanimity is not required. DGCL § 228(e) then requires the corporation to give **prompt notice** of the action taken to any stockholders who did not sign the consent, so that non-consenting holders are on notice and can exercise any statutory rights (such as appraisal under DGCL § 262) that depend on notice timing.

Public-company bylaws almost always **prohibit** stockholder action by written consent, forcing stockholder action into the annual or special-meeting process where the SEC proxy rules apply. The transition from private-company § 228 practice to public-company prohibition is a bylaws amendment adopted as part of IPO readiness (see [chapter 09](./09-ipo-readiness-and-transition-to-public-governance.md)).

## Committee charters — the meta-pattern

Each standing committee operates under a written charter approved by the board. The secretary function's role at this level is to (a) maintain the charter repository, (b) tee up an annual charter review by each committee and the full board, (c) publish the current charters on the corporate governance page of the company website once the company is public (an NYSE/Nasdaq listing-rule requirement), and (d) track charter amendments in the minute book. The substantive content of the audit, compensation, and nominating/governance committee charters is covered in [chapter 04](./04-standing-committees.md).

## Officer-and-director indemnification agreements

DGCL § 145 is the statutory baseline. Section 145(a) and (b) permit the corporation to indemnify directors and officers against expenses, judgments, and settlements arising from their service, subject to a good-faith standard. Section 145(c) makes indemnification **mandatory** to the extent the director or officer has been "successful on the merits or otherwise" in defense of the proceeding. Section 145(e) permits advancement of expenses incurred in defending a proceeding in advance of final disposition, upon an undertaking by the indemnitee to repay if it is ultimately determined that indemnification is not available.

The § 145 baseline is not enough. Every director and officer signs an individual indemnification agreement that layers contractual protections on top of the statute. The mature agreement — most commonly modeled on the NVCA form indemnification agreement — provides:

- **Mandatory indemnification** to the fullest extent permitted by law, not merely permissive; the corporation gives up the discretion § 145 grants it.
- **Mandatory advancement** of expenses under § 145(e) with the required undertaking to repay, including a defined turnaround time (commonly within 20 or 30 days of the indemnitee's written request) so that defense costs do not force the officer into personal cash-flow distress.
- **Presumption in favor of the indemnitee** on the question of whether the good-faith standard is met, with the corporation bearing the burden of rebutting the presumption; and a right to independent-counsel determination if the corporation contests entitlement.
- **Priority-of-payments** among indemnity sources. The NVCA-style model typically treats the corporation's indemnity as primary and the D&O policy as secondary, with any investor-affiliated indemnity (from a venture fund whose partner sits on the board) as tertiary. Some agreements — particularly those negotiated by investor-designated directors — flip the priority so that corporate indemnity is primary but the fund's indemnity is expressly excess and non-contributing. Getting this stack right matters when a claim actually hits, because it determines which insurer or balance sheet pays first.

The D&O program itself — Side A, B, and C coverage, tower structure, retentions, and the trip wires that cause coverage to fail — is the subject of [chapter 06](./06-do-insurance-program.md).

The secretary's job at intake: every new director and every new Section 16 officer signs an indemnification agreement on their first day. No exceptions, no "we'll get to it next week." The signed agreement is filed in the minute book and a signed original is delivered to the officer.

## The corporate-secretary cadence calendar

The secretary function is a set of recurring obligations, not a project. The disciplined cadence:

- **Annual bylaws review.** The general counsel and secretary walk the bylaws in Q4, flag amendments driven by DGCL changes or company-stage transitions, and tee up any needed amendments for board approval.
- **Annual committee-charter review.** Each committee reviews its own charter at a scheduled meeting; the full board ratifies the reviewed charters.
- **Annual board-evaluation kickoff.** The nominating/governance committee (or, pre-committee, the full board) runs a board self-evaluation — either questionnaire-based or interview-based — with results reported in aggregate and used to inform director-refreshment discussions.
- **Quarterly consent-tracking audit.** The secretary reconciles the list of consents circulated in the quarter against the list of consents executed and filed, resolves any stragglers, and flags any resolutions that authorized further-actions never taken.
- **Annual minute-book close-out.** At fiscal year-end (or immediately after the year-end board meeting), the secretary closes out the minute book for the year: index of meetings and consents, exhibit index, share-ledger reconciliation, and a written attestation that the record is complete. This is the artefact an M&A diligence room will produce first.
- **Quarterly indemnification-agreement re-attestation** for any new directors or officers appointed during the quarter — confirmation that each has signed the current form of agreement and that D&O policy schedules have been updated to name them.

Automating this cadence in a board portal or a shared corporate-secretary tracker (Diligent Entities, Athennian, or a purpose-built airtable/Notion database) keeps the recurring obligations from slipping through personnel changes.

## Summary

- The corporate secretary is a bylaws-designated officer under DGCL § 142 whose product is the corporate record — minutes, consents, charters, and indemnification agreements — that will be read years later by acquirers, regulators, and litigants.
- Action-and-decision minutes are the disciplined default; narrative minutes should be used sparingly for agenda items where the record of factors-considered protects the directors.
- Board unanimous written consents under DGCL § 141(f) are effective when the last director signs; the secretary owns the circulation-signature-filing pipeline and the version-controlled exhibit set.
- Stockholder consents follow DGCL § 228 majority-written-consent rules for private companies with a § 228(e) prompt-notice obligation to non-consenting holders; public-company bylaws almost always prohibit stockholder written consent.
- Indemnification agreements extend the DGCL § 145 baseline into mandatory indemnification, mandatory advancement under § 145(e), a presumption for the indemnitee, and a defined priority-of-payments stack; every new director and Section 16 officer signs on day one.
- The secretary function runs on a cadence — annual bylaws review, annual charter review, annual board evaluation, quarterly consent audits, annual minute-book close-out, quarterly indemnification re-attestation — not on ad hoc effort.
