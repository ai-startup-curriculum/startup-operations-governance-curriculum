# 1. Board architecture and the composition trajectory

> The bylaws-level rules that govern how the board operates, and the stage-by-stage composition arc from a two-founder board to a pre-IPO independent-majority board.

## Motivation

The board is the corporation's decision-making organ, and it is the only body that can lawfully take many of the actions a venture-backed company depends on — issuing stock, adopting equity plans, approving budgets, hiring and firing officers, approving financings and M&A. Whether the board can act quickly, act remotely, act in writing, and act with a quorum of one or two directors when a co-founder is unreachable is not decided in the moment; it is decided in the bylaws, at formation, and again at each restatement. This chapter covers the bylaws-level architecture that defines *how* the board operates, and the composition arc that defines *who* sits on it as the company progresses from pre-seed to pre-IPO. Founder-side seat mechanics (which founder holds a founder seat, what happens to the seat on departure) live in [mod-102](../mod-102-founding-team-legal-architecture/); investor-side seat rights (who gets a director designation in the Voting Agreement) live in the fundraising curriculum. This chapter owns the bylaws and the composition trajectory itself.

## Bylaws design for the board

The bylaws are adopted under DGCL § 109 and can be amended by the board unless the charter reserves the power to stockholders. The board-facing sections of a modern startup's bylaws deserve deliberate drafting; the boilerplate that comes out of an incorporation-service template is almost never what a Series-A board will want operating under.

- **Number of directors — fixed vs. range.** DGCL § 141(b) permits the bylaws to fix the number of directors *or* to set a range (a minimum and a maximum) with the exact number set by board resolution. A range is the modern default. It lets the board add a new investor director on Series-A closing day by simple resolution rather than a bylaw amendment, and it accommodates independents added over the next 24 months without redrafting the bylaws each time. A common range at formation is one to nine; some counsel start at one to seven and expand at Series-B.
- **Quorum.** DGCL § 141(b) sets the default quorum at a majority of the whole board, and permits the bylaws or charter to reduce that quorum — but "in no case shall a quorum consist of less than one-third of the total number of directors." A three-director board therefore has a floor of one director for quorum purposes, which is almost never desirable; most bylaws leave quorum at a majority. A quorum below majority should only be used deliberately (e.g., a very large public-company board where scheduling forces it), and never to let a single interested director act alone.
- **Notice of meetings.** Bylaws set the required advance notice for regular and special meetings, the permitted delivery methods (email is now standard), and who may call a special meeting (typically the chair, the CEO, or any two directors). Notice can be waived under DGCL § 229 by written waiver or by attendance without objection, and a well-drafted bylaws section says so.
- **Remote participation.** DGCL § 141(i) permits any board or committee meeting to be held by conference telephone or other communications equipment "by means of which all persons participating in the meeting can hear each other," and participation constitutes presence. Bylaws should incorporate § 141(i) so that a video board meeting is unambiguously a valid meeting, not a defective one that a plaintiff later attacks.
- **Action by unanimous written consent.** DGCL § 141(f) permits the board to act without a meeting by unanimous written consent, and the consent may be signed electronically. This is how the vast majority of board decisions get memorialised between quarterly meetings — routine option grants, form-of-agreement approvals, banking-authority updates. Bylaws should track § 141(f) verbatim and confirm electronic signatures are acceptable.
- **Officers.** DGCL § 142 permits any officer titles the bylaws provide, and the same person may hold multiple offices. Most modern startups elect at minimum a CEO, a President (often the same person as the CEO at formation), a Secretary, and a Treasurer or CFO; the bylaws should list the officer roles the corporation will maintain and specify who has authority to bind the corporation on contracts, banking, and stock issuances. Signing authority delegated through officer titles is covered further in [chapter 02](./02-fiduciary-duties-and-standards-of-review.md) and [chapter 03](./03-corporate-secretary-function.md).

### How bylaws evolve between formation and pre-IPO

At formation, the bylaws typically permit stockholder action by less-than-unanimous written consent under DGCL § 228. This is essential in a private company: the founders (and later a small syndicate of preferred holders) need to approve financings, plan increases, and charter amendments without convening a formal stockholder meeting. On the road to an IPO the bylaws (and often the charter) are restated to *prohibit* stockholder written consent, because public-company governance norms and stock-exchange listing standards expect all stockholder action to occur at a duly-noticed meeting with proxy solicitation. Similarly, quorum thresholds tighten, advance-notice bylaws for stockholder nominations get added, and forum-selection clauses get reconfirmed. The bylaws are not a one-time document; they are restated at Series-A (to accommodate the new board composition and investor rights), often again at Series-C, and finally in the S-1 as the "public-company bylaws" that take effect on effectiveness of the IPO.

## The board-composition trajectory

Board composition tracks fundraising stage almost mechanically. The pattern below is the modern venture-backed default; specific rounds can vary, but a corporate secretary who cannot describe this arc will be surprised by the seat count they inherit.

- **Pre-seed / seed pre-lead.** Founder-only board, one or two seats. A solo-founder company usually has a one-director board (the founder); a two-founder company typically has both founders on the board. There is no investor seat because there is no priced round yet.
- **Seat with a lead investor (Series Seed or similar).** Two founders plus one investor director, for three seats total. The investor seat is *not* created by the bylaws — it is conferred by the Voting Agreement (in NVCA-style seed documents, or in a Series Seed voting agreement). The bylaws expand the number of seats (via the range in § 141(b)) and the Voting Agreement obligates the stockholders to elect the lead investor's designee to the new seat.
- **Series-A.** The typical NVCA-form Series-A produces a five-seat board: two seats designated by the common (usually the two founders, or the CEO plus one other), one seat designated by the Series-A preferred (the lead investor), and two independents jointly designated by the common and the preferred acting together. A 3+1+1 variant (three common, one preferred, one joint independent) is also common where the founders want to preserve common-side control for another round. The Voting Agreement, not the bylaws, allocates who designates each seat.
- **Series-B / Series-C.** Successive priced rounds progressively dilute the founder share of the board. Series-B typically adds a seat for the Series-B lead, sometimes shifting a common seat to an independent, producing a six- or seven-seat board. Board *observers* — non-voting attendees with contractual attendance rights, granted to funds that did not get a full director seat — proliferate at this stage; a well-drafted Voting Agreement caps observer counts and requires observer confidentiality undertakings. By Series-C most boards have seven seats, with a majority no longer controlled by founders or any single investor.
- **Pre-IPO.** Seven to nine seats with a majority of independent directors is the target composition before an S-1 filing, driven by NYSE / Nasdaq listing standards and by the audit-committee independence requirements of Rule 10A-3 under the Exchange Act. If the CEO is also the chair, the independent directors typically designate a "lead independent director" to run executive sessions and to serve as the point of contact for stockholder engagement — an increasingly standard governance feature that stockholder-advisory firms and index-fund stewardship teams look for. Committee composition (audit, compensation, nominating & governance) becomes fully independent in the run-up to IPO, and committee charters are restated for public-company standards.

## Independent-director recruitment

Once the board expands beyond founders and investor designees, the corporate secretary and CEO (with GC support) run a deliberate independent-director search. Two distinct sourcing paths exist.

- **Board-network platforms.** Purpose-built platforms surface first-time and diverse director candidates who might not appear on a traditional search firm's slate. Verified resources include **Bolster**, **theBoardlist**, **Extraordinary Women on Boards**, **Him for Her**, **Ascend Pinnacle** (Asian and Pacific Islander executives), and **Nurole**. These platforms are typically the right first call for a Series-A or Series-B independent seat, where the company wants a working director rather than a marquee name.
- **Executive-search firms.** For a pre-IPO independent director — particularly the future audit chair or compensation chair — engaging one of the major search firms produces a curated slate, reference checks, and a formal process the board can point to in an S-1 governance narrative. The four firms most commonly engaged for board searches are **Spencer Stuart**, **Heidrick & Struggles**, **Russell Reynolds**, and **Egon Zehnder**.

Selection criteria for the board slate should be written down before the first candidate conversation. The recurring criteria are: **financial literacy** sufficient to serve on the audit committee and, ideally, one candidate with the SEC "audit committee financial expert" profile who can chair audit; **executive-compensation fluency** for the future compensation-committee chair (prior comp-committee service, or a CHRO / total-rewards background); **domain expertise** relevant to the business (a customer-industry operator, a technical expert, a go-to-market leader); **DEI composition** across gender, race, and background, both because it is the right composition for decision quality and because institutional investors and stock exchanges increasingly measure it; and **referenceability** — candidates whose reputations survive backchannel diligence with prior CEOs and co-directors.

## New-director onboarding package

Every new director — investor designee or independent — should receive a standard onboarding package from the corporate secretary before their first meeting. The package is a governance artefact; a director who later claims they did not know the company's obligations is worse off, and the company is worse off, if the onboarding record is incomplete. The standard package:

1. **Charter and bylaws** (as currently in effect, with a redline against the prior restatement).
2. **All executed board consents from the trailing 24 months**, indexed by date and subject.
3. **D&O policy summary and the director's executed indemnification agreement.** The indemnification agreement is signed on or before the director's first day.
4. **Organisational chart** and current headcount.
5. **Cap table** (current, with fully-diluted and outstanding views) and the most recent 409A valuation.
6. **The last three board packs** in full, so the new director can see the flow of information the board has been operating on.
7. **Standing-committee charters** (audit, compensation, nominating & governance — whichever exist at the current stage), covered in [chapter 04](./04-standing-committees.md).
8. **Code of ethics and insider-trading policy.** The insider-trading policy is signed and returned.
9. **Calendar of upcoming meetings** for the next twelve months, including committee meetings, plus the annual planning cycle.
10. **A formal orientation session** with the CEO, CFO, GC, and the audit-committee chair — usually one to two hours, scheduled within two weeks of the director's election.

Assembly and ownership of the onboarding package sits with the corporate-secretary function described in [chapter 03](./03-corporate-secretary-function.md).

## Coordination boundary

Two adjacent topics look like they belong in this chapter and do not:

- **Founder-side seat mechanics** — which of the founders holds the founder seat, what happens to that seat on a founder's departure, and how the founders vote among themselves on board-level decisions — belong to [mod-102](../mod-102-founding-team-legal-architecture/), which owns the founder-to-founder legal architecture.
- **Investor-side seat rights** — which fund gets a director designation at which round, the drafting of the Voting Agreement's director-election provisions, and observer rights — emerge in the priced-round Voting Agreement and are owned by the startup-finance-fundraising curriculum.

This chapter's job is the bylaws-level operating rules and the composition arc; the seat-allocation contracts that populate the arc live in those adjacent bodies of material.

## Summary

- The bylaws are the operating manual for the board: director count under DGCL § 141(b), quorum under § 141(b) (not below one-third), notice of meetings, remote participation under § 141(i), action by unanimous written consent under § 141(f), and officer roles under § 142.
- A range for director count (e.g., one to nine) beats a fixed number — it lets the board add seats by resolution as new investors and independents come on.
- Bylaws evolve on the road to IPO: stockholder written consent goes from permitted (private) to prohibited (public), advance-notice bylaws get added, quorum and forum clauses tighten.
- Composition tracks fundraising stage: founder-only at pre-seed; 2+1 at seed with a lead; the NVCA 2+1+2 (or 3+1+1) at Series-A; six or seven seats at Series-B/C with progressive founder dilution; seven to nine at pre-IPO with an independent majority and, if the CEO is chair, a lead independent director.
- Investor seats are conferred by the Voting Agreement, not the bylaws; the bylaws only supply the seat count.
- Independent-director sourcing splits between board-network platforms (Bolster, theBoardlist, Extraordinary Women on Boards, Him for Her, Ascend Pinnacle, Nurole) and executive-search firms (Spencer Stuart, Heidrick & Struggles, Russell Reynolds, Egon Zehnder), with selection criteria written down before candidate conversations begin.
- Every new director gets a standard onboarding package — charter, bylaws, 24 months of consents, D&O summary and indemnification agreement, cap table, three prior board packs, committee charters, code of ethics, insider-trading policy, meeting calendar, formal orientation — assembled by the corporate secretary described in [chapter 03](./03-corporate-secretary-function.md).
- Founder-side seat mechanics belong to [mod-102](../mod-102-founding-team-legal-architecture/); investor-side seat rights belong to the fundraising curriculum; this chapter owns the bylaws and the composition arc.
