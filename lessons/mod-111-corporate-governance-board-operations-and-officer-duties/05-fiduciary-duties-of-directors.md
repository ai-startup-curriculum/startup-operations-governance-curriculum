# 5. Fiduciary duties of directors and officers

> Care, loyalty, and oversight — the three doctrinal pillars every director and officer stands on, and the enhanced-scrutiny frameworks that override the business-judgment rule when the transaction is a sale of control, a defensive measure, or an interference with the franchise.

## Motivation

Fiduciary duties are the substantive standard a Delaware court will apply when a stockholder sues over a decision that went badly. A board that runs a real process — informed, disinterested, deliberate, documented — sits inside the business-judgment rule and is almost impossible to second-guess on the merits. A board that skips process, hides a conflict, or ignores a mission-critical risk sits outside the rule and faces entire-fairness or enhanced-scrutiny review, where the burden shifts and the outcome is unpredictable. This chapter walks the doctrinal map every startup director and officer must have loaded before taking the seat.

## The three duties

Delaware recognises two traditional duties — **care** and **loyalty** — and one modern subset of loyalty called the **duty of oversight** (or **Caremark duty**). Officers owe the same duties as directors, subject to the statutory differences noted below.

### Duty of care

The duty of care requires directors to decide on an informed, deliberate basis — read the materials, ask the questions, weigh the alternatives, take enough time. The seminal case is *Smith v. Van Gorkom*, 488 A.2d 858 (Del. 1985), where the Trans Union board approved a $55-per-share cash-out merger after a two-hour meeting, without reading the merger agreement and without a written fairness opinion. The Delaware Supreme Court held the directors grossly negligent and personally liable for the difference between the deal price and the intrinsic value.

*Van Gorkom* provoked **DGCL § 102(b)(7)**, which permits the certificate of incorporation to **exculpate directors from personal monetary liability for breach of the duty of care**. Section 102(b)(7) does **not** exculpate breaches of loyalty, acts not in good faith, intentional misconduct or knowing violations of law, unlawful distributions under DGCL § 174, or transactions from which the director derived an improper personal benefit. Delaware amended § 102(b)(7) in 2022 to permit **officer exculpation** as well, with narrower scope — it does not extend to claims brought by the corporation itself or to derivative claims on its behalf.

The care standard is **gross negligence**, not ordinary negligence. Under **DGCL § 141(e)** directors are entitled to rely in good faith on management, board committees, and outside experts, provided the reliance is reasonable and the source is competent.

### Duty of loyalty

The duty of loyalty forbids self-dealing. When a director stands on both sides of a transaction — or has a material financial interest in the counterparty — the transaction is an **interested-director transaction** governed by **DGCL § 144**, which provides three safe harbors:

1. disclosure to and good-faith approval by a majority of the **disinterested directors** (even if less than a quorum);
2. disclosure to and approval by a **majority of disinterested stockholders**; or
3. **entire fairness** — both **fair dealing** (process) and **fair price** (substance).

The conflicted director must **disclose** and **recuse from the vote**; the minutes must reflect both. A bare "abstained" without disclosure does not qualify for the § 144(a)(1) safe harbor.

The **corporate-opportunity doctrine** is loyalty's other branch: a fiduciary may not divert to themself, or to a competitor they control, an opportunity that (a) the corporation is financially able to undertake, (b) is in its line of business, (c) is of practical advantage to it, and (d) the corporation has an interest or reasonable expectancy in. The fiduciary must present it to the corporation and let the disinterested board decide, or lose it. Delaware permits contractual **corporate-opportunity waivers** in the charter or in a written agreement — which is why VC term sheets routinely include one covering the fund and its portfolio.

### Duty of oversight (Caremark)

*In re Caremark International Inc. Derivative Litigation*, 698 A.2d 959 (Del. Ch. 1996), holds that directors must **assure a corporate information and reporting system exists** sufficient to allow the board to reach informed judgments on compliance and performance. Liability attaches only where directors **utterly failed** to implement any reporting system, or, having implemented one, **consciously failed to monitor or oversee** its operations — demanding, but real.

*Stone v. Ritter*, 911 A.2d 362 (Del. 2006), confirmed *Caremark* as a subset of **loyalty**, with **bad faith** — conscious disregard of a known duty — as the necessary predicate. Because *Caremark* claims sound in loyalty, § 102(b)(7) exculpation does **not** shield directors from them.

*Marchand v. Barnhill*, 212 A.3d 805 (Del. 2019), sharpened the standard for **mission-critical risks**. The Blue Bell Creameries board faced a listeria outbreak that killed three consumers. The Delaware Supreme Court refused to dismiss the *Caremark* claim because the board had no committee, no protocol, no reporting cadence, and no meaningful information channel devoted to **food safety** — the single most mission-critical risk for an ice-cream manufacturer. Mere existence of "some" compliance system is not enough; the board must have a system specifically directed at the risks that would kill the company if they materialised.

## The business-judgment rule and its limits

The **business-judgment rule** is a **presumption** — not a substantive standard — that the directors acted on an informed basis, in good faith, and in the honest belief that the action was in the corporation's best interests. When it applies, courts will not second-guess the substantive decision, even if it turned out badly.

The plaintiff carries the burden of rebuttal. Rebuttal succeeds by showing **gross negligence** (care), **self-dealing or divided loyalty** (loyalty), or **bad faith** (conscious disregard of a known duty). If the plaintiff rebuts the presumption, the standard flips to **entire fairness** and the burden shifts to the defendants to prove both fair dealing and fair price. *Cede & Co. v. Technicolor, Inc.*, 634 A.2d 345 (Del. 1993), is the canonical statement of this burden-shifting mechanic.

## Enhanced-scrutiny frameworks

In three recurring situations Delaware applies an **intermediate** standard — more searching than the business-judgment rule, less demanding than entire fairness.

**Revlon — sale of control.** *Revlon, Inc. v. MacAndrews & Forbes Holdings, Inc.*, 506 A.2d 173 (Del. 1986), holds that once a sale of control becomes inevitable, the board's role changes from defender of the corporate bastion to **auctioneer** charged with obtaining the **highest immediate value** reasonably available for stockholders. Revlon triggers on a cash sale, a break-up, or a change-of-control transaction in which the target's public stockholders lose their ongoing equity participation in a controlled company. *Paramount Communications, Inc. v. QVC Network, Inc.*, 637 A.2d 34 (Del. 1994), clarified the trigger: a **stock-for-stock merger between two dispersed-holder companies** — where the combined company also has dispersed public ownership — does **not** activate Revlon, because control remains "in the market"; a transaction that moves a target into the hands of a single controller **does** activate Revlon, because stockholders lose the future opportunity to sell their control premium. Revlon does not dictate a specific process, but the record must show a real market check and a rationale for the chosen path.

**Unocal — takeover defence.** *Unocal Corp. v. Mesa Petroleum Co.*, 493 A.2d 946 (Del. 1985), governs board-adopted defensive measures. The board must show (a) reasonable grounds for believing a danger to corporate policy existed and (b) that the defensive measure was **reasonable in relation to the threat** — not preclusive, not coercive, proportionate. Poison pills and deal-protection measures get *Unocal* review.

**Blasius — interference with the franchise.** *Blasius Industries, Inc. v. Atlas Corp.*, 564 A.2d 651 (Del. Ch. 1988), governs board action taken for the **primary purpose of interfering with the stockholder franchise** — packing the board to defeat a consent solicitation, moving a record date to frustrate a proxy fight. Such action requires a **compelling justification**, higher than *Unocal* proportionality and rarely satisfied.

## Cleansing devices

**Corwin (non-controller deals).** *Corwin v. KKR Financial Holdings LLC*, 125 A.3d 304 (Del. 2015), holds that when a transaction not involving a controlling stockholder is approved by a **fully-informed, uncoerced vote of the disinterested stockholders**, review is reduced to the business-judgment rule. *Corwin* is the standard cleansing mechanic for arm's-length M&A; it does not apply to controlled-company transactions and does not cleanse *Caremark* or bad-faith claims.

**MFW (controller deals).** *Kahn v. M & F Worldwide Corp.*, 88 A.3d 635 (Del. 2014), governs cleansing of a **controlling-stockholder transaction** — otherwise entire-fairness by default. Business-judgment review applies only if, **from the outset (*ab initio*)**, the transaction is conditioned on **both** (i) approval by a **special committee** of independent, disinterested directors that is fully empowered and meets its duty of care, and (ii) an **uncoerced, informed vote of a majority of the minority** stockholders. Failing either prong forfeits BJR review and the transaction reverts to entire fairness.

## Derivative-suit demand futility

Under **Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 23.1** and Chancery Rule 23.1, a stockholder suing on behalf of the corporation must either make a pre-suit demand on the board or plead with particularity why demand would be futile. *Aronson v. Lewis*, 473 A.2d 805 (Del. 1984), articulated the historical **two-prong** futility test. <!-- needs-research: United Food & Commercial Workers Union & Participating Food Industry Employers Tri-State Pension Fund v. Zuckerberg, 262 A.3d 1034 (Del. 2021) — confirm exact reporter cite for the unified three-prong futility restatement. --> The Delaware Supreme Court has since restated futility as a **unified three-prong test** applied director-by-director, asking whether the director received a material personal benefit from the challenged conduct, faces a substantial likelihood of personal liability, or lacks independence from someone in either of the first two categories. Demand is excused if particularised facts satisfy any prong for at least half the board.

Even where demand is excused, a properly-constituted **special litigation committee** can seek dismissal under the two-step *Zapata Corp. v. Maldonado*, 430 A.2d 779 (Del. 1981), analysis: the court reviews the SLC's independence, good faith, and reasonable investigation, then applies its own independent business judgment.

## Officer fiduciary-duty parity

Officers owe the same duties of **care and loyalty** as directors. The 2022 amendment to DGCL § 102(b)(7) permits charter exculpation of officers for care claims, but with **narrower scope**: officer exculpation does not extend to claims brought by the corporation itself, nor to derivative claims on the corporation's behalf. Officers therefore retain meaningful residual care exposure even under a modern charter.

Each officer should be able to point to the reporting system in their lane and to the cadence at which it escalates to the board.

## The § 145 / § 174 backstops

**DGCL § 145** authorises the corporation to advance expenses and to indemnify directors and officers for actions in their fiduciary capacity, subject to statutory limits (no indemnification for adjudicated bad faith; no indemnification for a derivative action where liability to the corporation is adjudged, absent Chancery approval). **DGCL § 174** imposes personal joint-and-several liability on directors who wilfully or negligently approve an unlawful distribution or stock repurchase; § 102(b)(7) cannot exculpate a § 174 breach.

## Practical takeaways for the board

- **Show your work.** Board books, expert reports, and minutes should reflect the information the board received, the alternatives it considered, and the time it took. The record is the defence.
- **Recuse when conflicted.** Disclose, leave the room for the discussion and the vote, and reflect both in the minutes. § 144(a)(1) requires no less.
- **Retain independent advisors when the stakes warrant** — bankers, counsel, valuation experts, and (for controlled-company deals) an independent special committee with its own separate counsel. § 141(e) reliance requires that the advisor be qualified and the reliance reasonable.
- **Build the mission-critical-risk oversight system.** Identify the two or three risks that would kill the company. Assign board-level responsibility. Set a reporting cadence. Document it in minutes.
- **For a change-of-control transaction, run a Revlon-fit sale process** — real market check, meaningful fiduciary out, and a no-shop / go-shop calibrated to the pre-signing market outreach. If the counterparty is a controlling stockholder, install the MFW structure *ab initio* — before negotiations start, not after.

## Summary

- Directors owe three duties: **care** (informed, deliberate decisions), **loyalty** (no self-dealing; disclose and recuse), and **oversight** (*Caremark* — a good-faith monitoring system, sharpened by *Marchand* for mission-critical risks).
- The **business-judgment rule** is a presumption of good-faith, informed, disinterested decision-making; rebuttal by gross negligence, self-dealing, or bad faith flips the standard to **entire fairness** and shifts the burden to the defendants (*Cede & Co. v. Technicolor*).
- **DGCL § 102(b)(7)** exculpates directors from monetary liability for care breaches (2022 amendment extended narrower exculpation to officers). It does **not** exculpate loyalty, bad-faith, § 174, or *Caremark* claims.
- **DGCL § 144** cleanses interested-director transactions via (i) disinterested-director approval, (ii) disinterested-stockholder approval, or (iii) entire fairness.
- Enhanced scrutiny applies in three settings: **Revlon** (sale of control — auctioneer duty; *QVC* clarifies the change-of-control trigger), **Unocal** (defence — reasonable in relation to the threat), and **Blasius** (franchise interference — compelling justification).
- **Corwin** cleanses non-controller deals via a fully-informed, uncoerced disinterested stockholder vote; **MFW** cleanses controller deals only if conditioned *ab initio* on **both** an independent special committee **and** a majority-of-minority vote.
- **Demand futility** under Rule 23.1: *Aronson* two-prong test, restated as a unified three-prong director-by-director test; **Zapata** governs SLC dismissals.
- Officers owe **parity duties** and increasingly bear **Caremark-grade oversight duties within their functional lanes** — CFO for controls, GC for compliance, CISO for security, and so on.
- **Process is the defence.** Show the record: the information, the deliberation, the recusal, the advisor, the cadence, the vote.
