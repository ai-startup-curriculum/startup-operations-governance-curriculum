# Exercise 05 — Fiduciary-duty case teardown across Caremark, Van Gorkom, Revlon

> Estimated time: **~10 hours** · Related chapter: [05 — Fiduciary duties of directors and officers](../05-fiduciary-duties-of-directors.md)

## Problem statement

Maridian Compute, Inc. is a Delaware C-corp operating a GPU-fabric platform for AI-infrastructure customers. The corporation is late-stage pre-IPO, has a classified board with two founder directors, three preferred-designated directors, and four independent directors, and a charter that carries a standard DGCL § 102(b)(7) exculpation and a 2022-amended officer-exculpation extension. Two forcing-function events landed in the same quarter. **(i)** The board received an unsolicited written acquisition proposal from a strategic buyer at an enterprise value of <!-- needs-research: confirm $4.2B is within the band of recent comparable strategic-buyer approaches for pre-IPO AI-infrastructure targets --> $4.2B, all-cash, with a 45-day exclusivity ask and a "best-and-final" posture signalled in the cover letter. **(ii)** Two Caremark-adjacent incidents surfaced in the same quarter — a training-data provenance incident in which three named rightsholders have sent demand letters alleging copyright-infringement arising from a documented pipeline that scraped a corpus the engineering team had internally flagged as "unclear licence status" eleven months earlier, and a model-safety incident at a deployed customer in which an autonomous-decision feature executed a high-stakes action that produced a documented financial loss for the customer and a near-miss safety event for a downstream human user.

The incoming general counsel inherits the file eight weeks before the next full board meeting. She must prepare the corporation's fiduciary-duty posture simultaneously on two tracks: the deal-review workstream for the strategic approach, and the derivative-suit-defence workstream for the two incidents. The board has not yet constituted a transaction committee; the audit committee has not yet received a dedicated mission-critical-risk report on training-data provenance or model safety; the minutes of the last four quarters do not reflect a standing cadence for either risk. The D&O tower is in force but the Side-A DIC layer is thin; the indemnification agreements were signed at Series B and have not been refreshed.

The general counsel owns the case-teardown library and the four worked memos; the corporate secretary owns the minute-template authoring under her supervision; outside Delaware counsel will finalise the demand-futility pleading posture if a complaint lands.

The record is thinner than it should be. The audit committee's minutes for the trailing four quarters record the quarterly audit-committee update from the CFO on financial-reporting controls but do not record a standing update from the head of trust-and-safety on model safety or from the general counsel on training-data licensing. The AI-risk sub-committee was constituted in principle at the last board meeting but has not met, has no charter, and has no named members. The books-and-records demand window under DGCL § 220 is open — a plaintiff with Maridian stock and counsel can reach for board materials, minutes, and audit-committee files on a defensible § 220 theory tied to the two incidents. The general counsel's work in the next eight weeks is simultaneously defensive (frame the fiduciary posture if a complaint lands) and prospective (install the cadence that produces the record going forward).

The incoming general counsel has one opportunity to install the package before the strategic buyer's exclusivity clock pressures the board into a sale-process-without-process, and before a derivative plaintiff pressures the audit committee into a defensive posture that rests on an incomplete record. The eight-week window is the whole window.

Author the full fiduciary-duty package the general counsel will present to the board at the next full meeting.

## Requirements

### Part A — Case-teardown library

Author `case-teardown-library.md` — the operating reference the general counsel, the corporate secretary, and the committee chairs will read from when a fiduciary-duty question lands on an agenda. One entry per case. Each entry carries: **facts** (the operative pattern), **holding**, **standard of review** (business-judgment, enhanced-scrutiny, or entire-fairness), **operative doctrine** (the one or two sentences a practitioner cites), and **practitioner takeaway** (what the board does differently because of this case). Cover at minimum:

1. *Smith v. Van Gorkom*, 488 A.2d 858 (Del. 1985) — duty of care, gross-negligence standard, and the DGCL § 102(b)(7) exculpation the case provoked. Name the two-hour-meeting pattern and the absence of a written fairness opinion as the operative facts.
2. *In re Caremark International Inc. Derivative Litigation*, 698 A.2d 959 (Del. Ch. 1996) — the Chancellor Allen opinion locating oversight as a subset of loyalty, the two-prong test (utterly failed to implement any reporting system; having implemented one, consciously failed to monitor), and the "demanding, but real" liability standard.
3. *Stone v. Ritter*, 911 A.2d 362 (Del. 2006) — the confirmation that *Caremark* claims sound in loyalty with bad faith as the predicate, and the consequence that § 102(b)(7) does not shield.
4. *Marchand v. Barnhill*, 212 A.3d 805 (Del. 2019) — the mission-critical-risk sharpening, the Blue Bell food-safety pattern, and the failure to have a board-level committee, protocol, reporting cadence, or information channel devoted to the mission-critical risk.
5. *Revlon, Inc. v. MacAndrews & Forbes Holdings, Inc.*, 506 A.2d 173 (Del. 1986) — the auctioneer duty on a sale of control and the "highest immediate value" standard. *Paramount Communications, Inc. v. QVC Network, Inc.*, 637 A.2d 34 (Del. 1994), as the trigger clarification (control-in-the-market vs. control-to-a-single-controller).
6. *Unocal Corp. v. Mesa Petroleum Co.*, 493 A.2d 946 (Del. 1985) — the two-part defensive-measure test: reasonable grounds for the threat; reasonable in relation to the threat (not preclusive, not coercive, proportionate).
7. *Blasius Industries, Inc. v. Atlas Corp.*, 564 A.2d 651 (Del. Ch. 1988) — franchise interference and the compelling-justification standard rarely satisfied.
8. *Corwin v. KKR Financial Holdings LLC*, 125 A.3d 304 (Del. 2015) — the cleansing mechanic for non-controller deals (fully-informed, uncoerced disinterested-stockholder vote collapsing review to the business-judgment rule), and the boundary (does not apply to controlled-company transactions, does not cleanse *Caremark* or bad-faith claims).
9. *Kahn v. M & F Worldwide Corp.*, 88 A.3d 635 (Del. 2014) — the controller-deal cleansing mechanic with both prongs required *ab initio* (independent special committee fully empowered, meeting its duty of care; majority-of-minority vote, uncoerced and informed).
10. *United Food & Commercial Workers Union v. Zuckerberg*, 262 A.3d 1034 (Del. 2021) — the unified three-prong demand-futility test applied director-by-director. <!-- needs-research: confirm the exact reporter cite for the Zuckerberg unified three-prong restatement and the superseded status of the Aronson two-prong test. --> Reference *Aronson v. Lewis*, 473 A.2d 805 (Del. 1984), as the historical predecessor without re-stating its two-prong formulation as current law.
11. *Zapata Corp. v. Maldonado*, 430 A.2d 779 (Del. 1981), as the special-litigation-committee framework (SLC independence, good faith, reasonable investigation; court's independent business judgment).
12. *Cede & Co. v. Technicolor, Inc.*, 634 A.2d 345 (Del. 1993), as the canonical burden-shifting mechanic when the business-judgment rule's presumption is rebutted.

Reference DGCL §§ 102(b)(7), 141(e), 144, 145, 174 by section number. Where the chapter did not supply a specific holding or sub-point, flag with `<!-- needs-research: ... -->` rather than inventing.

Each entry closes with a short **Maridian application** note — a one-sentence binding of the doctrine to the current fact pattern (e.g., the *Van Gorkom* entry binds to the transaction-committee's reading-time and fairness-opinion discipline in Part C; the *Caremark* and *Marchand* entries bind to the mission-critical-risk cadence in Part B; the *Zuckerberg* entry binds to the director-by-director analysis in Part E). The application note is not a holding — it is a cross-reference marker that lets the committee chairs read the library as an operating index, not as a casebook.

### Part B — Caremark oversight memo

Author `caremark-oversight-memo.md` — the memo the general counsel will circulate to the audit committee applying *Caremark* and *Marchand* to the two incidents. Cover:

1. **The two-prong framing.** Name the *Caremark* prongs explicitly — (i) utter failure to implement any reporting system; (ii) having implemented one, conscious failure to monitor or oversee its operations. Apply each prong separately to the training-data provenance incident and to the model-safety incident. The eleven-month-old internal "unclear licence status" flag is the operative fact for conscious-failure-to-monitor on the provenance side; the absence of a standing board-level model-safety cadence is the operative fact on the model-safety side.
2. **The mission-critical-risk test under *Marchand*.** Identify training-data provenance and model safety as Maridian's mission-critical risks under the AI-infrastructure business model. Name why each qualifies: provenance because a copyright-infringement finding runs through the training corpus to the model weights to every downstream deployment; model safety because an autonomous-decision incident at any customer is the single risk pattern that would kill the company if a fatality or regulatory enforcement attached.
3. **The board-reporting cadence that answers the mission-critical-risk test.** Author the standing cadence the audit committee will adopt: a quarterly mission-critical-risk report from the head of trust-and-safety, with named numerator / denominator metrics (provenance-audited corpora as a share of training-corpus volume; model-safety incident count and severity classification; near-miss rate); a monthly escalation-review cadence between the general counsel, the CISO, and the head of trust-and-safety; a named information channel that routes a Severity-1 safety incident to the audit committee chair within a defined window.
4. **The minute discipline that documents the cadence.** The minute-template reference points (agenda item, the materials circulated, the officer who presented, the questions the board asked, the follow-ups logged), cross-referencing the Part F minute template. The *Marchand* lesson is that "some" compliance system is not enough; the record must show a system specifically directed at the mission-critical risk.
4a. **The pre-incident record cure.** Name the look-back cure the general counsel will attempt for the trailing four quarters — a retro-fit of the audit-committee minutes to capture mission-critical-risk discussions that occurred but were not minute-recorded, with the limit named (a retro-fit cannot invent cadence that did not occur; the cure is to the record, not to the facts). The retro-fit is executed via a corrective-minute protocol the corporate secretary runs, not via re-dating the original minutes.
5. **The remediation posture for the two incidents.** Name the immediate remediation steps (provenance audit of the identified corpus, kill-switch and human-in-the-loop controls on the autonomous-decision feature, customer-notice posture, preservation-of-documents hold), and route each step to the officer who owns it. The memo does not pre-decide the remediation substance; it frames the fiduciary question and names the owners.
6. **The officer-parity analysis.** Under the officer-Caremark line flagged in Part A, name the functional-lane oversight each officer carries: the head of trust-and-safety for the model-safety system; the CISO for the infrastructure-level data-handling controls over the training corpus; the general counsel for the legal-compliance system covering training-data licensing and the model-safety disclosure posture to customers. Each officer must be able to point to the system in their lane and to the cadence at which it escalates to the board.
7. **The DGCL § 141(e) reliance posture.** Name the external-expert reliance the audit committee will record — the provenance auditor, the model-safety auditor, outside counsel's privileged litigation-risk assessment, and the forensic engineering review. Section 141(e) reliance requires the advisor to be qualified and the reliance to be reasonable; the memo names both the advisor and the scope of reliance, so the record supports the presumption rather than merely asserting it.
8. **The AI-risk sub-committee stand-up.** The in-principle AI-risk sub-committee has no charter and no members. Name the stand-up deliverables — a charter under DGCL § 141(c) delegating oversight of model-safety and training-data-provenance risks, named membership (at least two independent directors), a quarterly meeting cadence, and a standing reporting line to the audit committee. The sub-committee is the structural answer to *Marchand*'s mission-critical-committee requirement.
9. **The bad-faith predicate the memo refuses to supply.** *Stone v. Ritter* identifies bad faith — conscious disregard of a known duty — as the predicate for *Caremark* liability. Name the fact patterns that would supply the predicate (deliberate suppression of an escalation; knowing refusal to act on a Severity-1 report; concealment of a mission-critical-risk incident from the audit committee) and name the discipline that refuses to generate those fact patterns (prompt escalation, truthful minute entries, no suppression of adverse information in the materials).

### Part C — Revlon / Unocal sale-process memo

Author `revlon-unocal-sale-process-memo.md` — the sale-process memo the general counsel will present to the special transaction committee the board will constitute at the next meeting. Cover:

1. **The Revlon-trigger analysis.** Apply *Revlon* and *QVC* to the $4.2B all-cash approach. The all-cash posture places stockholders out of the ongoing enterprise and extinguishes the future opportunity to sell a control premium — Revlon applies. Name the trigger explicitly; do not duck.
2. **The sale-process discipline.** Author the auctioneer-duty workstream: a real market check (named outreach to a defensible set of potential strategic and financial counterparties), a fairness opinion from a qualified investment bank under DGCL § 141(e) reliance, a meaningful fiduciary out in any definitive agreement, and a no-shop / go-shop calibrated to the pre-signing market outreach. *Revlon* does not dictate a specific process; the record must defend the rationale for the chosen path.
3. **The Unocal reasonableness / proportionality analysis if defensive measures are taken.** If the board installs a poison pill, a deal-protection measure, or any other defensive posture, apply *Unocal*: (i) reasonable grounds for believing a danger to corporate policy existed (the 45-day exclusivity ask, the "best-and-final" posture, the information-asymmetry on valuation); (ii) the measure is reasonable in relation to the threat — not preclusive, not coercive, proportionate. Name the defensive measures the committee is most likely to consider and the *Unocal* record each would require.
4. **The Blasius posture if franchise interference is in scope.** If the strategic buyer launches a proxy fight or a consent solicitation, any board response taken for the primary purpose of interfering with the stockholder franchise triggers the compelling-justification standard under *Blasius*. Flag the posture; do not pre-decide.
5. **The information-rights discipline for the transaction committee.** The § 141(e) record the committee will build — the banker book, the financial advisor's fairness analysis, counsel's structuring memo, the committee's own meeting cadence and minute discipline. The memo names the record; the record is the defence.
6. **The Corwin-cleansing optionality.** If the transaction ultimately clears to a stockholder vote, the Corwin-cleansing path is available only if the vote is fully informed and uncoerced and the transaction is not a controller-side deal (cross-reference Part D for the controller-side gating).
7. **The transaction-committee composition and charter.** Name the committee size (three or four independent directors with no economic or relational interest in the strategic buyer), the committee's full empowerment under DGCL § 141(c) to negotiate and to recommend, the committee's authority to retain its own financial advisor and its own legal counsel separate from management's advisors, and the committee's own meeting cadence. The committee charter is the record the court reads first when the process is challenged.
8. **The deal-protection-measure calibration.** Name the deal-protection measures the board is most likely to negotiate — a no-shop covenant with a fiduciary out, a termination fee <!-- needs-research: confirm the current practitioner band for termination fees as a percentage of equity value for pre-IPO strategic-buyer transactions at this scale -->, matching rights for the strategic buyer, and a go-shop window if the committee has not run a full pre-signing market check. Each measure is scored against the *Unocal* proportionality test — reasonable in relation to the threat, not preclusive, not coercive.
9. **The 45-day-exclusivity response posture.** Name the committee's response to the strategic buyer's 45-day exclusivity ask — a counter with a shorter exclusivity window, a committee-level market check as a precondition to exclusivity, a carve-out for unsolicited bona-fide proposals, and a fiduciary out that survives the exclusivity period. Granting exclusivity without a defensible process-record is the *Van Gorkom* failure mode translated to the modern sale-process.
10. **The information-asymmetry cure.** Name the diligence counter-flow the committee will run on the strategic buyer — the buyer's financing, the buyer's regulatory-approval risk, the buyer's capacity to close, and the buyer's track-record on prior deals. A fully-informed committee is a two-sided information flow; the record must show both sides.

### Part D — Corwin / MFW cleansing memo

Author `corwin-mfw-cleansing-memo.md` — the short memo the general counsel will circulate to the board explaining which cleansing device is available under which fact pattern. Cover:

1. **The Corwin availability analysis.** Name when *Corwin* cleansing is available (non-controller deal; fully-informed, uncoerced disinterested-stockholder vote) and when it is not (controlled-company transaction; coerced or structurally-coercive vote; disclosure deficiency; *Caremark* or bad-faith claim). The strategic-buyer approach is a non-controller deal and is Corwin-eligible if the disclosure record supports "fully informed."
2. **The MFW *ab initio* dual-condition pre-wiring.** Name the two conditions that must be installed **before negotiations start**, not after — (i) a special committee of independent, disinterested directors, fully empowered and discharging its duty of care; (ii) an uncoerced, informed majority-of-minority stockholder vote. Failing either prong forfeits business-judgment review and reverts the transaction to entire fairness.
3. **The controller-transaction trigger pattern.** For Maridian, any future transaction in which a preferred-designated director's fund stands on both sides — a bolt-on acquisition of a portfolio company, a secondary-sale pattern favouring one investor group, a recapitalisation with structurally-different treatment of the preferred stack — may trigger controller-deal treatment and must be pre-wired MFW-style *ab initio*. Name the diagnostic questions the general counsel asks before deciding whether MFW is required.
4. **The disclosure discipline that keeps Corwin available.** The proxy-statement content, the fairness-opinion summary, the treatment of conflicts and side-arrangements, and the recommendation statement — each is a disclosure-record component Corwin reads from. A material disclosure gap defeats "fully informed." Name the review cadence the general counsel will run before the proxy clears.
5. **The boundary to *Caremark* and bad-faith claims.** Neither Corwin nor MFW cleanses a *Caremark* oversight claim or a bad-faith claim. The Part B oversight workstream and the Part C sale-process workstream run on parallel tracks and the cleansing mechanics operate on the deal-approval track only.
6. **The independence test for the special committee.** Name the independence diagnostic the general counsel runs for each candidate committee member — no economic interest in the counterparty, no fund-level relationship, no social or professional dependency on the controller, and (for an MFW special committee) satisfaction of the fully-empowered-to-say-no standard, not merely a fully-empowered-to-negotiate standard. A committee that cannot credibly say "no" forfeits the MFW prong.
7. **The majority-of-minority vote mechanics.** Name the vote-threshold computation (majority of all outstanding minority shares, not merely a majority of minority shares voting), the uncoercion discipline (no structural coercion, no implicit threat of retaliation, no two-tier timing pressure), and the disclosure discipline that makes "informed" a defensible representation. Each component is a gate on the MFW cleansing path.

### Part E — Demand-futility analysis

Author `demand-futility-analysis.md` — the Rule 23.1 analysis under the *Zuckerberg* three-part test applied to a hypothetical derivative-suit complaint arising from the two incidents. Cover:

1. **The Rule 23.1 standing posture.** Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 23.1 and Chancery Rule 23.1 require either a pre-suit demand on the board or a plea with particularity of why demand would be futile. Name the pleading standard (particularised facts, not conclusory allegations) and the demand-refused-vs-demand-excused distinction.
2. **The *Zuckerberg* unified three-prong test.** Applied director-by-director, the test asks for each director whether (i) the director received a material personal benefit from the alleged misconduct; (ii) the director faces a substantial likelihood of personal liability for the misconduct; (iii) the director lacks independence from someone in either of the first two categories. Demand is excused if particularised facts satisfy any prong for at least half of the board. <!-- needs-research: confirm the exact reporter cite and the precise articulation of the Zuckerberg third prong before locking the memo. -->
3. **The director-by-director worked analysis for Maridian's current nine-director board.** For each of the two founder directors, the three preferred-designated directors, and the four independent directors, run each of the three prongs. Name the fact-pattern inputs the memo assumes — audit-committee membership, the trust-and-safety reporting line, the preferred-designated directors' fund relationships, the recency of each independent's appointment. For each director, record the prong-by-prong disposition (futile / not futile / contested) and the particularised facts the plaintiff would need to plead.
4. **The *Aronson* historical reference.** Reference *Aronson v. Lewis*, 473 A.2d 805 (Del. 1984), as the historical two-prong predecessor. Do not re-state its two-prong formulation as current law; *Zuckerberg* restates the futility analysis as a unified three-prong director-by-director test and is the operative standard.
5. **The SLC optionality under *Zapata*.** If demand is excused and the complaint is filed, a properly-constituted special litigation committee can seek dismissal under *Zapata Corp. v. Maldonado*, 430 A.2d 779 (Del. 1981). Name the two-step *Zapata* review (independence, good faith, reasonable investigation; court's own independent business judgment) and the committee-composition discipline the board would install.
6. **The document-preservation and privilege posture.** The litigation-hold trigger, the privilege segregation between general-counsel legal advice and officer business advice, the work-product posture of the SLC's investigation, and the § 220 books-and-records-demand pattern that typically precedes a particularised 23.1 plea.
7. **The DGCL § 102(b)(7) exculpation interaction.** Name the posture that § 102(b)(7) exculpation does **not** shield directors from loyalty, bad-faith, or *Caremark* claims — the training-data provenance incident and the model-safety incident both sound in oversight-as-loyalty under *Stone v. Ritter* and are therefore unshielded by the charter's exculpation. The 2022-amended officer-exculpation extension does not extend to claims brought by the corporation itself or to derivative claims on its behalf, so Maridian's officers also carry unshielded derivative-suit exposure for the two incidents.
8. **The DGCL § 145 advancement and indemnification posture.** Name the advancement trigger (written undertaking from the director or officer to repay if indemnification is ultimately disallowed), the indemnification carve-outs (no indemnification for adjudicated bad faith; no indemnification for a derivative action where liability to the corporation is adjudged, absent Chancery approval), and the indemnification-agreement refresh posture (the Series-B-vintage agreements have not been refreshed; refresh to the current NVCA-form is the named deliverable).
9. **The DGCL § 220 books-and-records-demand anticipation.** Name the plaintiff-side pattern — a § 220 demand on a defensible credible-basis theory, a document production tied to the two incidents, and the particularised-fact pleading that follows. The general counsel's response posture is to produce the formal board-level records (minutes, materials, committee charters) without producing privileged communications, and to resist overbroad requests for officer-level emails and Slack. The § 220 response is itself a fiduciary-record exhibit.
10. **The independence posture for the preferred-designated directors.** Name the fact patterns under which a preferred-designated director is independent from the alleged misconduct (no fund-level investment in the corpus-licensing counterparty; no fund-level investment in the model-safety incident customer; no fund-level investment in a competitor that would benefit from the alleged misconduct) and the fact patterns under which independence fails (fund-level portfolio-company overlap with the counterparty; a director's own fiduciary obligations to another portfolio company in the same lane). The independence analysis is director-by-director, not fund-level.
11. **The particularised-fact drafting discipline.** Name the pleading-standard rigor the plaintiff must meet (specific transactions, specific board meetings, specific agenda items, specific omissions from the minute book), the conclusory allegations that will not satisfy the standard, and the § 220 document production the plaintiff typically uses to anchor the particularised pleading. The general counsel's defensive posture is to make the minute book and the committee charters answer each conclusory allegation with a specific record entry.

### Part F — Caremark-protective board minute template

Author `board-minute-template-caremark-protective.md` — the minute template the corporate secretary uses when a board agenda item relates to a mission-critical risk, structured to protect the business-judgment presumption. Cover:

1. **The agenda-item header.** The named item, the owner, the risk classification (mission-critical: training-data provenance, model safety, security incident, regulatory enforcement), the materials circulated in advance, and the pre-read lead time (72 hours as the module default).
2. **The record of information received.** The officer who presented, the deck or memo circulated, the external experts consulted and the § 141(e) reliance representation, the questions directors asked by name, the alternatives the board considered, and the time the board spent on the item. The *Van Gorkom* lesson is that the record must show the board was informed and took enough time.
3. **The conflict-disclosure and recusal record.** Any director with a material interest discloses the interest, leaves the room for the discussion and the vote, and the minutes reflect both. A bare "abstained" without disclosure does not qualify for the DGCL § 144(a)(1) safe harbor — the minute template must force disclosure-and-recusal language, not "abstained."
4. **The resolution and vote.** The resolution text, the vote tally (for / against / abstain / recused), and any individual director statement for the record. Resolutions involving a mission-critical risk carry the named follow-ups, the owner, and the next-reporting cadence.
5. **The follow-up and next-cadence log.** The named follow-ups from the current meeting, the owner for each, the target completion date, and the standing next-report cadence for the risk. The *Marchand* lesson is that the cadence must be documented; the minute template is where the cadence lives.
6. **The record of executive session.** If an executive session was held (with or without management, with or without counsel), the minute entry records the fact of the session and the general subject, not the substance. The template preserves the fact of the session for the record without waiving privilege over the substance.
7. **The signature and distribution log.** The corporate secretary's signature, the approval cadence (next-meeting consent agenda), and the distribution list. The template points to the corporate-secretary-function discipline in [chapter 03](../03-corporate-secretary-function.md) without re-authoring it.
8. **The § 141(e) reliance representation block.** For any mission-critical-risk agenda item that involves external-expert reliance, the template forces a named representation — the advisor identity, the scope of the advisor's engagement, the materials the advisor reviewed, and the director's representation that reliance on the advisor was reasonable under DGCL § 141(e). A bare "the committee reviewed the materials" entry does not build the § 141(e) record.
9. **The *Caremark* protective-language block.** For a mission-critical-risk item, the template includes a standing paragraph recording the system that exists for the risk, the cadence at which it reports, the information channel that routes a Severity-1 event to the committee chair, and the officer accountable. The block exists to defeat the *Marchand* "no system devoted to the mission-critical risk" finding; each occurrence of the block in the minute book is a record against the plaintiff's particularised plea.
10. **The anti-patterns the template refuses.** The template refuses "discussed," "reviewed," "considered," and "abstained" as entries. "Discussed" is replaced with the agenda item, the materials, and the named questions; "reviewed" is replaced with the materials and the § 141(e) reliance; "considered" is replaced with the alternatives weighed and the rationale; "abstained" is replaced with disclosure-and-recusal language. The anti-patterns cross-reference the corporate-secretary discipline in [chapter 03](../03-corporate-secretary-function.md).
