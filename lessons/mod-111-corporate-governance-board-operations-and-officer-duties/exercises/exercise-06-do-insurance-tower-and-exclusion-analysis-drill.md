# Exercise 06 — D&O insurance tower and exclusion-analysis drill

> Estimated time: **~8 hours** · Related chapter: [06 — D&O insurance program](../06-do-insurance-program.md)

## Problem statement

Maridian Compute, Inc. is a pre-IPO Delaware C-corp operating an AI-infrastructure / GPU-fabric platform selling capacity contracts into large-model training customers. The corporation is approximately twelve months from its target listing, is on a confidential S-1 drafting cadence with the underwriting syndicate, and has a seven-director board (four independent directors seated against the NYSE §303A / Nasdaq 5605 independence math, two investor-designated directors remaining under the preferred-stock director rights set to collapse at listing, and the founder-CEO). The corporation is renewing its D&O programme on a schedule that lands the bind date three months before the S-1 effective date, with the broker-marketing meeting scheduled in six weeks and the underwriter meeting with the primary-carrier panel three weeks after that.

The existing programme is the artefact of a Series-C placement that has not aged well against the IPO trajectory. The current structure is a single-carrier primary ABC written at Series-C with a modest excess layer and no Side-A DIC. The placement broker has signalled, in a pre-renewal strategy note to the general counsel and CFO, that (i) the incumbent primary carrier will re-underwrite the account on the IPO trajectory and reset retentions, exclusions, and pricing against the public-company underwriting grid; (ii) a Side-A DIC layer written by a specialist Side-A carrier is now the baseline expectation of the excess panel and will be a precondition for several of the excess carriers to quote at all; (iii) the total tower limit will need to step materially above the current Series-C limit to clear the underwriter's comfort threshold for a GPU-infrastructure IPO candidate exposed to Securities Act §11 / §12(a)(2) / §15 strict-liability and Exchange Act §10(b) / Rule 10b-5 fraud-on-the-market claims; and (iv) the 6-year IPO run-off tail commitment must be negotiated into the renewal now so the pricing multiple is anchored before the S-1 goes effective.

Three operating realities have forced the tower-rebuild question onto the general counsel's agenda. **(i)** The investor-designated directors have asked, in the last governance subcommittee executive session, whether the Side-A protection on the current programme is adequate to withstand an insolvency scenario or a derivative-suit settlement that DGCL §145(b) prohibits the corporation from indemnifying — the honest answer is no, and the independent directors have signalled that an inadequate Side-A posture will factor into their continued-service calculus at renewal. **(ii)** The underwriting syndicate's due-diligence memo flagged the thin D&O tower and the single-carrier concentration as a disclosure item the S-1 risk factors will need to address if it is not remediated before bind. **(iii)** The chapter-03 NVCA-form indemnification agreements signed by every director and officer are only as strong as the balance sheet and the D&O tower sitting behind them; the priority-of-payments clause in those agreements names the D&O policy as the first source of recovery, and the current tower cannot plausibly fund a securities class-action defence through final adjudication.

The failure-mode taxonomy the tower is sized against is specific. Post-IPO stock-drop class actions under Securities Act §11 and §12(a)(2) attach on strict-liability grounds against every signatory of the registration statement; Section 15 reaches control persons. Fraud-on-the-market §10(b) / Rule 10b-5 class actions attach on a scienter theory and typically survive a motion-to-dismiss even on thin pleadings when the corporation has missed forward guidance. Derivative suits tracking Caremark and Marchand oversight theories attach to the AI-governance substance the board has built (or failed to build) in the pre-IPO window. SEC enforcement actions and Wells notices arrive on their own cadence. Each scenario depletes the tower on a different trajectory, and the Side-A DIC layer exists to catch the director whose corporate indemnity has been exhausted or barred.

The general counsel has eight weeks to walk into the board governance subcommittee with a D&O tower specification, an exclusion carve-back matrix, an IPO-tail commitment memo, a broker-and-carrier selection pack, a benchmark-quote-defence memo, and a one-page memo tying the indemnification-agreement priority-of-payments stack to the tower. She owns the end-to-end package; the CFO co-owns the pricing and capacity analysis; the corporate secretary owns the application-warranty discipline and the prior-knowledge inventory; the board governance subcommittee owns the final approval. Author the full package.

## Requirements

### Part A — D&O tower specification

Author `d-and-o-tower-specification.md` — the stacked tower the general counsel and CFO will circulate to the governance subcommittee. Cover:

1. **The tower structure.** Author the full stack as a table: **primary ABC layer** (with Side A, Side B, and Side C sub-limits named per side, and with Side C scoped to securities claims only — §10(b) / §11 / §12(a)(2) / §15 — consistent with the public-company Side-C discipline); **excess layers 1 through N** written following-form above the primary, each a separate contract with a separate carrier to reduce correlated-insolvency and correlated-denial risk; **Side-A DIC layer** written at the top of the tower on broader terms than the ABC stack, with narrower exclusions, no application warranties beyond the bare fact of the ABC placement, and no retention. For each layer name the role (primary, excess, Side-A DIC), the limit, the attachment point, the retention posture (Side A at zero retention; Side B / Side C retention scaled to the public-company underwriting grid), the coverage sides the layer responds to, and the severability posture. Every dollar figure — limit, attachment, retention, premium indication — carries `<!-- needs-research: ... -->`.
2. **The order-of-payments endorsement.** The tower-wide endorsement that sequences Side A payments to individual directors ahead of Side B corporate reimbursements and Side C entity payments when limits are being depleted. Name the endorsement as a non-negotiable across every layer; name the carrier-form objections the broker should expect and the counter-language the placement broker will insist on.
3. **The following-form discipline across excess layers.** The requirement that each excess layer follow form to the primary unless it expressly broadens coverage; the specific carve-outs (regulatory, pollution, personal-conduct) where an excess carrier is permitted to narrow terms only if the Side-A DIC layer drops down to fill the gap.
4. **The Side-A DIC drop-down triggers.** The three operational triggers the Side-A DIC will respond to:
   - **(i) Wrongful denial by an underlying ABC carrier** — the DIC drops down to fund defence costs and indemnify the director while the coverage dispute with the ABC carrier runs in parallel.
   - **(ii) Exhaustion of underlying ABC limits** — the DIC extends limit above the ABC stack, preserving individual-director protection after entity-side claims have depleted the ABC tower.
   - **(iii) Corporation financially or legally unable to indemnify** — insolvency (bankruptcy, contractual bar in a debt covenant, statutory bar), DGCL §145(b) bar on derivative-suit settlements and judgments, and hostile post-transaction refusal by an acquirer's new board.
   Reference DGCL §145(a) (third-party claims), §145(b) (derivative claims — defence costs only), §145(c) (mandatory indemnification where the director is successful on the merits), and §145(e) (mandatory advancement) for the statutory indemnification and advancement framework the Side-A DIC sits behind.
5. **The carrier diversification discipline.** The requirement that no single carrier writes more than a named share of total tower limit, and that the primary and the Side-A DIC are written by different carriers to avoid a correlated denial. The specific share-cap figure carries `<!-- needs-research: ... -->`. Name the allocation discipline for mixed-claim scenarios (covered and uncovered conduct in the same complaint) — the allocation clause should default to a "relative exposure" or "larger settlement rule" allocation, not a line-item apportionment, so defence-cost funding is not held up by allocation disputes between the carrier and the corporation.
6. **The application-warranty discipline the tower depends on.** The tower's coverage integrity depends on the application the CEO, CFO, and general counsel sign — misstatement voids coverage, and under some carrier forms imputes the signer's knowledge to the entire pool of insureds. Name the severability discipline that cabins imputation to innocent directors; name the "bind first, warranty-letter second" operational rule; name the corporate-secretary's pre-bind knowledge inventory (threatened suits, departing-employee complaints, regulator correspondence, Wells-notice risk) that must be reconciled to the application draft before the CEO signs.

### Part B — Exclusion carve-back matrix

Author `exclusion-carveback-matrix.md`. The value of the tower is determined less by the limit than by the exclusions and the negotiated carve-backs. Cover:

1. **The exclusion matrix in operational-table form.** A table with one row per standard carrier-form exclusion and columns for (exclusion name, carrier-form default language, the carve-back Maridian will insist on, the fallback position the broker may accept, the layer the carve-back must appear on). At minimum the matrix covers: **insured-vs-insured**, **conduct / fraud / dishonesty**, **pending and prior litigation ("PPL")**, **personal-profit / personal-benefit**, **bump-up**, **pollution**, **bodily-injury / property-damage**, **ERISA**, **antitrust**, **regulatory / SEC**, and **professional-services**.
2. **The insured-vs-insured carve-outs.** The named carve-outs that preserve coverage for:
   - Derivative suits brought by stockholders on behalf of the corporation (the single most important carve-out — a derivative suit is technically brought "in the name of the corporation" against the directors, both of which are insureds, and without this carve-out the policy's core purpose is defeated).
   - Whistleblower claims under SOX §806 and Dodd-Frank §922.
   - Suits by a post-bankruptcy trustee, receiver, examiner, debtor-in-possession, or creditors' committee.
   - Suits by a former officer or director after a specified separation period (the separation-period figure carries `<!-- needs-research: ... -->`).
   - Cross-claims and third-party claims in a suit brought by a non-insured party.
3. **The conduct exclusion with final-adjudication language.** The requirement that fraud, dishonesty, and personal-profit exclusions attach only after a **final, non-appealable adjudication** of the excluded conduct in the underlying proceeding — not in a coverage action. Until that adjudication exists, the carrier funds defence costs. Name the severability discipline that preserves coverage for innocent insureds when the adjudicated insured is excluded.
4. **The prior-acts and PPL discipline.** The scope of the PPL exclusion — limited to actual pending litigation and specifically disclosed matters, not to any circumstance that might give rise to a claim. The prior-acts coverage date that the renewal must preserve; the discipline of binding first and updating knowledge second so post-application knowledge is preserved for the next renewal.
5. **The bump-up exclusion carve-back.** The carve-back language for the bump-up exclusion (which otherwise strips coverage for claims alleging the corporation paid inadequate consideration in an M&A deal or a going-private transaction) — preserving defence-cost coverage, preserving coverage for individual directors under Side A, and limiting the exclusion to the bump-up component of a judgement rather than to the full damages award. The threshold-amount language carries `<!-- needs-research: ... -->`.
6. **The regulatory / SEC carve-back.** The narrowing of the regulatory exclusion to purely civil regulatory penalties that are uninsurable as a matter of public policy; the preservation of defence-cost coverage and investigation coverage for SEC enforcement, DOJ investigations, Wells-notice responses, and Section 11 / 12 / 15 and Section 10(b) / Rule 10b-5 actions. The Side-A DIC carves back the regulatory exclusion more aggressively than the ABC tower.
7. **The BI/PD derivative carve-back.** The carve-back that preserves coverage for derivative claims alleging directors breached their Caremark oversight duty by allowing conduct that caused bodily injury or property damage — the underlying tort is pushed to the CGL / product-liability towers owned by mod-112, but the derivative defence costs sit on the D&O tower.
8. **The ERISA, antitrust, pollution, and professional-services exclusions.** Each pushed to its purpose-built tower (fiduciary-liability for ERISA, pollution tower for environmental, E&O / tech-E&O for professional-services, antitrust-specific policies where available) with the D&O tower preserving derivative-oversight coverage only. Name the specific carve-back language the broker should insist on so the D&O tower does not become the forum for a coverage fight that belongs on the sibling tower.
9. **The cost-of-defence funding mechanic.** The requirement that the primary carrier fund defence costs on an advancement basis — not reimbursement — through to final adjudication. Name the "duty to defend" versus "duty to pay" distinction; most public-company D&O is written as duty-to-pay with carrier advancement subject to consent-to-settle. Name the consent-to-settle discipline so the corporation does not forfeit coverage by settling without carrier involvement.

### Part C — IPO run-off tail memo

Author `ipo-runoff-tail-memo.md`. The 6-year run-off tail is the single most consequential D&O purchase at IPO close. Cover:

1. **The 6-year tail duration and its statutory anchor.** The tail is sized against the longest-running statute-of-repose exposure from the IPO: Securities Act §11 at 3 years from the effective date of the registration statement, Exchange Act §10(b) / Rule 10b-5 at 5 years from the underlying violation under Sarbanes-Oxley §804, and the Securities Act §12(a)(2) and §15 secondary-liability exposures that trail the §11 statute. The 6-year tail covers the full window in which pre-IPO conduct could give rise to a securities claim. Name the reasons shorter tails (1-year, 3-year) are not appropriate for an IPO.
2. **The pre-IPO carrier-negotiation discipline.** The requirement that the tail quote be anchored at renewal — expressed as a multiple of the expiring annual premium — before the S-1 goes effective, not after. The multiple figure carries `<!-- needs-research: ... -->`. The named leverage the corporation retains pre-bind (carrier competition, placement discretion, the broker's market-check) collapses once the S-1 is effective and the tail becomes a forced-purchase item.
3. **Interaction with the new public-company tower.** The tail freezes the pre-IPO policy at the trigger date — no coverage for wrongful acts committed after the IPO closing — and runs in parallel with the public-company tower that attaches at closing. The public-company tower covers wrongful acts committed after closing; the tail covers wrongful acts committed before closing, for claims made during the 6-year tail period. Name the potential-claim notification discipline that preserves pre-bind matters on the tail rather than pushing them into the new public-company policy.
4. **Change-in-control definition discipline.** The requirement that the change-in-control clause in the expiring policy be read against the IPO structure — primary offering, secondary selling-stockholder component, direct listing, de-SPAC alternative — to confirm the clause actually triggers at the IPO. Name the specific definitional points the corporate secretary and placement broker should align: majority-stock acquisition, sale of substantially all assets, controlling-investor transaction thresholds, and the de-SPAC-specific trigger if that alternative is live.
5. **Who owns the trigger decision.** The named ownership — the corporate secretary owns the trigger-event calendar; the CFO owns the premium funding; the general counsel owns the tail-quote negotiation; the governance subcommittee approves the tail commitment at the same meeting that approves the S-1 for effectiveness. The board resolution ratifying the tail purchase is in the UWC packet for the pricing meeting.
6. **The M&A-alternative tail posture.** The named posture if the IPO is overtaken by an M&A exit before the listing date — the target-side tail runs alongside the acquirer's ongoing D&O tower, the target-tail covers former target directors and officers for pre-close wrongful acts, and the acquirer's tower covers the combined company's directors and officers for post-close wrongful acts. Name the named-insured discipline for former target officers who join the acquirer and the preservation of the target-tail claims-notice channel.
7. **The disclosure posture in the S-1 risk factors.** The named risk-factor language the general counsel will coordinate with underwriting counsel if the tower is not yet rebuilt by S-1 effectiveness — a disclosure that the D&O programme is being restructured on the IPO trajectory and that the final structure may differ from the disclosed posture at pricing. The preferred posture is to have the bind complete and the tail commitment in place before the first-draft S-1 is filed so the risk-factor section can describe the completed structure rather than a programme-in-progress.

### Part E — Benchmark-quote defence memo

Author `benchmark-quote-defense-memo.md`. The placement broker's benchmark quote will almost certainly under-tower the programme against the IPO-trajectory exposure. Cover:

1. **The three or four routine objections to the recommended tower.** The named objections the governance subcommittee should expect and the counter-argument the general counsel will defend for each. At minimum the memo addresses:
   - **(i) "The benchmark survey for stage-X AI-infrastructure peers shows a lower total tower limit."** Counter: the benchmark surveys lag the IPO trajectory by a renewal cycle and are anchored on private-company exposure, not on Securities Act §11 / §12(a)(2) / §15 strict-liability and Exchange Act §10(b) / Rule 10b-5 fraud-on-the-market exposure that attaches at the registration statement's effective date.
   - **(ii) "The Side-A DIC adds premium for coverage that will rarely be triggered."** Counter: the Side-A DIC is the only layer that responds in insolvency, DGCL §145(b) derivative-settlement, and hostile-post-transaction scenarios, and the independent directors have signalled it is a seat-acceptance precondition.
   - **(iii) "The retention is below the public-company grid and the carrier will insist on a higher number."** Counter: the retention is the lever the corporation negotiates against the limit and the exclusion carve-backs; accepting a higher retention to preserve limit and carve-back scope is a defensible trade, but accepting both a higher retention and a weaker exclusion schedule is not.
   - **(iv) "The 6-year tail multiple is above the broker's benchmark."** Counter: the benchmark multiple reflects an average across stale market-cycle data; the current market and the AI-infrastructure risk profile justify paying above-benchmark to anchor the quote before the S-1 is effective and the corporation loses negotiating leverage.
2. **The benchmark-survey posture.** The named survey sources referenced by role only (industry D&O benchmark survey A, broker-published market report B) with `<!-- needs-research: ... -->` on the specific survey edition, publication date, and headline figures. The memo does not quote benchmark figures the general counsel has not verified against the current edition.
3. **The written record discipline.** The requirement that the governance subcommittee's decision to approve the recommended tower above the benchmark — or to accept a benchmark-aligned tower against the general counsel's recommendation — be recorded in minutes with the business-judgment-rule discipline from [chapter 02](../02-board-cadence-and-materials.md) and [chapter 05](../05-fiduciary-duties-of-directors.md). The decision is a §141(a) board decision subject to the duty-of-care standard the subcommittee will be evaluated against if a claim lands. Name the §141(e) reliance-on-experts protection the subcommittee is entitled to when it relies on the general counsel, the CFO, and the placement broker — the subcommittee's informed-reliance posture is what converts the decision into a business-judgment-rule decision rather than a bare-rationality decision.
4. **The AI-infrastructure risk-profile narrative.** The named narrative the general counsel will carry into the governance-subcommittee pre-read to anchor the above-benchmark tower against Maridian's specific exposure — the GPU-fabric capacity contracts, the concentration of large-model-training customers, the forward-revenue-recognition exposure that creates §10(b) / Rule 10b-5 risk at the first missed quarter, the Caremark AI-oversight exposure the Chancery Court's growing docket has flagged, and the §11 strict-liability exposure on the registration statement. The narrative is the bridge between the chapter-05 fiduciary-duty framework and the Part A tower structure; it is what justifies paying above the benchmark without appearing to over-buy.
5. **The failure-mode inventory the tower is sized against.** The named inventory of the specific claim scenarios the tower must fund through final adjudication: a §11 / §12(a)(2) class action following a post-IPO stock drop; a §10(b) / Rule 10b-5 fraud-on-the-market class action following a material disclosure miss; a derivative suit alleging Caremark AI-oversight failure; a derivative suit alleging Revlon-duty breach in a change-of-control alternative; an SEC enforcement action with a Wells notice preceding it; a DOJ investigation. Each scenario should be traceable to the tower limit the Part A specification defends.

### Part F — Indemnification-and-D&O interaction memo

Author `indemnification-and-d-and-o-interaction-memo.md`. The one-page memo tying the chapter-03 NVCA-form indemnification agreements to the D&O tower. Cover:

1. **The priority-of-payments stack.** The named sequence: (i) the D&O policy responds first under the priority-of-payments clause in the indemnification agreement; (ii) the corporation's own indemnification obligation under DGCL §145(a) (third-party claims), §145(b) (derivative claims, defence costs only), and §145(e) (mandatory advancement) responds second; (iii) any secondary indemnitor (a fund sponsor's indemnification of its designated director) responds third. Reference the NVCA-form indemnification-agreement priority clause as the controlling contract term. The corporate indemnity is primary against the corporation and the director; the D&O policy reimburses the corporation on Side B after the retention and funds the director directly on Side A when the corporation cannot.
2. **The Side-A directly-behind-the-balance-sheet posture.** The confirmation that the Side-A layer sits directly behind the corporate indemnity — when the corporation can indemnify and does, Side B reimburses the corporation; when the corporation cannot indemnify (insolvency, DGCL §145(b) derivative-settlement bar, hostile post-transaction refusal), Side A pays the director directly. The Side-A DIC drops down when the ABC Side A is denied or exhausted.
3. **The mandatory-advancement interface.** The chapter-03 NVCA mandatory-advancement clause requires the corporation to advance defence costs promptly upon receipt of an undertaking to repay if the director is ultimately found not entitled to indemnification. The advancement runs through Side B retention first, then Side B reimbursement. The named operational discipline: the corporate secretary tracks the undertaking, the CFO funds the advance, Side B reimburses after the retention is exhausted.
4. **The order-of-payments endorsement reference.** The cross-reference to Part A's order-of-payments endorsement — when tower limits are being depleted, Side A payments to individual directors are made ahead of Side B corporate reimbursements and Side C entity payments. Without that endorsement an entity claim could exhaust limits before individual directors are paid, defeating the priority-of-payments logic in the indemnification agreement.
5. **The DGCL §145(b) derivative-settlement bar and the Side-A response.** The specific statutory gap the Side-A layer fills — DGCL §145(b) bars a Delaware corporation from indemnifying a director or officer for amounts paid in settlement of a derivative action, and judgements in derivative actions are similarly barred (only defence costs are indemnifiable, and only if the director acted in good faith). The Side-A layer is the only recourse the director has in that scenario, and the Side-A DIC drops down when the underlying Side-A denies or is exhausted. Name this as the single most important structural reason Maridian is adding a Side-A DIC layer to the renewal.
