## Federal WARN Act — layoff and plant-closing notice

- **Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification Act (WARN Act)** — 29 U.S.C. §§ 2101–2109.
  - **§ 2101** — Definitions (employer, plant closing, mass layoff, employment loss, single site of employment, part-time employee).
  - **§ 2102** — Notice requirement (60-day advance written notice to affected employees or their representative, the state dislocated-worker unit, and the chief elected official of the unit of local government).
  - **§ 2102(b)** — The three exceptions (faltering-company, unforeseeable business circumstances, natural disaster). The exceptions reduce the notice period but do not eliminate the obligation to give as much notice as is practicable and to state the reason for the reduced notice.
  - **§ 2104** — Civil enforcement (back pay and benefits per employee for each day of violation up to 60 days; $500-per-day civil penalty payable to the local government unit, avoidable if the employer pays each affected employee within 3 weeks after the closing / layoff).
- **DOL implementing regulations** — 20 C.F.R. Part 639. Definitions, aggregation rules, single-site-of-employment analysis, and notice-content requirements.
- **DOL WARN Act Employer's Guide.** https://www.dol.gov/agencies/eta/layoffs/warn <!-- needs-research: confirm the current URL and version of the DOL Employer's Guide before treating specific formulations as canonical. -->
- **Aggregation of employment losses** — 29 U.S.C. § 2102(d) and 20 C.F.R. § 639.5(a)(1)(ii): individual employment losses within a 90-day period are aggregated for WARN purposes unless the employer can show the losses are the result of separate and distinct causes.

## State mini-WARN statutes

Verify the current statutory text and any amendments before quoting scope thresholds. The state landscape has moved substantially in the 2020–2026 window; several states expanded coverage during and after the pandemic-era emergency period.

- **California** — California WARN Act, Cal. Labor Code §§ 1400–1408. 75+ employees (currently or in the preceding 12 months); 60-day notice; mass-layoff threshold of 50+ employees at a "covered establishment" in any 30-day period. Notice to affected employees, the California Employment Development Department (EDD), and local officials. Judicial interpretation of the "unforeseeable business circumstances" exception was narrowed in *International Brotherhood of Boilermakers v. NASSCO Holdings Inc.* (Cal. Ct. App.) and related decisions; pandemic-era emergency executive orders modifying the notice period have expired. <!-- needs-research: confirm current EDD guidance and any 2024–2026 statutory amendments to Cal-WARN. -->
- **New York** — NY WARN Act, NY Labor Law §§ 860–860-i; 12 NYCRR Part 921. 50+ employees (excluding part-time); 90-day notice; mass-layoff threshold of 25+ affected representing at least 33% of the workforce, or 250+ regardless of percentage. Notice to affected employees, the NY Department of Labor's Rapid Response Unit, the chief local elected official, and the local Workforce Development Board.
- **New Jersey** — Millville Dallas Airmotive Plant Job Loss Notification Act (NJ WARN), N.J.S.A. §§ 34:21-1 to 34:21-7. 100+ employees (any status, not just full-time); 90-day notice; mass-layoff threshold of 50+ full-time or part-time employees with no percentage-of-workforce threshold. Uniquely mandates severance — 1 week of pay per year of service — for a covered event; doubled if the employer fails to give adequate notice. Amended by A4768 (effective 2023) to expand the coverage baseline and cement the mandatory-severance requirement.
- **Wisconsin** — Business Closing and Mass Layoff Law, Wis. Stat. § 109.07. 50+ employees; 60-day notice; business closing (25+) or mass layoff (25+ or 25% of workforce, whichever is greater) at a single site.
- **Illinois** — Illinois WARN Act, 820 ILCS 65. 75+ full-time employees; 60-day notice; plant closing (25+) or mass layoff (33% of workforce and at least 25 full-time employees, or 250+).
- **Iowa** — Iowa Code § 84C.
- **Maine** — Me. Rev. Stat. tit. 26, § 625-B (severance pay on relocation or closing).
- **Maryland** — Economic Stabilization Act, Md. Code, Labor & Employment §§ 11-301 to 11-304 (Maryland's WARN analogue was mandatory as of 2020; earlier version was voluntary).
- **Massachusetts** — no formal state WARN; some sector-specific and public-employer notice statutes.
- **Minnesota** — Minn. Stat. § 116L.976 (voluntary reporting statute; no mandatory notice period).
- **New Hampshire** — RSA 275-F.
- **Ohio** — Ohio Rev. Code § 4113.55 (30-day notice for certain covered actions).
- **Oregon** — no formal state WARN; specific notice requirements for public bodies.
- **Tennessee** — Tenn. Code § 50-1-601 et seq. (business closing / plant closing notice for employers with 50+ employees at a single site).
- **Vermont** — Vt. Stat. tit. 21, § 411 et seq. (notice on business closing).

## OWBPA and the age-40+ release

- **Older Workers Benefit Protection Act (OWBPA)** — amended the Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA), 29 U.S.C. § 626(f). Establishes the requirements for a knowing and voluntary waiver of ADEA claims:
  - **§ 626(f)(1)(A)–(E)** — written in a manner calculated to be understood; specific reference to ADEA rights; no waiver of future claims; consideration in addition to what the employee is already entitled to; written advice to consult counsel.
  - **§ 626(f)(1)(F)** — 21-day consideration period for a single-employee release; 45-day consideration period if the release is offered as part of an exit incentive or other employment-termination program offered to a group or class of employees.
  - **§ 626(f)(1)(G)** — 7-day post-signing revocation period during which the release is not enforceable.
  - **§ 626(f)(1)(H)** — group-release disclosure: the employer must inform each affected age-40+ employee, in writing, of (i) the class / unit / group of individuals covered, (ii) the eligibility factors, (iii) applicable time limits, and (iv) the job titles and ages of all individuals eligible or selected for the program and the ages of all individuals in the same job classification or organizational unit who are not eligible or selected.
- **Strict compliance rule** — *Oubre v. Entergy Operations, Inc.*, 522 U.S. 422 (1998). Failure to comply with OWBPA renders the ADEA waiver ineffective, even if the employee retains the severance consideration; the employee may sue for ADEA violations without tendering back the severance.
- **EEOC guidance** — "Understanding Waivers of Discrimination Claims in Employee Severance Agreements." https://www.eeoc.gov/laws/guidance/understanding-waivers-discrimination-claims-employee-severance-agreements <!-- needs-research: confirm current EEOC guidance version and any subsequent updates to the OWBPA compliance framework. -->

## ADEA and other federal anti-discrimination statutes cited across mod-107

- **Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA)** — 29 U.S.C. §§ 621–634. Applies to employers of 20+ employees.
- **Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964** — 42 U.S.C. § 2000e et seq. Foundational for demographic-slice review of PIP openings, promotion decisions, and RIF selection.
- **Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA)** — 42 U.S.C. § 12101 et seq. Accommodation obligations that may modify a PIP or a promotion decision.
- **Pregnancy Discrimination Act (PDA), Pregnant Workers Fairness Act (PWFA, 2022), and PUMP Act (2022)** — accommodation obligations relevant to PIP timing and RIF selection.
- **Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act (GINA)** — 42 U.S.C. § 2000ff et seq.
- **Section 1981 of the Civil Rights Act of 1866** — 42 U.S.C. § 1981. Race-based discrimination in the making and enforcement of contracts, including employment contracts. Longer statute of limitations than Title VII.
- **National Labor Relations Act (NLRA)** — 29 U.S.C. §§ 151–169. Section 7 protected activity constrains the confidentiality and non-disparagement clauses in severance agreements (see below).

## Speak Out Act and the EFAA — sexual-harassment and sexual-assault carve-outs

- **Speak Out Act of 2022** — Pub. L. 117-224, codified at 42 U.S.C. §§ 19401–19404. Voids the enforceability of *pre-dispute* nondisclosure and non-disparagement clauses relating to a sexual-assault or sexual-harassment dispute.
- **Ending Forced Arbitration of Sexual Assault and Sexual Harassment Act of 2021** (EFAA) — Pub. L. 117-90, codified at 9 U.S.C. §§ 401–402. Voids *pre-dispute* arbitration agreements and class-action waivers as applied to sexual-assault and sexual-harassment claims, at the election of the claimant. Signed February 2022.
- **Internal Revenue Code § 162(q)** — no employer tax deduction for settlements or attorneys' fees paid in connection with sexual-harassment or sexual-abuse matters if the settlement is subject to a nondisclosure agreement.

## State "Silenced No More" and separation-agreement carve-out statutes

Verify current text before drafting jurisdiction-specific severance templates. Several statutes were amended in the 2022–2024 window.

- **California** — Silenced No More Act, SB 331 (effective January 1, 2022), amending Cal. Gov. Code § 12964.5 and Cal. Code Civ. Proc. § 1001. Prohibits confidentiality clauses in settlement and separation agreements that restrict discussion of any unlawful workplace conduct under FEHA, not only sexual-harassment claims. Requires specific carve-out language in separation agreements offered in California.
- **New York** — NY Gen. Oblig. Law § 5-336 and NY C.P.L.R. § 4548 / § 5003-b. Restrictions on confidentiality clauses in settlement agreements relating to discrimination, harassment, and retaliation claims; requires specific consideration and revocation windows.
- **New Jersey** — N.J.S.A. § 10:5-12.8 (S121, effective March 2019). Voids non-disclosure provisions in employment contracts and settlement agreements that would conceal details of discrimination, retaliation, or harassment claims.
- **Illinois** — Workplace Transparency Act, 820 ILCS 96/1-1 et seq. Restrictions on confidentiality clauses covering discrimination, harassment, or retaliation claims; requires specific consideration and 21-day consideration + 7-day revocation windows for waivers of claims under Illinois human-rights law.
- **Oregon** — Workplace Fairness Act, ORS 659A.370–659A.377. Restrictions on non-disclosure, non-disparagement, and no-rehire clauses in separation agreements relating to discrimination or harassment claims.
- **Washington** — RCW 49.44.210. Voids non-disclosure and non-disparagement agreements that conceal illegal discrimination, harassment, retaliation, wage-and-hour violations, or sexual-assault claims.
- **Maine** — 26 M.R.S. § 599-D (2022 legislative session; restrictions on non-disclosure clauses in employment-related settlement agreements).

## NLRB Section 7 and *McLaren Macomb*

- **National Labor Relations Act (NLRA)** — 29 U.S.C. §§ 151–169. Section 7 (29 U.S.C. § 157) protects the right of employees to engage in concerted activity for mutual aid or protection, including discussion of terms and conditions of employment.
- ***McLaren Macomb***, 372 NLRB No. 58 (2023). NLRB held that severance agreements offered to non-supervisory employees containing overly broad confidentiality and non-disparagement clauses violate Section 8(a)(1) of the NLRA because they tend to chill Section 7 protected activity. Practical consequence: severance agreements must include a narrowly tailored non-disparagement clause and an explicit Section 7 carve-out ("nothing in this agreement prohibits the employee from engaging in activity protected by Section 7 of the NLRA, including discussion of wages, hours, and working conditions"). <!-- needs-research: track subsequent NLRB decisions applying and refining McLaren Macomb; the framework has been developed in Board and General Counsel guidance memoranda since 2023. -->
- **NLRB General Counsel Memorandum GC 23-05** (March 22, 2023). GC guidance on the scope and application of *McLaren Macomb*. https://www.nlrb.gov/news-outreach/news-story/nlrb-general-counsel-issues-memo-on-mclaren-macomb <!-- needs-research: confirm most recent NLRB GC memorandum on severance-agreement clauses. -->

## Final-pay timing statutes (state layer)

The state landscape is meaningful and highly variable. Verify each state's current statute and the Department of Labor's guidance before publishing a final-pay checklist.

- **California** — Cal. Labor Code §§ 201–203. Involuntary termination: final wages, including accrued vacation, due on the day of termination. Voluntary resignation with 72+ hours notice: due on the last day of work. Voluntary resignation without such notice: due within 72 hours. **§ 203 waiting-time penalty** — up to 30 days of wages for wilful failure to pay.
- **New York** — NY Labor Law § 191. Final wages due not later than the next regularly scheduled payday.
- **Illinois** — 820 ILCS 115/5 (Wage Payment and Collection Act). Final wages due at time of separation if possible, and no later than the next regularly scheduled payday.
- **Texas** — Tex. Labor Code § 61.014. Involuntary termination: within 6 days. Voluntary: next regularly scheduled payday.
- **Massachusetts** — M.G.L. c. 149, § 148. Involuntary: same day. Voluntary: next regularly scheduled payday.
- **Washington** — RCW 49.48.010. Final wages due on the next regularly scheduled payday.
- **Colorado** — Colo. Rev. Stat. § 8-4-109. Involuntary termination: immediately, or if payroll department is unavailable, next payday. Voluntary: next regularly scheduled payday.
- **Michigan, Ohio, Georgia, Florida, Pennsylvania** — next regularly scheduled payday under state wage-and-hour statutes.
- **Accrued but unused vacation / PTO on separation** — state-variable. California treats accrued vacation as a vested wage that must be paid out. Most other states leave payout to the employer's PTO policy. Confirm the state-by-state PTO-payout rule for every jurisdiction in which the corporation operates.

## COBRA and state mini-COBRA

- **Consolidated Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1985 (COBRA)** — ERISA §§ 601–608, 29 U.S.C. §§ 1161–1168; IRC § 4980B; PHSA §§ 2201–2208, 42 U.S.C. §§ 300bb-1 to 300bb-8.
  - Applies to group health plans of employers with 20+ employees on more than 50% of typical business days in the prior calendar year.
  - Qualifying event of termination (other than for gross misconduct) triggers 18-month continuation coverage; other qualifying events (divorce, death, dependent aging out) may trigger 36-month continuation.
  - Notice cadence — general notice at plan enrollment; employer notice to plan administrator within 30 days of qualifying event; plan administrator notice to qualified beneficiary within 14 days; 60-day election window; first premium due within 45 days of election.
  - Beneficiary pays up to 102% of the premium (100% cost plus 2% administrative fee).
- **DOL COBRA Continuation Coverage FAQ.** https://www.dol.gov/general/topic/health-plans/cobra
- **State mini-COBRA statutes** — cover employers below the federal 20-employee threshold and may extend the continuation period beyond 18 months. Verify current text.
  - **California** — Cal-COBRA, Cal. Health & Safety Code § 1366.20 et seq. (HMO plans); Cal. Ins. Code § 10128.50 et seq. (indemnity plans). Provides continuation for employers of 2–19 employees; Cal-COBRA can extend federal COBRA up to 36 months of total coverage under specified circumstances.
  - **Connecticut** — Conn. Gen. Stat. § 38a-538.
  - **New York** — NY Ins. Law § 3221(m) and § 4305(e) (up to 36 months of continuation).
  - **Texas, Illinois, New Jersey, Massachusetts, and others** — verify current statute; scope and duration vary.
