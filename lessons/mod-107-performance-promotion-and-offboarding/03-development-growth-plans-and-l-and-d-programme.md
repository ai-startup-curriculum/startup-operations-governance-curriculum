# 3. Development, growth plans, and the L&D programme

> The other half of performance management — the semi-annual growth conversation, the individual development plan, the manager-coaching programme, the L&D budget, and the internal-mobility door that keeps people from having to quit to grow.

## Motivation

Performance management (chapter 01) rates *what happened*. Promotion (chapter 02) rewards it. Neither answers the question every good employee is asking at least once a year: **what am I growing into, and what does this corporation do to help me get there?**

If the corporation has no answer, three things happen:

- The strongest employees leave first — they are the ones with external options, and "there is nowhere to grow here" is one of the most common reasons cited in the exit interview (chapter 08).
- The weakest employees stay longest — they have the fewest external options and see the least urgency to grow, and the corporation drifts.
- The middle of the pool disengages quietly — they show up, they hit their goals, and they optimise their week for a job outside work.

The development / growth-plan / L&D programme is the operating instrument that puts a plausible answer in front of every employee every six months. It costs real money — a Series-A norm is $1–3k per employee per year, more at exec level — and real management time, and it pays back in retention, promotion pipeline, and internal mobility.

## The four operating decisions

1. **The semi-annual growth conversation.** A calendared 1:1 in which career, not performance, is the topic.
2. **The individual development plan (IDP).** The written artifact that comes out of the growth conversation.
3. **The manager-coaching programme.** How the corporation trains and supports the managers who deliver the growth conversation.
4. **The L&D budget and tool suite.** Per-employee allocation, exec-level overlay, and platform selection.

An eventual fifth decision, sitting inside the same programme:

5. **The internal-mobility programme.** The door that lets an engineer become a PM, a customer-success rep move into product marketing, a support agent grow into engineering — without leaving.

## Decision 1 — The semi-annual growth conversation

Growth conversations are separate from performance conversations for a reason: mixing them collapses both. If the manager is delivering a rating in the same meeting, the employee cannot honestly discuss what they want to grow into without fear the answer will be read as "not committed to the current role."

**The cadence.** Semi-annual, offset from the formal review cycle. If reviews land in November and June, growth conversations land in March and September — six weeks after each comp / promotion cycle closes, when the outcome is known and the employee's forward-looking energy is available.

**The instrument.** A 60-minute 1:1 with a written prompt sent to the employee 48 hours ahead:

- What have you enjoyed most / least in your work over the last six months?
- Where do you want to be in 12 months? In 3 years?
- What skills / experience do you need to build to get there?
- What does this manager / company need to do differently to help?
- What role / team, if any, would you consider moving into?

**The output.** A written summary in the shared 1:1 doc — the employee's answers, the manager's commitments, one to three concrete development goals for the next six months. This is the seed of the IDP.

The single most common failure mode is treating the growth conversation as optional or slippable. It is not — it is the *only* moment in the calendar in which the manager is explicitly asking "what do you want next?" and the employee is explicitly allowed to answer without hedging. Missing it once is defensible; missing it in successive cycles is a leading indicator that the employee will be gone within a year.

## Decision 2 — The individual development plan (IDP)

The IDP is the artifact that turns the growth conversation into a track-able six-month plan. A minimum-viable IDP has five fields:

1. **Growth goal.** Skill, competency, experience, or role the employee is developing toward. Named specifically ("scope from lead-engineer projects to multi-team programmes," not "grow as a leader").
2. **Why.** What promotion path, role change, or personal-mastery outcome this goal supports.
3. **How.** The specific activities — a stretch project, a training, a coaching engagement, a rotation, a mentorship — the employee will do to develop the goal.
4. **Support.** What the manager, the corporation, or the L&D budget will provide (a $2k Reforge course budget, a monthly BetterUp coach, a dedicated stretch project, sponsor introductions).
5. **Review checkpoint.** When this IDP will be re-reviewed — typically the next growth conversation six months out, with a shorter mid-cycle checkpoint at the 90-day mark.

**IDPs are not performance goals.** Performance goals (what the employee will *deliver* this cycle) live in the performance-management operating system in [chapter 01](./01-performance-management-operating-system.md); IDPs (what the employee will *become*) live separately. Fusing the two is a common tool-driven mistake — Lattice, 15Five, and Betterworks all offer "goals" modules that can be used for either, and using the same module for both makes the distinction invisible and the growth conversation short.

**IDPs should be lightweight.** Two to three growth goals per cycle is enough. A ten-goal IDP is a wishlist, not a plan.

## Decision 3 — The manager-coaching programme

Growth conversations only work if the manager can hold them. Most first-time managers cannot — they were promoted for individual contribution, not for the ability to help another person figure out what to be next. The corporation's job is to develop the managers first.

**The three-layer manager-development programme:**

1. **New-manager training.** A one-time onboarding programme for anyone promoted into a first management role. Content: the manager-role transition, the 1:1 cadence, the feedback frame (SBI: situation-behaviour-impact), the review-writing and review-delivery workshops from [chapter 01](./01-performance-management-operating-system.md), and the growth-conversation instrument from this chapter. Cadence: within 90 days of the promotion.
2. **Ongoing manager training.** A recurring rhythm — monthly manager forum, quarterly manager-development workshop, annual manager offsite — that keeps the manager corps calibrated and gives newer managers access to more experienced ones. Content: rotates across specific topics (hiring, hard feedback, calibration, PIP mechanics, layoff communications, etc.).
3. **1:1 coaching.** A per-manager coaching engagement — external (BetterUp, Better Manager, individual coach) or internal — that gives every manager, especially newer ones, a person outside the reporting chain to talk to about their people problems. External coaching is expensive (~$2–8k per manager per year) but is one of the highest-ROI L&D investments a Series-A / Series-B people function makes.

**The programme's ownership.** The head of people typically owns the design and delivery; a dedicated L&D or people-development lead becomes justifiable at ~100+ employees; a Chief People / People Ops leader owns the strategic direction of manager development at Series-B and beyond.

## Decision 4 — The L&D budget and tool suite

**Budget allocation.** The mainstream Series-A / Series-B pattern:

- **Per-employee L&D stipend** — typically $1,000–$3,000 per employee per year, spendable on courses, books, conferences, and coaching. Some companies structure this as a use-it-or-lose-it annual allowance (encourages spending); others structure it as a two-year rollover (encourages larger investments). At the manager and executive level, the budget grows meaningfully — $5–10k per manager per year, $15–30k+ per executive per year (typically covering executive coaching and Reforge / peer-CxO cohorts).
- **Programmatic L&D budget** — funded centrally at a rate of ~1–2% of payroll at Series-A / Series-B (industry-typical is 1.4%; benchmark against Training industry benchmarks and the ATD *State of the Industry* report — <!-- needs-research: confirm the current-year ATD State of the Industry benchmark before quoting a specific percentage of payroll as canonical -->). Covers company-wide programmes: manager training, new-hire onboarding L&D, DEI training, compliance training, tuition-reimbursement.

**Platform selection.**

- **General-purpose learning platforms.** [LinkedIn Learning](https://learning.linkedin.com/), [Udemy Business](https://business.udemy.com/), [Coursera for Business](https://www.coursera.org/business), [MasterClass](https://www.masterclass.com/business). Broad content library; suited to self-directed skills-building; typical per-seat pricing ~$200–$400 / year.
- **Cohort-based executive and functional programmes.** [Reforge](https://www.reforge.com/) (product / growth / marketing / engineering), Harvard Business School Online, Wharton Online, Stanford GSB executive programs. Higher price point (~$1,500–$5,000+ per cohort), higher signal, higher application to a specific role.
- **Coaching platforms.** [BetterUp](https://www.betterup.com/), [Better Manager](https://bettermanager.us/), Ezra, Torch. Per-user pricing typically $2k–$8k / user / year for a coaching engagement. Best for individual managers and executives, not for broad L&D.
- **Compliance and required-training platforms.** [EVERFI](https://everfi.com/), [Emtrain](https://emtrain.com/), [Traliant](https://www.traliant.com/), [Ethena](https://www.goethena.com/). Cover state-mandated harassment prevention (California SB 1343, New York State + NYC, Illinois SB 75, Delaware, Connecticut, Maine, Washington, etc.), anti-bribery, security awareness. Content library rather than developmental — but the compliance-training piece is a *required* line item, not optional.

**The selection rule.** Match platform to need: a per-seat learning platform for the general population, a coaching platform for managers and executives, a cohort programme budget for the top 10–20% of the workforce, a compliance platform for the required-training baseline. Do not pay for four overlapping platforms because each function head asked for their favourite.

## Decision 5 — The internal-mobility programme

The internal-mobility programme is what turns "there's no path here" into "there is a path — you don't have to leave to find it."

**The core elements:**

- **An internal job board.** Every new open req is posted internally at least a week before it goes external. Internal candidates can apply through the standard ATS from [mod-104 chapter 02](../mod-104-hiring-onboarding-and-hr-operations/02-ats-selection-and-integration.md) — often flagged so recruiters and hiring managers give the internal candidate a first-look interview.
- **A transfer policy.** The rules: how long an employee must be in role before they can transfer (typically 12 months); whether the current manager can block a transfer (typically no, but they must be notified before the transfer conversation starts); how the transfer timeline runs (typically 4–8 weeks to allow the current team to plan for the departure); how compensation is handled on transfer (typically no adjustment unless the new role is at a different level).
- **A cross-functional rotation programme.** More formal than a transfer: a 3–6 month tour of duty in another function, with return rights to the original team. Rotations are expensive to run (they demand backfill planning on both sides) but are one of the most durable retention instruments the corporation has for the top-quartile of the workforce.
- **A transparency norm.** Managers are trained to have the "what would you want to do next?" question as a routine part of the growth conversation, and to not treat an internal-transfer inquiry as disloyalty. The internal-mobility programme fails whenever the ambient signal from managers is "if you want to move, we'll help — but I'll be disappointed."

**The measurable outcomes.** Internal mobility as a share of total hires is the primary metric — a healthy Series-B / growth-stage company runs 15–30% of total hires as internal moves. The other metric worth tracking is *time-to-first-move* — how long, on average, before an employee makes their first internal transition. Companies with vibrant internal-mobility programmes see this in the 18–30 month range; companies without them see almost no internal mobility at all.

## Concrete example: a Series-B L&D programme

A 200-person, Series-B B2B company. Head of People is designing the L&D programme.

- **Growth conversations.** Semi-annual (March, September), separate from formal reviews (November, June). Written prompt sent 48 hours ahead; 60-minute 1:1; written summary in the shared 1:1 doc.
- **IDPs.** Two-goal template in Notion. Growth goal, why, how, support, checkpoint. Reviewed at each growth conversation.
- **Manager coaching.** New-manager training within 90 days of promotion (three-day workshop). Monthly manager forum (one hour). Quarterly manager development workshop (half day, topic rotates). BetterUp coaching engagement for every people manager at a cost of ~$3.6k / manager / year.
- **L&D budget.** $2k / employee / year, use-it-or-lose-it. $5k for managers, $15k for VPs, $25k for C-level. Programmatic budget of ~1.5% of payroll for manager training, DEI and compliance training, and the new-hire onboarding L&D portion of the [mod-104](../mod-104-hiring-onboarding-and-hr-operations/06-first-90-day-onboarding.md) programme.
- **Platforms.** LinkedIn Learning as the per-seat baseline; Reforge subscription for the top ~15% of the population; BetterUp for people managers; Ethena for compliance and harassment-prevention training.
- **Internal mobility.** Internal-first job-post window of one week; 12-month tenure rule for transfers; annual cross-functional rotation programme (four to six seats a year); Head of Talent Acquisition and Head of People jointly own the programme.

Total annual cost: ~$1.1M for the 200-person company (roughly 3% of payroll). Retention impact and internal-hire percentage are tracked in the quarterly people-ops review.

## Summary

- Development is the other half of performance management. Neglecting it costs the top of the workforce first.
- The semi-annual growth conversation is separate from the formal review — do not merge them.
- The IDP is a lightweight artifact (two to three goals) written in the growth conversation and reviewed at the next one.
- Manager coaching (new-manager training, ongoing manager forum, 1:1 coaching) is the highest-ROI L&D investment the corporation makes.
- Per-employee L&D budget of $1–3k / year at Series-A, higher at manager and exec levels. Platform selection matches need — learning platform for the general population, coaching for managers, cohort programmes for the top decile, compliance for the required baseline.
- Internal mobility is the door that keeps talent from having to leave to grow. Post internally first; author a transfer policy; consider a rotation programme at Series-B+.
