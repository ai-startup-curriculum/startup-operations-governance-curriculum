# 7. The EEOC protected-category framework across the employment lifecycle

> Protected-category analysis is not a diversity program. It is a body of federal, state, and city statutes that make certain hiring, comp, promotion, and termination decisions unlawful — and expensive.

## Motivation

Every non-trivial workforce decision — who to recruit, who to interview, who to hire, what to pay, who to promote, who to terminate, who to accommodate, who to lay off — is made in the shadow of a federal, state, and increasingly city-level set of anti-discrimination statutes. The statutes do not require the corporation to reach any particular outcome. They forbid the corporation from making the decision *because of* a protected characteristic (disparate treatment) and, in most contexts, from using facially-neutral practices that produce a substantial adverse effect on a protected group without a legitimate business justification (disparate impact).

Get this wrong and the corporation buys some combination of: an EEOC Charge of Discrimination, a state fair-employment-practice-agency (FEPA) charge, a right-to-sue letter and Title VII / § 1981 / state-law litigation, a Department of Labor OFCCP audit if the corporation ever becomes a federal contractor, a state attorney general investigation, a state civil-rights-agency action, and — in California, New York, and increasingly elsewhere — private class-action exposure with fee-shifting statutes that make defense uneconomic. A single-plaintiff Title VII case commonly runs the defendant $250,000–$500,000+ in outside-counsel fees before a summary-judgment ruling. A class action, or a case that reaches trial, runs into the millions.

The framework is well-defined, has been stable for decades on the federal core (Title VII, ADA, ADEA, EPA, PDA, GINA), and is expanding rapidly at the state and city level (gender identity, sexual orientation, source of income, criminal history, hair texture, salary history, reproductive-health decisions, weight, height, and others). A COO / GC operating in 2026 needs to know both the federal core and the state-and-city expansion — the corporation cannot rely on a federal-only checklist when its actual workforce lives in Los Angeles, Seattle, Denver, Chicago, and New York.

## The federal statutory core

### Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 (42 U.S.C. § 2000e et seq.)

Title VII prohibits discrimination by covered employers (15+ employees) on the basis of **race, color, religion, sex, or national origin** in any term or condition of employment — hiring, firing, compensation, benefits, promotions, transfers, work assignments, layoffs, or any other action affecting employment status.

- **"Sex" includes sexual orientation and gender identity** since *Bostock v. Clayton County*, 590 U.S. 644 (2020). The Court held that firing an employee because they are gay or transgender is discrimination "because of sex" within the meaning of Title VII. This is federal law, applicable in every state, in every industry, at every employer of 15+.
- **"Religion" includes sincerely-held religious beliefs and practices** and imposes a **reasonable-accommodation** duty on the employer unless the accommodation would impose an "undue hardship." In *Groff v. DeJoy*, 600 U.S. 447 (2023), the Court raised the undue-hardship standard from the prior *Hardison* "de minimis" formulation to a substantial-cost-in-the-conduct-of-the-employer's-business standard. This materially expanded employer religious-accommodation obligations.
- **"National origin"** covers ancestry, ethnicity, accent, and (in most circumstances) English-only rules that lack a legitimate business justification.
- **"Race" and "color"** are separately protected — an intra-racial color-based claim is cognisable.

Title VII creates two liability theories:

- **Disparate treatment** — intentional discrimination against an individual or a class because of a protected characteristic. Proved by direct evidence (rare) or by the *McDonnell Douglas* burden-shifting framework (prima facie case → employer's legitimate non-discriminatory reason → pretext).
- **Disparate impact** — a facially-neutral policy that has a substantial adverse effect on a protected group, unless the employer can show the policy is job-related and consistent with business necessity, and unless the plaintiff can show a less-discriminatory alternative would serve the same interest (Griggs / *Wards Cove* / § 703(k) framework).

**Remedies.** Back pay (up to 2 years), front pay, reinstatement, compensatory damages for emotional distress, punitive damages if the employer acted with malice or reckless indifference (capped at $50,000 to $300,000 by employer size under 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(3)), attorneys' fees to the prevailing plaintiff under 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-5(k). Additional uncapped compensatory and punitive damages are available under 42 U.S.C. § 1981 for race-based claims — which is why race-discrimination plaintiffs almost always plead § 1981 alongside Title VII.

### The Americans with Disabilities Act (42 U.S.C. § 12101 et seq.)

The ADA prohibits discrimination on the basis of **disability** by covered employers (15+ employees), and requires **reasonable accommodation** of qualified individuals with disabilities unless the accommodation would cause an "undue hardship."

- **Definition of disability** — a physical or mental impairment that substantially limits one or more major life activities, a record of such impairment, or being regarded as having such impairment. The ADA Amendments Act of 2008 (ADAAA) rejected the Supreme Court's earlier narrow constructions and directed a broad interpretation. Substantially fewer individuals fail the "qualifying disability" threshold today than before 2008. Practically, if a candidate or employee requests an accommodation, treat the underlying condition as a qualifying disability and move directly to the interactive-process and reasonable-accommodation analysis.
- **Reasonable accommodation** — the employer must engage in an "interactive process" with the individual to identify accommodations that would enable the individual to perform the essential functions of the job. Typical accommodations include modified work schedules, ergonomic equipment, remote-work arrangements, extended leaves beyond FMLA/state leave entitlement, job restructuring, and reassignment to a vacant position. The employer is not required to eliminate essential functions, lower productivity standards, or provide personal-use items.
- **Undue hardship** — significant difficulty or expense, evaluated against the employer's overall resources, the nature and cost of the accommodation, and the impact on operations. High bar for a corporation of any size.
- **The medical-inquiry / medical-exam rules (42 U.S.C. § 12112(d)).** *Before* a conditional offer, the employer may not ask about disability or require a medical exam. *After* a conditional offer and *before* the start date, the employer may require a medical exam or medical inquiry if it is uniformly required of all entrants into the same job category. *After* employment starts, medical inquiries and exams must be job-related and consistent with business necessity.
- **Pre-employment "essential functions" questions are permitted.** The employer may ask a candidate whether they can perform the essential functions of the job, with or without accommodation — but not what accommodation they might need.

**Remedies.** Same structure as Title VII (back pay, front pay, compensatory / punitive damages within the § 1981a cap, attorneys' fees). Additional remedies under state-law disability statutes are common — California's FEHA has no damages cap, for instance.

### The Age Discrimination in Employment Act (29 U.S.C. § 621 et seq.)

The ADEA prohibits discrimination against employees or applicants **age 40 or older** by covered employers (20+ employees). Unlike Title VII, the ADEA does not use the § 1981a damages structure; remedies are back pay + liquidated damages equal to back pay if the violation was "wilful" (29 U.S.C. § 626(b)), plus attorneys' fees. No compensatory or punitive damages under federal ADEA — but state analogs (California FEHA, New York State Human Rights Law) frequently offer both.

The **Older Workers Benefit Protection Act (OWBPA)** — 29 U.S.C. § 626(f) — sets specific formalities for ADEA waivers in separation agreements: written, referring specifically to ADEA rights, advising consultation with counsel, 21-day consideration period (45 days for a group termination with disclosure of the class members' ages and job titles), 7-day revocation period. A separation agreement that lacks any of these formalities does not waive ADEA claims regardless of what it says — see [mod-102 chapter 07](../mod-102-founding-team-legal-architecture/07-founder-employment-relationship-and-departure.md) and [mod-107](../mod-107-performance-promotion-and-offboarding/) for depth.

### The Pregnancy Discrimination Act (42 U.S.C. § 2000e(k))

The PDA amends Title VII to make pregnancy, childbirth, and related medical conditions protected characteristics under the "sex" prong. An employer must treat a pregnant employee the same as a similarly-situated non-pregnant employee — in leave, in accommodation, in job assignment, in benefits.

The **Pregnant Workers Fairness Act (PWFA)** — Pub. L. No. 117-328, effective June 27, 2023 — separately requires reasonable accommodation of known limitations related to pregnancy, childbirth, or related medical conditions, applying the ADA-style interactive-process and undue-hardship framework. The **Providing Urgent Maternal Protections for Nursing Mothers Act (PUMP Act)** — 29 U.S.C. § 218d — extends lactation-accommodation obligations that had been limited to non-exempt employees under prior law.

### The Equal Pay Act (29 U.S.C. § 206(d))

The EPA — an amendment to the FLSA — prohibits sex-based wage discrimination for employees performing "equal work" in the same establishment. Equal work is jobs requiring equal skill, effort, and responsibility, performed under similar working conditions. Wage differentials are permitted only under one of four affirmative defenses: seniority system, merit system, system measuring earnings by quantity or quality of production, or "any other factor other than sex."

The EPA carries a two-year statute of limitations (three years for wilful violations), back pay plus liquidated damages equal to back pay, and attorneys' fees. It does not require intent to discriminate — a wage differential that lacks an affirmative-defense justification is unlawful.

**State equal-pay statutes are broader.** California's Fair Pay Act (Cal. Lab. Code § 1197.5), New York's Achieve Pay Equity Act, and equivalents in a growing set of states extend the equal-work standard to "substantially similar work" (rather than "equal work"), extend the "same establishment" scope, and narrow the "any other factor" defense to bona-fide-factor-other-than-sex analyses. Several statutes also prohibit **salary-history inquiries** by employers, on the theory that prior-employer wage discrimination is inherited if the current employer sets pay by reference to prior salary. See [chapter 03](./03-offer-letter-architecture-and-pay-transparency.md) for the drafting implications.

### The Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act (42 U.S.C. § 2000ff et seq.)

GINA prohibits employment discrimination on the basis of **genetic information**, defined broadly to include the individual's genetic tests, the genetic tests of family members, and the manifestation of a disease or disorder in family members. It also imposes strict limits on the employer's acquisition of genetic information, including through wellness programs and post-offer medical exams.

GINA is often overlooked because it comes up less frequently than the other statutes — but the acquisition-limitation piece (§ 2000ff-1(b)) is what usually snares an employer. If a wellness program asks about family medical history, the corporation has acquired genetic information; if a post-offer medical exam includes questions about family history, the corporation has acquired genetic information. The safe posture: the corporation's wellness vendor and post-offer medical-exam vendor should have GINA-compliant intake forms that exclude family-medical-history questions, and the corporation should not receive genetic information as part of any employment process.

### The Uniformed Services Employment and Reemployment Rights Act (38 U.S.C. § 4301 et seq.)

USERRA prohibits employment discrimination against members of the uniformed services and provides reemployment rights after military service. It applies to all employers regardless of size. USERRA claims are enforced by the DOL Veterans' Employment and Training Service (VETS) and by private civil suit; remedies include back pay, liquidated damages for wilful violations, and injunctive reinstatement.

### 42 U.S.C. § 1981

Section 1981, part of the Civil Rights Act of 1866, prohibits race-based discrimination in the making and enforcement of contracts — including employment contracts. It applies to all employers regardless of size. Its remedies include uncapped compensatory and punitive damages and attorneys' fees. Race-discrimination plaintiffs routinely plead § 1981 alongside Title VII to escape the § 1981a damages cap.

### The National Labor Relations Act (29 U.S.C. § 151 et seq.)

The NLRA is not principally an anti-discrimination statute, but it prohibits employer retaliation against employees for **protected concerted activity** — discussing wages, working conditions, or unionisation with coworkers. This matters here because a substantial number of "hostile work environment" or "retaliation" allegations that plaintiffs frame under Title VII also state a claim under NLRA § 7, and because certain confidentiality and non-disparagement provisions in offer letters, separation agreements, or employee handbooks that limit an employee's ability to discuss wages or working conditions with coworkers can violate NLRA § 8(a)(1) as unlawful workplace rules. See NLRB, *McLaren Macomb*, 372 NLRB No. 58 (Feb. 21, 2023), voiding overbroad confidentiality and non-disparagement clauses in a separation agreement.

## The state-and-city expansion layer

The federal core is the floor, not the ceiling. State and city statutes expand the protected-category list, lower the employer-size threshold (many state statutes cover employers with 1+ or 5+ employees, rather than Title VII's 15+ or ADEA's 20+), and often provide broader remedies without federal damages caps.

**Sexual orientation and gender identity.** Federal law (*Bostock*) protects both. State laws in California, New York, Illinois, Colorado, Washington, Massachusetts, and many others have long done so with more granular provisions (bathroom-access, pronoun-use, name-change accommodation, dress-code neutrality).

**Marital status, familial status, source of income.** Many state and city laws prohibit discrimination based on marital status, whether the individual has children, or the source of the individual's lawful income (housing statutes matter for benefits programs; employment applications sometimes matter too).

**Criminal history — "ban the box" and Fair Chance laws.** California (Fair Chance Act, Cal. Gov. Code § 12952), New York City (Fair Chance Act, NYC Admin. Code § 8-107(11)), Los Angeles City, Illinois, and many other jurisdictions restrict when an employer may inquire about criminal history (typically after a conditional offer of employment), require individualised assessment before an adverse action based on criminal history, and require specific notice and rescission-of-offer procedures. Employers in these jurisdictions cannot use a blanket "no felons" screen without violating the statute. See [chapter 08](./08-state-law-variance.md).

**Salary history.** California (Cal. Lab. Code § 432.3), New York State, Massachusetts, Colorado, Washington, Oregon, Illinois, and many cities prohibit employers from asking candidates about salary history and, in some cases, from relying on salary history to set pay. See [chapter 03](./03-offer-letter-architecture-and-pay-transparency.md).

**Salary transparency in job postings.** Colorado (2021), Washington (2023), New York City (2022), New York State (2023), California (2023), Illinois (2025), Minnesota (2025), Vermont, Massachusetts. Requires disclosure of the compensation range in the job posting itself. Enforcement is variable; California's law includes a private right of action. See [chapter 03](./03-offer-letter-architecture-and-pay-transparency.md) for depth.

**Reproductive-health decisions.** New York, California, Illinois, and several other states prohibit discrimination based on an employee's reproductive-health decisions, including contraception, pregnancy, and abortion.

**Hair texture and protective hairstyles (CROWN Acts).** California (Cal. Gov. Code § 12926), New York, New Jersey, Illinois, and a growing set of states prohibit discrimination based on hair texture or protective hairstyles historically associated with race.

**Off-duty conduct, cannabis, medical-marijuana use.** State-level variance is significant. New York, California, and others protect off-duty lawful conduct or off-duty cannabis use with narrow safety-sensitive-position exceptions. Confirm the current state list before implementing a drug-testing program.

**Weight and height.** New York City (Local Law 61 of 2023) prohibits employment discrimination based on weight or height. Michigan has prohibited height/weight discrimination for decades. Localised statutes in San Francisco, Washington DC, and others.

**Caste.** Seattle (2023) prohibits caste-based discrimination. California passed SB 403 in 2023, though it was vetoed by Governor Newsom. <!-- needs-research: verify the current status of caste-based discrimination statutes at the state and local level. -->

**Immigration status and national-origin protection.** In addition to federal Title VII "national origin" coverage, IRCA (8 U.S.C. § 1324b) prohibits citizenship-status discrimination and document-abuse in the I-9 process — enforced by the DOJ Immigrant and Employee Rights Section (IER).

**Domestic-violence-victim protection.** California, New York, Illinois, and others protect employees who are victims of domestic violence, sexual assault, or stalking from adverse employment actions and require reasonable safety accommodation.

The state-and-city layer expands materially every year. A national employer's compliance posture is to (a) maintain a running matrix of the state and city protected categories at every location where it employs anyone, and (b) either default to the union of protections in its national policies (the highest-common-denominator approach) or maintain per-location policy variants (the per-jurisdiction approach). See [chapter 08](./08-state-law-variance.md).

## The lifecycle: applying the framework at each decision point

### 1. Recruiting and job postings

- **Job description.** Focus on essential functions and qualifications, not proxies for protected characteristics. Avoid "recent college graduate" (age-proxy), "digital native" (age-proxy), "young and energetic team" (age-proxy), unnecessary physical-ability requirements (disability-proxy), and gendered language ("salesman," "waitress," "guys").
- **Advertising channels.** Facebook / Meta Ads and LinkedIn have historically permitted audience-targeting parameters that constituted disparate-treatment discrimination when used for employment ads (e.g., age-bracket targeting). Settlements with HUD and the DOJ have required both platforms to remove protected-category targeting from employment and housing advertisers. The corporation is nonetheless responsible for how it uses the platform — do not target job ads by age, gender, or any protected characteristic.
- **Salary-transparency-compliant range disclosure** — see chapter 03.
- **Reasonable-accommodation and equal-opportunity statements** — the standard "Acme is an equal-opportunity employer and considers all qualified applicants without regard to [full protected-category list]. Applicants with disabilities who require accommodation in the application process may contact [designated address]" boilerplate. Include the full statutory list applicable to every jurisdiction the corporation employs in.

### 2. Application intake

- **Do not ask** — on the application, in the ATS pre-screen, or informally — questions about age, date of birth (except where necessary to confirm the applicant is 18+), race, ethnicity, national origin, religion, marital status, family status, sexual orientation, gender identity, disability, genetic information, prior workers'-compensation claims, arrest record (many states) or conviction record (subject to Fair Chance rules), or, in salary-history-ban jurisdictions, prior salary.
- **Voluntary self-identification** for EEO-1 / diversity-reporting purposes is permitted if it is (i) genuinely voluntary, (ii) stored separately from the hiring file, (iii) not accessible to decision-makers, and (iv) accompanied by the required non-disclosure statement.
- **Ban-the-box compliance** — do not ask about criminal history on the initial application in ban-the-box jurisdictions; defer the inquiry to the post-conditional-offer stage.

### 3. Interviewing

Interviewers must be trained. The single largest source of preventable discrimination-claim liability at a startup is an untrained interviewer who asks a "friendly" question about the candidate's family plans, religious observance, national origin, age ("so when did you graduate?"), disability ("that's a great accent — where are you from?"), or living situation.

Interviewers may ask:

- Whether the candidate is legally authorised to work in the US and whether the candidate will require sponsorship, now or in the future. (Do not ask about citizenship or immigration status specifically.)
- Whether the candidate can perform the essential functions of the job, with or without reasonable accommodation.
- Whether the candidate is willing to travel, work required hours, or relocate — as job-related, non-discriminatory business inquiries.
- Any question directly probing the candidate's skills, experience, and qualifications.

Interviewers may **not** ask:

- Age, date of birth, or graduation year.
- Marital status, family status, plans for children, childcare arrangements.
- Country of origin, ancestry, native language (except where a specific language is a bona-fide occupational qualification).
- Religion, religious practice, availability for weekend or evening work framed in a way that probes religious observance.
- Sexual orientation, gender identity, pronouns (some jurisdictions require an opt-in, but never as a screening question).
- Disability, medical history, prescription use, workers'-comp history, mental-health history.
- Criminal history in ban-the-box jurisdictions before a conditional offer; anywhere before a job-related, individualised assessment.
- Salary history in salary-history-ban jurisdictions.
- Anything that could be understood as a proxy for a protected characteristic.

**Interview scorecards** should map to the essential functions and required qualifications in the job description; free-form scorecards that record subjective impressions ("didn't seem like a culture fit," "wouldn't fit in with the team") are disparate-impact and disparate-treatment evidence in a subsequent lawsuit.

### 4. Hiring and offer

- **Pay-setting.** Set the offered salary from the corporation's compensation architecture (mod-106), the posted range (chapter 03), the level and role, the candidate's skills, and market data — not from the candidate's prior salary (in ban jurisdictions, this is required; elsewhere, it is best practice for avoiding EPA / state pay-equity liability).
- **Contingent-condition compliance.** Any conditional offer that is contingent on background check must comply with the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA) at 15 U.S.C. § 1681 et seq., the corresponding state consumer-reporting statutes, and any applicable Fair Chance / ban-the-box rules — including the specific pre-adverse-action notice, the individualised-assessment requirement, and the post-adverse-action notice.
- **I-9 verification** must be conducted uniformly on every new hire; document-abuse (requiring more or specific documents than the I-9 permits) violates IRCA § 274B and the DOJ IER enforces it aggressively.
- **Reasonable-accommodation onboarding.** If a new hire discloses a disability at any point during onboarding — including at the I-9 stage or at the new-hire physical — start the interactive process immediately. Do not delay start dates on the basis of an accommodation request.

### 5. Compensation-setting and adjustment

- **Equal Pay Act / state pay-equity analysis.** Regularly audit pay by role and level, controlling for legitimate factors (experience, performance, seniority), and identify any pay differentials that lack a legitimate justification. Correct the differentials promptly. In California, an EPA-compliant analysis is a critical defense to a Cal. Lab. Code § 1197.5 claim.
- **Pay-transparency compliance in postings.** See chapter 03. The disclosure obligation attaches to *all* postings covered by the applicable statute — internal postings included in many jurisdictions.
- **Salary-history bans.** Do not rely on prior salary to set pay in ban jurisdictions.

### 6. Performance, promotion, and merit

- **Performance-management systems** should have written, job-related criteria, calibration to control for rater bias, and documentation. A performance-management system that produces materially disparate scores by race, sex, or age without a legitimate explanation is disparate-impact evidence.
- **Promotion criteria** must be job-related and consistent with business necessity. Subjective "leadership potential" or "executive presence" criteria are disparate-impact vulnerabilities; if the corporation uses them, calibrate them and document them.
- **Merit-cycle audits** — the same audit as for pay-setting: identify differentials, control for legitimate factors, correct unjustified differentials.

### 7. Accommodation

- **ADA / state disability-law accommodation.** Interactive process, essential-function analysis, reasonable-accommodation identification, undue-hardship analysis, documentation. Do not default to "no" on accommodation requests. Do not require the employee to identify the accommodation; the interactive process is the employer's obligation.
- **PWFA pregnancy accommodation.** Same process, applied to pregnancy-related known limitations.
- **Title VII religious accommodation.** Same process post-*Groff*, with the substantial-cost undue-hardship standard.
- **Reasonable safety accommodation for domestic-violence victims** where state law requires.
- **State-law leave entitlements** (see chapter 08) — the corporation must not treat protected leave as a reason for adverse action.

### 8. Discipline, PIP, and termination

- **Documentation.** The corporation's ability to defend a discrimination claim depends heavily on contemporaneous documentation of the legitimate, non-discriminatory reason for the adverse action. The absence of documentation shifts the trial narrative toward the plaintiff.
- **Consistency.** Similarly-situated employees should receive similar treatment. A performance issue that leads to termination for a member of a protected class but not for a similarly-situated non-member is disparate-treatment evidence.
- **Termination review.** For every termination, before the corporation communicates the decision, run a checklist that asks: (a) what is the documented, legitimate, non-discriminatory reason; (b) has similar conduct been treated similarly for other employees; (c) has the employee recently engaged in any protected activity (complaint, accommodation request, protected leave) that could support a retaliation claim; (d) has the employee recently disclosed any protected characteristic (disability, pregnancy, religion, etc.); (e) is there anything in the employee's file (email, Slack, performance review) that would look bad in a deposition. See [mod-107](../mod-107-performance-promotion-and-offboarding/) for the depth playbook.
- **Retaliation.** *Any* adverse action taken because an employee engaged in protected activity — complaining about discrimination, requesting an accommodation, taking protected leave, opposing an unlawful practice, filing an EEOC charge — is unlawful retaliation. Retaliation claims are the fastest-growing category of EEOC charges and are often easier for plaintiffs to prove than the underlying discrimination claim.
- **Separation agreements and OWBPA compliance.** See [mod-102 chapter 07](../mod-102-founding-team-legal-architecture/07-founder-employment-relationship-and-departure.md) for the OWBPA formalities. Confidentiality and non-disparagement clauses must comply with NLRA *McLaren Macomb* — no clause may prohibit the employee from discussing wages, working conditions, or the terms of the separation with coworkers, or from filing a charge with the EEOC or a state FEPA. See [chapter 09](./09-arbitration-and-class-action-waivers.md) for the arbitration-clause interaction with EEOC charge-filing rights.

### 9. Offboarding and RIFs

- **Reduction-in-force analysis.** For any RIF that affects a meaningful population, run an adverse-impact analysis on the selection criteria (which employees are being laid off, by protected class) and document the legitimate, non-discriminatory basis for the selection (position elimination, performance ranking, seniority, etc.). RIFs that produce disparate impact on employees age 40+ are a common ADEA collective-action pattern.
- **OWBPA-compliant group release.** If two or more employees age 40+ are being separated in connection with an employment-termination program, the OWBPA group-release formalities apply — 45-day consideration period, written disclosure of the class members' ages and job titles, 7-day revocation, right to consult counsel, specific reference to ADEA rights.
- **State-law WARN and mini-WARN compliance.** 60-day notice under the federal WARN Act for covered mass layoffs; state mini-WARNs impose additional requirements (California, New York, Illinois, and others). Failure to notify creates back-pay-during-notice-period exposure. See [mod-107](../mod-107-performance-promotion-and-offboarding/).

## Enforcement channels

Discrimination exposure surfaces through several distinct channels:

- **The EEOC** — 300-day filing window (180 days if there is no state FEPA); investigates and issues a right-to-sue letter or, in rare cases, prosecutes directly. State FEPAs (California DFEH / CRD, New York DHR, Illinois DHR, etc.) may have longer filing windows (three years in California under FEHA).
- **The DOL OFCCP** — audits federal contractors for affirmative-action-plan compliance under Executive Order 11246 and its statutory analogs. Not relevant to most early-stage startups; relevant if the corporation ever accepts a federal contract or subcontract.
- **State civil-rights agencies** — parallel enforcement under state anti-discrimination statutes with often broader remedies than Title VII.
- **State attorneys general and city agencies** — California AG, NYC Commission on Human Rights, Los Angeles City Attorney; increasingly aggressive on employment-discrimination enforcement.
- **Private civil litigation** — after the EEOC or state FEPA issues a right-to-sue letter (or, for § 1981 and some state statutes, without any administrative exhaustion). Multi-plaintiff and class actions concentrate exposure.
- **The DOJ Immigrant and Employee Rights Section** — IRCA § 274B enforcement (citizenship-status, document-abuse).
- **The NLRB** — unfair-labor-practice charges for retaliation against protected concerted activity, overbroad workplace rules, or unlawful confidentiality / non-disparagement provisions.

## Concrete example: a hiring plan across California, New York City, and Illinois

Acme Robotics is filling a Senior Software Engineer role, remote, with the incumbent working from Chicago; the corporation has employees in San Francisco, New York City, and (with this hire) Chicago. The compliant framework:

- **Job description and posting.** Essential functions and required qualifications, no age or "young and energetic" language, salary range disclosed per California SB 1162, New York State S.9427-A, New York City Local Law 32, and Illinois HB 3129. Equal-opportunity statement lists the union of protected categories across the three jurisdictions.
- **Application form.** No demographic questions on the application. Voluntary EEO self-identification stored separately. No salary-history questions. No criminal-history questions (Illinois ban-the-box under 820 ILCS 75, NYC Fair Chance Act, California Fair Chance Act — all apply).
- **Interviewing.** Interviewers trained on impermissible questions. Structured scorecards mapped to essential functions.
- **Offer and background check.** After conditional offer, background check with FCRA and Illinois / NYC / California Fair Chance Act compliance — pre-adverse-action notice with a copy of the report and a summary of rights, five-business-day (or applicable jurisdictional) response window, individualised-assessment write-up if the corporation considers adverse action based on criminal history, post-adverse-action notice if the offer is rescinded.
- **Reasonable-accommodation posture.** Offer letter includes accommodation-request contact. Interactive process triggered on any request.
- **Pay-setting.** From the compensation architecture, within the posted range. No reference to prior salary. Documented pay-setting rationale filed with the hire record.
- **New-hire onboarding.** I-9 conducted uniformly, no document abuse. New-hire benefits election. Wage-and-hour classification (chapter 02) documented.

## What this chapter does not cover

- **Harassment prevention and complaint-response.** The Title VII / *Faragher* / *Ellerth* affirmative-defense architecture, harassment-prevention training (California AB 1825 / SB 1343 mandatory training; NY State and NYC mandatory training), complaint intake, investigation protocol, remedial action. Covered in [mod-104](../mod-104-hiring-onboarding-and-hr-operations/) and [mod-108](../mod-108-culture-employee-experience-and-dei/).
- **DEI program design.** Diversity-recruiting, employee-resource-groups, pay-equity program at depth, workforce-representation reporting. Covered in [mod-108](../mod-108-culture-employee-experience-and-dei/). Note the post-*SFFA v. Harvard* legal landscape on race-conscious employment programs and the increasing scrutiny of DEI initiatives from state attorneys general.
- **Federal-contractor OFCCP compliance and affirmative-action plans.** Only relevant if the corporation becomes a federal contractor. Covered in [mod-112](../mod-112-enterprise-risk-insurance-and-compliance/) if applicable.
- **AI-driven hiring tools and automated employment-decision systems.** New York City Local Law 144 (effective July 5, 2023) requires bias audits and specific disclosures for employer use of "automated employment decision tools" in hiring or promotion. The EEOC has issued guidance on Title VII implications of algorithmic decision-making. ## Summary

- The federal statutory core — Title VII, ADA, ADEA, PDA / PWFA / PUMP, EPA, GINA, USERRA, § 1981, NLRA — defines the floor of prohibited employment discrimination on the basis of race, color, religion, sex (including sexual orientation and gender identity post-*Bostock*), national origin, disability, age 40+, pregnancy, genetic information, uniformed-service status, and protected concerted activity.
- The state-and-city layer expands the protected-category list (sexual orientation, gender identity, marital status, source of income, criminal history, salary history, weight, height, hair texture, reproductive-health decisions, off-duty conduct, cannabis use, caste), lowers the employer-size threshold, and often provides uncapped compensatory / punitive damages and fee-shifting.
- Two liability theories — disparate treatment and disparate impact — govern almost every discrimination claim. Documentation, consistency, and job-relatedness are the corporation's three principal defenses.
- The framework applies across the employment lifecycle — job posting, application intake, interviewing, hiring, compensation-setting, performance and promotion, accommodation, discipline, termination, and RIFs. Each stage has recurring pitfalls (untrained interviewers, salary-history questions, background-check adverse-action procedural failures, poorly-drafted separation-agreement clauses).
- Retaliation is the fastest-growing enforcement category and often easier to prove than the underlying discrimination claim. Every adverse action against an employee who has recently engaged in protected activity requires a termination-review protocol.
- The corporation must maintain a running matrix of the federal, state, and city protected categories at every location where it employs anyone, and either default to the highest-common-denominator national policy or maintain per-location variants. See [chapter 08](./08-state-law-variance.md) for the state-variance treatment.
