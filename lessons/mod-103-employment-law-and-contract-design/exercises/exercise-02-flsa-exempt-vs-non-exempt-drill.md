# Exercise 02 — FLSA exempt vs. non-exempt drill

> Estimated time: **~3 hours** · Related chapter: [02 — FLSA exempt vs. non-exempt](../02-flsa-exempt-vs-non-exempt.md)

## Problem statement

Continuing from exercise 01, Acme Robotics has decided (following your worker-classification cleanup) that the following individuals will be W-2 employees. The Head of Finance now needs you to determine which of them are **FLSA-exempt** (paid a salary, not entitled to overtime) and which are **non-exempt** (entitled to overtime at 1.5× regular rate for hours over 40/week, plus applicable state daily-overtime rules and meal-and-rest premiums). The corporation's current payroll system codes everyone paid on a "salary" as exempt, which is the mistake pattern chapter 02 opens with.

You are producing (a) a per-employee salary-basis and duties-test analysis, (b) a corrected classification for each employee, (c) a quantified exposure memo for the misclassified population, and (d) a going-forward classification policy.

## Requirements

### Part A — Per-employee salary-basis and duties-test analysis

For each employee (1–14), produce an analysis entry (≤ half a page each) covering:

1. **Salary basis — federal.** Is the current annual compensation at or above the federal salary-basis threshold (as currently operative)? Cite the current threshold with a `<!-- needs-research -->` marker if you cannot verify the current operative federal figure per chapter 02.
2. **Salary basis — state.** For any employee in California, Washington, New York, Colorado, or Alaska, is the current compensation at or above the applicable state threshold (typically higher than federal)? Cite the applicable state figure with a `<!-- needs-research -->` marker if you cannot verify.
3. **Duties test — which exemption is being claimed and does it apply.** Executive (29 C.F.R. § 541.100), administrative (29 C.F.R. § 541.200), professional (§ 541.300 — learned or creative), computer-employee (§ 541.400 / 29 U.S.C. § 213(a)(17)), outside-sales (§ 541.500). Apply the specific test elements as in chapter 02. State the *primary duty* and the *facts* supporting or defeating exemption.
4. **Highly-compensated employee (HCE) test — 29 C.F.R. § 541.601.** If the employee's total annual compensation exceeds the HCE threshold, apply the shortened HCE duties test as an alternative pathway. Cite the current-year threshold with a `<!-- needs-research -->` marker.
5. **State duties-test variance.** California uses a "quantitative" duties test (more than 50% of time spent on exempt-qualifying tasks) rather than the federal "primary duty" totality. For any California employee, flag any way this changes the answer.
6. **Correct classification.** Exempt or non-exempt.
7. **Confidence level.** High / medium / low. Any employee whose exemption depends on a close reading of the primary-duty test or on a specific state variance should be low- or medium-confidence.

### Part B — Corrected classification and remediation

For each employee currently classified as exempt but who is actually non-exempt, produce a remediation entry:

1. **Reclassification decision.** Move from "exempt" to "non-exempt" in the payroll system.
2. **Hourly-rate calculation.** Compute the regular hourly rate (annual salary ÷ 52 weeks ÷ typical hours worked per week if you convert from salary; consult payroll and counsel on the exact methodology — the DOL fluctuating-workweek method or the standard 40-hour-week method are the two common approaches, each with different back-overtime consequences).
3. **Back-overtime exposure.** Compute for each misclassified employee the estimated back overtime for a two-year lookback (three years if wilful), at 1.5× regular rate for hours over 40/week. Include 100% liquidated damages under 29 U.S.C. § 216(b). Include attorneys' fees exposure.
4. **State-law additions.** California daily overtime, meal-and-rest premium pay, Cal. Lab. Code § 226 wage-statement penalties, § 203 waiting-time penalties, § 2802 expense reimbursement, PAGA per-pay-period penalties. New York wage-statement and § 191 weekly-pay-for-manual-workers implications if any employee arguably qualifies as a "manual worker" under NYDOL interpretation. Colorado COMPS Order additions.
5. **Communication and change-management plan.** How you propose to communicate the reclassification to each affected employee. This is a sensitive conversation because the reclassification is often perceived as a *downgrade* (loss of exempt status → time-tracking obligations, perceived reduction in autonomy), even though the underlying substantive right (overtime) increased. Address the framing.
6. **Systems changes.** Time-tracking obligation added, meal-and-rest-break tracking for California employees, on-call time accounting for Grace and others whose duties include on-call.

### Part C — Q4 hiring-plan gating on exempt vs. non-exempt

For the Q4 hires from exercise 01 (SWEs, Head of Marketing, video producer via 1099, EA, and the SDR pool as W-2 non-exempt per exercise 01), produce a classification recommendation before the offer letters go out:

- Two Senior Software Engineers.
- One Head of Marketing.
- One EA to CEO.
- Six Enterprise SDRs (W-2 per exercise 01's rejection of the 1099 structure).

The SDR pool in particular is where the corporation must decide up front — inside-sales roles are almost never eligible for the outside-sales exemption (which requires the employee to be "customarily and regularly engaged away from the employer's place or places of business"), and the administrative exemption is a stretch. Assume non-exempt and design the wage-hour compliance workflow accordingly.

### Part D — Going-forward classification policy

Produce a written classification policy for the corporation covering:

- **Default posture.** New hires are classified per a written analysis, not per the offer author's assumption. The default is "non-exempt unless the salary-basis and duties tests are affirmatively satisfied."
- **Which roles/levels are presumptively exempt** vs. presumptively non-exempt. Anchor to chapter 02's practical implications for engineers (over the HCE floor typically exempt via computer-employee or HCE pathway; below the HCE floor requires close analysis), PMs (administrative-exemption analysis), designers (creative-professional analysis), inside-sales (almost always non-exempt), account executives (outside-sales analysis requires actual outside-sales activity), office managers (typically not administrative-exempt), EAs (typically not administrative-exempt).
- **State-specific overlays.** California quantitative test, higher salary-basis thresholds in CA / WA / NY / CO, state daily-overtime rules.
- **Review cadence.** Annual re-classification review, plus trigger-based review on any role change, promotion, or compensation change.
- **Documentation.** The classification decision for every hire is documented in the personnel file with the salary-basis and duties-test analysis on the record.

### Part E — Design-choices memo

A 1–2 page memo covering:

- The framework you applied and the recurring pitfalls (the "salary alone equals exempt" mistake, the misuse of the administrative exemption, the misuse of the computer-employee exemption for junior engineers below the exempt threshold, the misuse of the executive exemption for "team leads" without hire/fire authority).
- The employees you found hardest to classify and why. Nika (team lead but no hire/fire authority), Jae (product manager — administrative or professional?), Kai (designer — creative-professional or non-exempt?), and Isla (customer-success manager — administrative or non-exempt?) are candidates.
- The state-of-the-art posture on the fluctuating-workweek method vs. standard-workweek method for back-overtime calculation. Note the practical trade-off and cite the DOL guidance you rely on.
- Any `<!-- needs-research -->` items you flagged and how the corporation should resolve them (current federal salary-basis threshold under the 2024 final rule and litigation status per chapter 02; current-year state salary-basis thresholds for CA / WA / NY / CO / Alaska; current HCE threshold).

## Starter guidance

- Chapter 02 is the primary reference for the two-part test (salary basis + duties), the five duties exemptions, and the practical implications for engineers, PMs, designers, inside-sales, and office managers.
- The single most common misclassification is treating a "salaried" employee as exempt without applying the duties test. Frame every analysis around the two-part test structure, not around the pay structure.
- The computer-employee exemption (29 C.F.R. § 541.400 / 29 U.S.C. § 213(a)(17)) is available for engineers, but there are salary-basis requirements: the employee must be paid at least the exempt-salary threshold OR at least $27.63/hour (a fixed statutory figure, not indexed). Verify with the current statute.
- The "team lead" exemption analysis under the executive test requires (a) two or more full-time subordinates and (b) hire/fire authority or particular weight in that decision. A team lead without hire/fire authority typically fails the executive test.
- Inside-sales reps are non-exempt unless a highly-atypical fact pattern places them within a different exemption. Do not attempt to squeeze them into the outside-sales exemption.
- The California "quantitative" duties test (more than 50% of time spent on exempt-qualifying activities) is stricter than the federal primary-duty analysis. Any California employee whose federal analysis is close on the primary-duty test likely fails California's more-than-50%-time test.
- Back-overtime calculation methodology (fluctuating workweek vs. standard workweek) is a legal question with material dollar implications; state the framework and flag the choice for legal review.
- Do not attempt to compute the exact current-year dollar exposure without confirming the current salary-basis threshold, minimum wage, and hourly-rate computation methodology. Flag with `<!-- needs-research -->` where the current-year figure is required.
