# 6. The non-compete landscape and its alternatives

> Non-competes are dying. The alternatives — narrow non-solicits, garden leave, trade-secret-protection carveouts — are the surviving default for most modern startup workforces.

## Motivation

For most of the twentieth century the non-compete was the default post-employment restrictive covenant in the US technology industry — a promise by the departing employee not to work for a competitor for a defined period (often 12 months, sometimes 24) in a defined geography (often "anywhere the corporation does business," which in a modern SaaS company means "everywhere"). Enforceability varied by state (California banned them long ago; most other states enforced them under a reasonableness test), but the *default drafting posture* in employment agreements was to include one.

That default has broken. Three things happened in the last decade:

1. **California's example spread.** Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 16600 has voided post-employment non-competes for over a century. The California technology industry grew into the world's largest technology cluster without enforceable non-competes; the empirical evidence that non-competes are necessary for technology-industry innovation weakened. Other states followed with bans (Minnesota 2023) or heightened restrictions (Massachusetts 2018, Washington 2020, D.C. 2022, New York 2023 pending, and others).
2. **The Federal Trade Commission tried to ban them nationally.** The FTC's 2024 final rule (16 C.F.R. Part 910) would have voided almost all post-employment non-competes nationwide. The rule was **blocked by a federal district court** in *Ryan LLC v. Federal Trade Commission* (N.D. Tex. 2024) and vacated on a nationwide basis, but the FTC's enforcement agenda around non-competes as unfair methods of competition under FTC Act § 5 remains active, and the ultimate legal status of a broad FTC rule is unresolved. <!-- needs-research: verify the current status of the FTC non-compete rule and Ryan LLC v. FTC after any 2025–2026 appellate proceedings, FTC re-rulemaking, or new legislation. -->
3. **Practitioner practice migrated.** Startup employment agreements increasingly omit non-competes for rank-and-file employees, retain them narrowly for senior executives with genuine access to trade secrets, and lean on non-solicits and trade-secret-protection language instead. Even in states where a non-compete would be enforceable, the *drafting default* has shifted toward "narrow non-solicit, robust trade-secret language, no non-compete unless there is a specific reason."

This chapter maps the current non-compete landscape (federal FTC rule, state bans, state restrictions), catalogues the surviving alternatives (non-solicits, garden leave, targeted trade-secret carveouts), and applies the framework to a hiring plan.

## The federal FTC rule — status and posture

**16 C.F.R. Part 910** ("Non-Compete Clause Rule") was issued in final form by the FTC on April 23, 2024, and would have (i) prohibited employers from entering new post-employment non-compete clauses with any workers, (ii) rendered existing non-compete clauses with workers other than senior executives unenforceable, and (iii) required employers to notify workers with existing non-competes (other than senior executives) that they were no longer enforceable. The rule was scheduled to take effect September 4, 2024.

**Litigation blocked the rule.** In *Ryan LLC v. Federal Trade Commission*, 3:24-cv-00986 (N.D. Tex.), the district court on August 20, 2024, held that the FTC lacked statutory authority under the FTC Act to promulgate a substantive rule addressing unfair methods of competition, and set aside the rule on a nationwide basis. Other cases in different circuits reached different results (a Pennsylvania court denied a preliminary injunction; a Florida court granted one). The FTC appealed. <!-- needs-research: current status of the FTC's appeal of Ryan LLC, any parallel or successor rulemaking, congressional non-compete legislation (e.g., the Workforce Mobility Act proposals), and state-level federal-preemption effects. -->

**Practical implications for a corporation today:**

- **The rule is not in effect.** State law governs non-compete enforceability.
- **The FTC's underlying enforcement posture remains.** The FTC has pursued and continues to pursue individual enforcement actions against non-competes it views as anti-competitive under FTC Act § 5, independent of the rule.
- **The regulatory direction is toward broader federal restriction over time.** Even if the FTC rule remains vacated, congressional action or subsequent rulemaking on narrower grounds is plausible. The corporation should not build a long-term workforce-management strategy that assumes broad non-compete enforceability.
- **Drafting posture.** Absent a specific enforceable-and-defensible use case, do not include non-competes in employment agreements. The friction with recruits, the risk of a state-law challenge, and the possibility of federal action outweigh the modest incremental protection a non-compete adds over a well-drafted non-solicit and trade-secret regime.

## State-level bans on post-employment non-competes

Four states categorically ban post-employment non-competes for employees:

- **California — Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 16600 et seq.** Non-competes are void, with narrow exceptions for the sale of a business (§ 16601), dissolution of a partnership (§ 16602), and dissolution of an LLC (§ 16602.5). California courts and the California legislature have repeatedly reinforced this rule.
  - **AB 1076 (2023) — Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 16600.1.** Requires employers to notify current and former employees (employed since January 1, 2022) in writing by February 14, 2024, that any non-compete clause in their agreement is void. Failure to notify creates a private right of action and civil penalty exposure.
  - **SB 699 (2023) — Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 16600.5.** Voids non-competes regardless of where or when signed if the employee works in California, and creates a private right of action for the employee to recover attorneys' fees for enforcement of the voided clause.
  - **Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 16600(b)** (as amended by AB 1076) — the void rule applies "regardless of whether the contract or provision was signed in California."
- **Minnesota — Minn. Stat. § 181.988** (effective July 1, 2023). Post-employment non-competes are void as against public policy for employees and independent contractors, with narrow exceptions for the sale-of-business context and dissolution.
- **Oklahoma — Okla. Stat. tit. 15, § 219A.** Non-competes are void, with a narrow sale-of-business exception under § 219 and § 219.1.
- **North Dakota — N.D. Cent. Code § 9-08-06.** Non-competes are void, with narrow sale-of-business and dissolution exceptions under § 9-08-06(1)–(2).

**Practical implication:** in any of these four states, any post-employment non-compete on a rank-and-file employee is void and cannot be enforced. Attempting to enforce one exposes the corporation to fee-shifting (California under § 16600.5) and to bad-employer reputation. Draft the employment agreements for these states without non-competes; if the corporation uses a national template, either strip the non-compete for these states or make the whole template non-compete-free.

## State-level restrictions

A larger set of states restricts (rather than bans) post-employment non-competes. The common restriction types include minimum-income thresholds, notice requirements, garden-leave (paid compensation during the restricted period) requirements, duration limits, and industry-specific bans (health care, low-wage workers, broadcast employees).

- **Massachusetts — G.L. c. 149 § 24L** (Non-Compete Agreement Act, effective October 1, 2018). Post-employment non-competes must (i) be in writing signed by both parties, (ii) state that the employee has the right to consult with counsel, (iii) not exceed 12 months (or 24 months if the employer proves the employee breached fiduciary duty or unlawfully took corporate property), (iv) be geographically reasonable, (v) be reasonable in scope of prohibited activities, (vi) be supported by garden-leave pay (at least 50% of the employee's highest base salary within the two years preceding termination) or other mutually-agreed consideration, and (vii) be presented to the employee at least 10 business days before start of employment (for new hires) or 10 business days before effective (for existing employees). Also void for certain worker categories: non-exempt employees, undergraduate / graduate students, employees under 18, and employees terminated without cause.
- **Washington — RCW 49.62** (effective January 1, 2020). Post-employment non-competes are enforceable only if (i) the employee's annual earnings exceed a defined threshold (adjusted annually — <!-- needs-research: current-year Washington earnings threshold under RCW 49.62.020, indexed to the consumer price index. -->), (ii) the non-compete is disclosed to the employee in writing no later than acceptance of the offer, (iii) the duration is 18 months or less (rebuttable presumption), and (iv) if the non-compete is enforced against a laid-off employee, the corporation pays the employee's base salary during the restricted period. Also imposes disclosure and duration limits on non-compete provisions in employee stock or bonus agreements.
- **Illinois — 820 ILCS 90 (Freedom to Work Act, amended 2022).** Non-competes void for employees earning under $75,000 per year (indexed); non-solicits void for employees earning under $45,000 per year (indexed). For higher-paid employees, non-competes must be supported by adequate consideration (typically two years of continued employment or additional consideration at signing) and must be reasonable in duration, geography, and activity scope. Employers must advise the employee to consult with counsel and provide the agreement 14 calendar days before start or execution.
- **New York — pending legislation.** The New York State legislature passed a broad non-compete ban in 2023 (S.3100A / A.1278B); Governor Hochul vetoed it in December 2023 citing carve-out concerns. Subsequent proposals have narrowed the scope (income thresholds, senior-executive carve-outs). <!-- needs-research: verify the current status of New York non-compete legislation and any legislative or gubernatorial action after 2024. -->
- **D.C. — D.C. Code § 32-581.01 et seq. (Ban on Non-Compete Agreements Amendment Act of 2020, as amended 2022).** Prohibits non-competes for employees earning less than a defined threshold (indexed — <!-- needs-research: current D.C. threshold. -->); permits them for higher-paid employees with disclosure and duration limits.
- **Colorado — C.R.S. § 8-2-113 (as amended by HB 22-1317, effective August 10, 2022).** Non-competes void except for the sale-of-business exception, for a "highly-compensated" employee (indexed threshold), and for narrow trade-secret protection with an income threshold. Notice and duration requirements apply. Injunctive-relief provisions are heavily restricted.
- **Virginia — Va. Code § 40.1-28.7:7 (2020).** Prohibits non-competes for "low-wage employees" (defined by reference to state average weekly wage).
- **Maryland — Md. Code Ann., Lab. & Empl. § 3-716 (as amended 2019 and 2024).** Prohibits non-competes for lower-wage employees.
- **Maine — 26 M.R.S. § 599-A (2019).** Requires notice, prohibits for employees under a threshold, and requires supporting consideration.
- **Rhode Island — R.I. Gen. Laws § 28-59 (2020).** Prohibits non-competes for non-exempt, low-wage, undergraduate / graduate student, and under-18 employees.
- **New Hampshire — RSA § 275:70-a.** Requires the employer to provide the non-compete before an offer of employment; prohibits for low-wage employees.
- **Oregon — ORS § 653.295 (2007, amended 2021).** Non-competes must satisfy several conditions: employee is exempt, salary above the applicable state median, notice before employment or on promotion, defined duration (12 months or shorter). Some non-competes are void by statute (broadcasting employees).

<!-- needs-research: many additional states (Nevada, Utah, Kentucky, Louisiana, Georgia, Texas, Florida, and others) have partial restrictions, industry-specific bans, or unique reasonableness formulations. Publish a current-state matrix before authoring a national non-compete policy. -->

**The composite picture.** In an increasing majority of US states, post-employment non-competes for rank-and-file employees are either void or subject to material restrictions (income thresholds, notice periods, garden-leave requirements, duration limits). In a shrinking majority of states, non-competes remain enforceable under a "reasonableness" test if drafted narrowly.

## Where narrow non-competes still work — and for whom

Even in the least-restrictive jurisdictions, courts apply a **reasonableness test** to non-competes: reasonable in duration, reasonable in geographic scope, reasonable in the activities restricted, and reasonable in relation to a legitimate business interest (typically trade secret protection, customer relationships, or significant investment in specialised training).

The reasonableness test consistently permits non-competes in a narrow set of situations:

- **Sale-of-business context.** The seller of a business signs a non-compete to protect the buyer's investment in the acquired goodwill and trade secrets. Enforceable in almost every state, including California (Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 16601). Common in acqui-hire and M&A contexts.
- **Executive-level roles with genuine trade-secret access.** A C-suite executive at a company whose competitive position depends on unpublished technology can sometimes be bound by a narrow non-compete (12-month, competitor-specific, geography defined by market) in states that enforce them. Increasingly rare in practice as garden-leave arrangements substitute for post-termination non-competes.
- **Highly-specialised roles where the corporation has invested substantially in training or in disclosing trade secrets.** Rare in modern software; more common in traditional manufacturing.

The population that historically got non-competes and increasingly does not:

- **Software engineers (all levels).** In California, void. In Washington and Colorado, restricted by income and duration. Elsewhere, enforceable in principle but out of fashion.
- **Sales representatives.** Most jurisdictions restrict; most modern practice uses customer non-solicits instead.
- **Product managers, designers, marketers, operators.** Same as engineers.
- **Low-wage workers, non-exempt employees, students, minors, healthcare workers.** Increasingly banned by statute in many states.

## The surviving alternatives — the practitioner defaults

### Non-solicitation of employees

A promise by the departing employee not to solicit the corporation's employees to leave for a defined period after termination (commonly 12 months). The interest protected is the corporation's investment in its workforce and the disruption cost of coordinated departures.

**State-law posture.** More broadly enforceable than non-competes, but California is a notable exception:

- **California.** Employee non-solicits are generally *unenforceable* against California employees after **AMN Healthcare, Inc. v. Aya Healthcare Services, Inc.**, 28 Cal. App. 5th 923 (2018), which held that a broad employee non-solicit was a restraint of trade under § 16600. Narrow trade-secret-based non-solicits ("shall not use the corporation's confidential recruiting information to solicit employees") may be defensible but the categorical bar on general employee non-solicits governs the drafting posture. Assume unenforceable.
- **Most other states.** Enforceable under a reasonableness test if narrowly drawn (12 months, specific employees the departing employee worked with, in a defined role). Illinois requires the employee to earn above the applicable threshold.

**Drafting posture:** include a narrow employee non-solicit (12 months, employees the departing employee worked with directly during the last 12 months of employment) for non-California employees; either omit or frame narrowly as a trade-secret-based clause for California employees.

### Non-solicitation of customers

A promise by the departing employee not to solicit the corporation's customers (or prospects) for a defined period after termination. The interest protected is the corporation's customer relationships and the sales investment.

**State-law posture.** Widely enforceable under a reasonableness test if narrowly drawn (12–24 months, customers the departing employee worked with during a defined lookback period, in a defined role). California enforces customer non-solicits *narrowly* — a clause targeted at customer-list trade secrets is enforceable, but a broad general customer non-solicit is likely void under § 16600 principles. The Ninth Circuit has treated this as a case-by-case matter turning on whether the clause protects actual trade secrets.

**Drafting posture:** include a narrow customer non-solicit (12–18 months, customers the departing employee worked with in the last 12 months of employment) with express trade-secret framing for California employees.

### Garden leave

A structure in which the corporation continues to pay the departing employee's base salary during a defined post-termination "garden leave" period, in exchange for the employee's agreement not to compete during that period. The employee remains technically employed (or on garden leave) and is bound by continuing employment obligations including confidentiality and non-solicitation.

**Advantages:**

- The employee is compensated during the restricted period, which addresses the affordability and unconscionability arguments courts use against non-competes.
- Massachusetts explicitly recognises garden-leave pay (50% of highest base salary) as an alternative form of consideration for a compliant non-compete under G.L. c. 149 § 24L.
- The corporation retains the employee's continuing loyalty obligations, including the fiduciary duty of an employee to the employer.
- Voluntary structure — the employee is more likely to accept a garden-leave arrangement (with pay) than a naked non-compete (without pay).

**Disadvantages:**

- Cost to the corporation — the departing employee is on the payroll during the garden-leave period.
- In California and other states with strong § 16600-style prohibitions, garden leave does not necessarily save a non-compete-in-substance; the employee may still be free to work for a competitor after the garden-leave period.

**Common use.** Executive-level roles, particularly in finance-adjacent industries (asset management, hedge funds, some regulated industries). Growing in software-industry senior-executive employment agreements as a substitute for post-termination non-competes.

### Trade-secret-protection carveouts and inevitable disclosure

The corporation's most durable post-termination restrictive covenant is not a "non-compete" at all — it is the confidentiality obligation of the PIIA ([chapter 04](./04-employee-piia.md)) plus the DTSA / state trade-secret law ([mod-102 chapter 05](../mod-102-founding-team-legal-architecture/05-dtsa-whistleblower-and-trade-secret-baseline.md)) enforced when a departing employee's new role would inevitably require disclosure of the corporation's trade secrets.

The **inevitable disclosure doctrine** — most-cited from *PepsiCo, Inc. v. Redmond*, 54 F.3d 1262 (7th Cir. 1995) — permits a former employer to obtain an injunction against a departing employee's new employment on the ground that the employee will inevitably rely on or disclose the former employer's trade secrets in the new role, even without a signed non-compete. The doctrine is recognised in some states (Illinois, some others) and rejected in others (California explicitly rejects it — *Whyte v. Schlage Lock Co.*, 101 Cal. App. 4th 1443 (2002)). Where recognised, it operates as a de facto narrow non-compete for a specific-competitor role.

**Practical drafting posture.**

- Robust confidentiality obligations in the PIIA (chapter 04).
- Robust trade-secret-baseline program (mod-102 chapter 05) — marked confidential information, controlled access, DTSA whistleblower notice.
- A narrow explicit "trade-secret protection" clause in the executive agreement that prohibits use or disclosure of trade secrets in any post-employment role — subject, of course, to the DTSA whistleblower and NLRA carveouts.
- Reliance on state trade-secret statutes (California's UTSA at Cal. Civ. Code § 3426; DTSA at 18 U.S.C. § 1836) and, where applicable, inevitable-disclosure doctrine.

This posture works in California, works in every other state, does not run afoul of state non-compete bans, and is not affected by any FTC rulemaking on non-competes (which explicitly carves out trade-secret protection). It is the corner-stone of the modern startup post-employment restrictive-covenant strategy.

### Additional structural alternatives

- **Notice periods.** An employment agreement that requires the employee to give notice (30 days, 60 days, 90 days) before resigning. The corporation retains the option to waive the notice period (accept the resignation immediately) or to pay through the notice period. Common at executive level.
- **Equity claw-back for competitive activity.** The equity grant agreement includes a claw-back or forfeiture provision if the employee competes with the corporation within a defined period. Enforceability turns on state contract law and, in California, on whether the forfeiture operates as a de facto non-compete (which would be voided under § 16600 as extended by SB 699 and AB 1076). Courts have been mixed. Not a reliable stand-alone tool.
- **Consulting-after-departure arrangements.** A voluntary consulting arrangement post-departure with negotiated payment and restrictive-covenant terms — sometimes used to bridge a senior executive's departure without a formal non-compete.

## The FTC rule's carve-outs and interaction with senior-executive exceptions

Even the FTC rule (were it in effect) explicitly carved out **senior executives** (defined at 16 C.F.R. § 910.1 as workers earning above a specified threshold — $151,164 per year — and holding a policy-making position). This carve-out matched practitioner practice: even under a broad ban, corporations retained the ability to enforce existing non-competes with genuinely senior executives with genuine trade-secret access. If the FTC re-issues a rule or Congress legislates, expect this carve-out to persist.

For a corporation authoring today: draft a senior-executive employment agreement that includes an enforceable-in-most-states non-compete (12 months, competitor-specific, defined geography, tied to base-salary garden-leave consideration) if state law permits, along with the surviving alternatives. For everyone else, rely on the alternatives.

## Concrete example: the restrictive-covenant pack for a multi-state workforce

Acme Robotics has a 60-person workforce across California, New York, Washington, Colorado, Massachusetts, and Illinois. The employment-agreement package includes:

- **All employees, all states — in the PIIA:**
  - **Confidentiality obligations** (indefinite for trade secrets, 3 years for other Confidential Information).
  - **DTSA whistleblower notice.**
  - **NLRA / SEC-whistleblower carveout.**
  - **State-law invention-assignment carveouts** as applicable.
  - **Return of property on termination.**
  - **Trailing invention assignment** (6 months post-termination, narrow to inventions using confidential information or substantially conceived during service).
- **All non-California employees — in the PIIA:**
  - **Employee non-solicit** — 12 months post-termination, employees the departing employee worked with in the last 12 months of employment.
  - **Customer non-solicit** — 12 months post-termination, customers the departing employee worked with in the last 12 months of employment.
- **California employees — in the PIIA:**
  - **No general employee non-solicit** (unenforceable per *AMN Healthcare*).
  - **Narrow customer non-solicit** framed as trade-secret protection with respect to customer-list confidential information.
- **All employees — no post-employment non-compete.** The default drafting posture.
- **Senior executives (VP-level and above) — in the executive employment agreement (a separate document):**
  - **60-day notice-of-resignation obligation** with garden-leave pay for the notice period.
  - **12-month post-termination non-compete** in enforceable states (Massachusetts, Illinois, New York, others), tied to garden-leave pay covering the restricted period.
  - **No post-termination non-compete for California-based executives** (§ 16600 governs; SB 699 voids non-competes signed elsewhere and enforced against California employees).
  - **Executive-level customer non-solicit** — 12–18 months post-termination, defined customers.
  - **Board-service and outside-activity disclosure and approval framework.**
- **Sale-of-business context (M&A of Acme):** the transaction documents include seller / founder non-competes tied to the sale under Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 16601 (for California sellers) and the equivalent state statutes. These are enforceable and standard.

The pack survives the current state-law and FTC-rule landscape. It leans on the alternatives (confidentiality, trade-secret law, non-solicits, garden leave) that continue to work. It reserves the narrow non-compete for the narrow senior-executive population where state law permits and where consideration (garden-leave pay) supports enforcement.

## The 2024 California notice obligation (AB 1076): do it

**Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 16600.1** (added by AB 1076, effective January 1, 2024) requires California employers to notify current and former employees (employed since January 1, 2022) by **February 14, 2024**, in writing individualised to the employee, that any non-compete clause in their agreement is void. Failure to notify is a violation of Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200 (unfair competition), triggering statutory penalties and creating a private right of action.

For any corporation that employed anyone in California under an agreement containing a non-compete (or an ambiguous "post-employment competition" restriction) at any time from January 1, 2022, forward, the notice obligation was retrospective. If the corporation missed the February 14, 2024, deadline, the compliance posture is to send the notice now, on the theory that late compliance is better than no compliance. Consult counsel for a formulation.

## Summary

- Non-competes are dying. The FTC's 2024 rule was blocked in federal court but the direction of federal regulation and state legislation is toward broader restriction over time. California, Minnesota, Oklahoma, and North Dakota categorically ban non-competes for employees; Massachusetts, Washington, Colorado, D.C., Illinois, New York (pending), and many others impose income thresholds, notice periods, duration limits, and garden-leave requirements.
- California's § 16600 has always voided non-competes. AB 1076 (2023) added a notice obligation. SB 699 (2023) voided non-competes signed anywhere and enforced against California employees, and provides fee-shifting to the employee.
- The surviving practitioner defaults for post-termination restrictive covenants:
  - **Robust confidentiality and trade-secret protection** under the PIIA and DTSA / state trade-secret statutes.
  - **Narrow non-solicits** of employees and customers, tailored to the specific employees or customers the departing worker had direct contact with in a defined lookback period. California enforces employee non-solicits narrowly if at all (post-*AMN Healthcare*).
  - **Garden leave** — continued base-salary payments during a post-termination non-work period, with continuing employment obligations. Recognised in Massachusetts as an alternative form of non-compete consideration; growing in senior-executive agreements generally.
  - **Notice-of-resignation periods** — 30 / 60 / 90 days at executive level.
  - **Trade-secret-protection clauses** — narrow and durable across all state-law regimes and any plausible federal rulemaking.
- Non-competes remain narrowly viable for senior executives with genuine trade-secret access in states where they are enforceable, and always viable for sale-of-business transactions (Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 16601 and equivalents).
- Draft the restrictive-covenant pack from the surviving alternatives outward. Non-competes are the exception, not the default.
