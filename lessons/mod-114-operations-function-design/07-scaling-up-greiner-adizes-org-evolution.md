# 7. Scaling Up, Greiner, Adizes — the org-evolution canon

> The operating rhythm authored in [chapter 06](./06-operating-cadence-and-okrs-rhythm.md) is the *what*. The reason that rhythm has to be installed — and the predictable organisational crises it is designed to run against — is the *why*. Four bodies of work (Greiner, Adizes, Scaling Up / Rockefeller Habits, and the OKRs canon) describe the same company at different altitudes, and a COO / CoS who has read them recognises the next crisis one stage before it arrives.

## Motivation

Scale-ups do not fail at random. They fail in predictable patterns at predictable sizes. The 20-person company that cannot survive its first functional-leadership hire; the 75-person company whose founder refuses to delegate line authority; the 250-person company whose managers cannot get a decision through the newly-formed layer above them; the 1,000-person company that drowns in process its own managers introduced to tame the chaos of 250. These are not idiosyncratic. They are the stages of an evolutionary trajectory first mapped in the 1970s by Larry Greiner and extended through the 1980s and 1990s by Ichak Adizes, and they continue to describe modern software-company trajectories closely.

A COO / CoS whose job is to *run the company* needs to know which stage the company is in and which crisis is next, because the operating decisions that work at one stage break at the next. Weekly 1:1s that worked at 30 people become unmanageable at 300. Direct CEO decisions that worked at 50 people choke the executive team at 150. Explicit process that saved the company at 300 strangles it at 1,000. Without a stage model, the COO defends the last stage's operating pattern against a company that has moved on.

This chapter is the stage model. It covers Greiner's growth-and-crisis model, Adizes's corporate lifecycle, Verne Harnish's Scaling Up / Rockefeller Habits, the OKRs canon within that frame, and the practical rhythm (weekly / monthly / quarterly / annual) a COO installs to run against the predictable crises. It is the lens for the specific operating-cadence design in [chapter 06](./06-operating-cadence-and-okrs-rhythm.md) and the executive-team rhythm in [chapter 09](./09-executive-team-operating-rhythm.md).

## Greiner's growth model

Larry Greiner's *Evolution and Revolution as Organizations Grow* (first published in the *Harvard Business Review* in 1972, revised in 1998) identified five (later six) evolutionary stages of organisational growth, each followed by a predictable crisis that triggers the transition to the next stage. The model is almost 50 years old and remains the single most-cited frame for predictable organisational-growth failure patterns.

The five phases Greiner named — each paired with the crisis that ends it:

### Phase 1 — Growth through creativity → Leadership crisis

The founding stage. Small team, informal communication, founder as the decision-maker on everything. Growth is driven by product-market creativity. Everyone does everything. There is no org chart because there is no org.

**The leadership crisis.** As the company grows past the point where the founder can personally direct every function (typically 20–40 employees for a venture-backed software company; the exact number varies by product complexity and operating intensity), the informal pattern breaks. The founder is a bottleneck on hundreds of decisions. Specialist knowledge is needed — a Head of Engineering, a Head of Sales, a Head of People — that the founder does not have. The crisis forces the hiring of professional management.

**What this looks like at a scale-up:** Series A to early Series B. The founder is still answering every customer email and interviewing every candidate. The first VP hires are landing but not yet trusted with real decision authority. The CEO is running on 18-hour days.

**Resolution:** Hire the first functional VPs; delegate decision authority; introduce the first formal operating processes.

### Phase 2 — Growth through direction → Autonomy crisis

With functional management in place, the company grows under centralised direction. The CEO and the executive team set direction; the functions execute. Formal budgets, hierarchical reporting, and functional specialisation appear.

**The autonomy crisis.** The specialists hired to resolve the leadership crisis — now running Sales, Engineering, Product, People — chafe against the central direction. They know their functions better than the CEO does. Mid-level managers feel constrained by rules authored by executives who do not operate at the mid-level. The crisis forces delegation of operating authority to functional and middle management.

**What this looks like at a scale-up:** Mid Series B to Series C. The VP Sales wants to set compensation plans without CFO sign-off on every element. The VP Engineering wants to make architecture decisions without the CTO relitigating them. The CFO wants to run close without the CEO reviewing the trial balance.

**Resolution:** Delegate authority downward; introduce profit-and-loss ownership at the function or business-unit level; move the CEO out of operating detail.

### Phase 3 — Growth through delegation → Control crisis

With authority delegated, the business grows through autonomous functional and business-unit execution. Managers run their areas with real decision rights. The CEO focuses on strategy and capital, not operations.

**The control crisis.** With delegation comes divergence. Each business unit optimises for its own measures. Cross-unit coordination fails. The CEO loses visibility into what is happening operationally. Resource allocation across units becomes contentious because the units are now autonomous enough to defend their own interests. The crisis forces re-centralisation of coordinating mechanisms.

**What this looks like at a scale-up:** Series C to growth. Multiple product lines; multiple regions; multiple functions with their own operating models that no longer coordinate. The weekly ops review runs on data that disagrees across functions. The quarterly OKRs cascade is formal but functionally decoupled from execution.

**Resolution:** Introduce coordinating mechanisms — formal planning cycles, cross-functional councils, shared services (People, Finance, IT, Legal) that enforce consistency across units. This is where **BizOps** (chapter 01) becomes a dedicated function and where the **operating cadence** (chapter 06) becomes a formally enforced discipline.

### Phase 4 — Growth through coordination → Red-tape crisis

With coordinating mechanisms in place, the business grows through coordinated, cross-unit execution. Shared services deliver scale. Formal planning cycles align resources. Governance structures manage trade-offs.

**The red-tape crisis.** The coordinating mechanisms become the problem. Decisions require sign-off from multiple committees. Shared services introduce queueing delays. Formal planning cycles consume more time than they inform. Managers who used to decide now wait for the planning cycle. The crisis forces de-formalisation and re-emphasis on trust and problem-solving.

**What this looks like at a scale-up:** Late growth stage to pre-IPO. The organisation is 1,000–5,000+ employees. Running a cross-functional programme requires six approval signatures. The product roadmap is set 18 months in advance in a planning cycle that cannot absorb market change. Decisions migrate upward because no one at the operating level has clear authority.

**Resolution:** Simplify processes; push authority downward again; emphasise cross-functional problem-solving over formal coordination; introduce teaming models that cut across the formal hierarchy.

### Phase 5 — Growth through collaboration → Growth-crisis (and Greiner's Phase 6 — Growth through alliances / networks)

In Greiner's model, the company grows through collaboration — matrixed teams, cross-functional problem-solving, strategic alliances. Greiner later added a sixth phase of growth through external alliances / networks, acknowledging that very large companies grow through M&A and partnership structures as much as organic hiring.

**What this looks like:** Enterprise-scale companies. Beyond the scope of most scale-up operating decisions, but worth knowing because the growth stage has to design for the enterprise stage.

### How Greiner's model informs the COO's operating decisions

- **Recognise the current phase.** The operating patterns that worked in the prior phase will break in the current one. The leadership crisis's resolution (first VPs) is not the autonomy crisis's resolution (delegation of operating authority).
- **Anticipate the next crisis.** A company in Phase 2 (growth through direction) is 12–24 months from an autonomy crisis. The COO's job is to prepare for it — authority-delegation design, management-development programme, operating-metric model that supports delegation.
- **Match the operating-cadence depth to the phase.** Lightweight cadence suffices in Phase 1 and 2. Phase 3 needs the structured four-layer cadence of [chapter 06](./06-operating-cadence-and-okrs-rhythm.md). Phase 4 risks collapsing under its own coordination weight — the COO is a brake on process overshoot.

## Adizes's corporate lifecycle

Ichak Adizes's *Managing Corporate Lifecycles* (first published 1988, revised 1999 and in later editions) describes the organisation as a living entity moving through developmental stages — Courtship, Infancy, Go-Go, Adolescence, Prime, Stable, Aristocracy, Early Bureaucracy, Bureaucracy, Death. Each stage has characteristic strengths, characteristic pathologies, and characteristic transitions. The model has been popular with founder-CEOs and operating executives because its vocabulary — "Go-Go," "Prime," "Aristocracy" — gives language to patterns most operators recognise.

### The stages (abbreviated)

- **Courtship / Infancy.** Pre-product and early product. Founder-driven, action-biased, under-processed. Many organisations die in Infancy for lack of commercial traction.
- **Go-Go.** First real growth. Everything is working; the founder-CEO cannot stop saying yes to new opportunities; the organisation is dizzy with success and lacks the systems to sustain it. Founder's trap at the top end.
- **Adolescence.** The transition from founder-dominated to professionally-managed. Painful; often conflict-ridden; many organisations stall or regress here.
- **Prime.** The target state. The organisation balances flexibility and control; growth is systematic; the organisation is self-sustaining. Adizes's framing is that Prime is not a stage you arrive at and stay in — it is a stage you maintain through active discipline.
- **Stable.** Prime's slow decline. The organisation still performs but is losing its growth edge. Fewer new initiatives; more defence of existing ones.
- **Aristocracy.** The decline continues. The organisation becomes risk-averse; the executive dress code and office décor becomes more important than the product; growth is through M&A rather than organic. Classic enterprise pathology.
- **Early Bureaucracy / Bureaucracy / Death.** Terminal stages. Internal politics consume the organisation; the market has moved on; the organisation lives on past products until it finally dies or is acquired.

### How Adizes informs the COO's operating decisions

The practical lesson from Adizes is that **Prime is a maintained state, not a destination.** The forces that pull an organisation out of Prime toward Aristocracy — risk aversion, status consciousness, process-worship — are present at every stage, and the COO's job is to counteract them.

Specifically:

- **Watch for the Go-Go pathologies.** Over-committing to new initiatives, undisciplined hiring, systems bought under pressure, cross-functional execution breaking under volume. The scale-up at 100 employees that is winning in the market but losing operationally is in late Go-Go. The resolution is Adolescence — professional management, with the pain that comes with it.
- **Design the Adolescence transition explicitly.** The founder-to-professional-management transition is where many scale-ups break — founders who cannot accept the shift (resolving to the Horowitz "wartime CEO" trap of over-personalising decisions) or executive teams that cannot absorb the founder's past operating authority. The COO / CoS is often the hinge figure in this transition.
- **Hold the line against Aristocratic drift.** As the company matures, the pressure to introduce ever-more process, ever-more hierarchy, ever-more defensive governance is constant. The COO's job is to introduce the *necessary* process without introducing the *unnecessary* process. Chapter 06's rhythm is the necessary process; chapter 05's procurement matrix is the necessary process; adding a layer of VPs between a Director and a C-suite executive to "preserve span of control" at 400 employees is the unnecessary process.

## Scaling Up / Rockefeller Habits

Verne Harnish's *Scaling Up: How a Few Companies Make It... and Why the Rest Don't* (2014; a successor to *Mastering the Rockefeller Habits*, 2002) is the practitioner playbook most widely adopted by founder-led private companies in the mid-market. It is not a formal management-science model; it is a packaged operating system that compiles practices from the Grove / Doerr / Harnish / Collins canon into a specific set of habits, meetings, and documents.

### The Four Decisions

Scaling Up frames every operating decision as one of four categories — the **Four Decisions** — that the executive team has to get right simultaneously:

1. **People.** Hiring the right executives, developing them, holding them accountable. Includes the "A Players" discipline (hire top-decile talent and remove non-performers).
2. **Strategy.** The 3HAG (3-year highly-achievable goal) / 10-year BHAG; the core customer; the brand promise; the differentiators; the growth drivers.
3. **Execution.** The operating rhythm (daily huddle, weekly meeting, monthly meeting, quarterly meeting, annual meeting), the KPIs, the priority discipline (quarterly / annual priorities).
4. **Cash.** The cash flow story; the cash cycle; the funding plan; the operating cash discipline.

Harnish's claim is that all four have to be present and running. A company excellent at People and Strategy but weak on Execution fails to deliver on its potential; a company excellent at Execution but weak on Cash runs out of money; and so on.

### The Rockefeller Habits (the 10 habits)

Harnish's *Mastering the Rockefeller Habits* enumerates ten specific habits the executive team adopts. The practical ones for a scale-up:

- **An executive team that is healthy and aligned** (chapter 09 territory).
- **Everyone is aligned with the Top 5 (annual) and Top 1 (quarterly) priorities.**
- **Communication rhythm is established and information moves through the organisation quickly and accurately** (chapter 06 territory).
- **Every facet of the organisation has a person assigned with accountability for ensuring goals are met.**
- **Ongoing employee input is collected to identify obstacles and opportunities.**
- **Reporting and analysis of customer feedback data is as frequent and accurate as reporting and analysis of financial data.**
- **Core values and purpose are "alive" in the organisation.**
- **Employees can articulate the company's key components of strategy.**
- **All employees can answer quantitatively whether they had a good day or week** (through a shared KPI or scorecard).
- **The company's plans and performance are visible to everyone.**

### The Scaling Up meeting rhythm

Scaling Up's cadence — closely related to the four-layer cadence of chapter 06 — adds a **daily huddle** at the top:

- **Daily huddle.** 15 minutes, standing, same time every day. Each person: top priority for the day; the one metric; blockers.
- **Weekly meeting.** 60–90 minutes. KPI review, priority progress, decisions.
- **Monthly meeting.** 2–4 hours. Strategic topic, operating review, learning.
- **Quarterly meeting.** 1–2 days. Priority setting, team health, strategic review.
- **Annual meeting.** 2–3 days. Strategic planning, team-building, annual-plan setting.

The daily huddle is distinctive. It works at some companies (particularly operations-heavy or sales-heavy teams where daily tempo is real) and feels oppressive at others (particularly engineering-heavy teams where deep-focus days are the primary unit of value). Scale-ups adopt or discard the daily huddle based on culture-fit.

### How Scaling Up informs the COO's operating decisions

Scaling Up gives the COO a **packaged option** — a complete operating system to install, with templates, meetings, and documents (the "One-Page Strategic Plan" is the most-recognisable artifact). For a COO landing in a founder-led company that has no operating rhythm, Scaling Up is often the fastest path to installing one. The alternative is to compose the rhythm from first principles against the specific company's needs — more customised, more time-consuming.

## The OKRs canon within the stage model

OKRs — introduced in [chapter 06](./06-operating-cadence-and-okrs-rhythm.md) — are a quarterly goal-setting discipline that fits naturally into the Phase 3 (growth through delegation) and Phase 4 (growth through coordination) stages of Greiner. They are an answer to the control crisis: a lightweight coordinating mechanism that allows delegated authority while maintaining alignment.

The canon:

- **Andrew Grove / Intel.** The practice originated as "iMBOs" at Intel under Andy Grove. *High Output Management* (1983) codifies the broader management philosophy; the specific OKRs practice is less-well-documented in Grove's own writing and lives more in the oral tradition of Intel alumni.
- **John Doerr / Google.** Grove's practice was transmitted to Doerr (a Grove-era Intel executive turned Kleiner Perkins partner), and Doerr introduced it to Google in 1999. Doerr's *Measure What Matters* (2017) is the primary modern canonical text.
- **Christina Wodtke.** *Radical Focus* (2016, revised 2021) and *The Team That Managed Itself* (2019) are the current practitioner reference. Wodtke's framing — "cadence of commitment," "connected not cascaded," the OKR as "a focusing device" — is more usable at scale-up than Doerr's enterprise-case-studies framing.

### Where OKRs fit the stage model

- **Phase 1–2 companies (pre-30 employees).** OKRs are usually too much structure for too little coordination need. A quarterly priority list in a shared document suffices.
- **Phase 2–3 transition (30–150 employees).** OKRs become the right structure. Chapter 06's quarterly cycle applies.
- **Phase 3–4 (150–1,000 employees).** OKRs are the primary coordinating mechanism across the delegated authority of functions. Mid-quarter check-in and quarterly retrospective are non-negotiable.
- **Phase 4+ (1,000+ employees).** OKRs continue, but risk becoming formalistic. The red-tape crisis affects OKRs too — a 300-line OKR document that nobody reads is Phase 4 pathology.

### The common failure modes of OKRs mapped to the stage model

- **Setting OKRs before the company has a Phase 2 operating pattern.** OKRs without a coordinating need are decorative. Fix: wait for the autonomy crisis before formalising OKRs.
- **Cascading OKRs mechanically through five layers.** Phase 3 pathology — every manager has OKRs that are rote derivations of the layer above. Fix: Wodtke's "connected" model.
- **OKRs becoming budget targets.** The CFO starts treating OKRs as commitments and penalises misses. OKRs collapse into KPIs and lose their stretch function. Fix: formally separate OKRs (stretch) from the operating plan (commitment).
- **OKRs continuing in Phase 4 beyond their usefulness.** The company has become process-heavy; OKRs are one more process; nobody engages. Fix: simplify or replace with a lighter alternative (quarterly priorities + KPIs may suffice).

## The practical operating rhythm — synthesising the canon

The three bodies of work (Greiner, Adizes, Scaling Up) agree in substance: a scaling company needs a layered operating rhythm, with cross-functional coordinating mechanisms, that it maintains through active discipline against the natural drift toward either chaos (early stage) or bureaucracy (late stage).

The practical COO / CoS synthesis — the rhythm as installed in a typical scale-up:

### Stage-adjusted rhythm

- **Pre-Phase 2 (fewer than ~30 employees).** CEO's weekly staff meeting + a monthly operating review. OKRs not yet formalised. Annual planning is a quick exercise in December. No daily huddle. The CEO still runs operating detail personally.
- **Phase 2–3 transition (~30–150 employees).** The chapter 06 four-layer cadence lands: weekly ops review, monthly business review, quarterly OKRs, annual planning. The CoS is hired specifically to run this rhythm. The first VPs have real operating authority within their functions.
- **Phase 3 proper (~150–500 employees).** The rhythm is institutionalised. BizOps owns the KPI dashboard; the CoS owns the operating cadence; the COO (if hired) chairs the E-team weekly. Cross-functional coordination requires formal structures — programme reviews, pricing councils, hiring councils.
- **Phase 4 (~500–2,000 employees).** The rhythm matures. The risk is overshoot — too many meetings, too many committees, too much process. The COO's active job is to prune — kill the meetings that have outlived their purpose, flatten the governance that has accreted.
- **Phase 4+ (2,000+ employees).** Beyond scale-up territory. The rhythm resembles public-company operating rhythm; the CoS / COO roles resemble public-company Chief of Staff and President roles. New dynamics (investor relations, public-company governance) enter the picture.

### What stays constant across stages

- **The four altitude layers.** Daily (if adopted) / Weekly / Monthly / Quarterly / Annual. The content changes; the fact of the layers does not.
- **A single canonical dashboard.** One source of truth for the metrics the executive team runs on. Chapter 06.
- **A follow-through discipline.** Every decision has an owner and a date. Nothing drops.
- **A pre-read discipline.** Meetings run on pre-reads, not on-the-fly presentation. Pre-reads distributed 24 hours in advance.
- **A retrospective discipline.** Every operating cycle (quarter, year) ends with an honest retrospective. The retrospective is for *learning*, not for blame.

### What changes across stages

- **Who chairs.** The CEO chairs everything at Phase 1. The CoS / COO chairs the operating cadence at Phase 3. Function heads chair functional cadence at Phase 4.
- **Depth of formalism.** Phase 2's weekly meeting is a conversation. Phase 4's weekly meeting is a structured review with pre-reads, dashboards, decision-logs, and follow-through tracking.
- **Centralisation vs. delegation.** Phase 1 is centralised at the CEO. Phase 2 is centralised at the executive team. Phase 3 is delegated to function heads. Phase 4 is matrixed across functions and business units.
- **The operating risks.** Phase 1 risk is chaos; Phase 4 risk is bureaucracy. The operating cadence's design counter-weights whichever risk is dominant at the current stage.

## Concrete example — Series-B to Series-D rhythm evolution

A representative trajectory across three stages:

### Series B, 80 employees — the rhythm installs

**Stage.** Phase 2 (growth through direction). Autonomy crisis emerging — the VP Sales and VP Engineering are chafing at CEO decision overreach.

**Rhythm installed.**
- Weekly: Monday 9am E-team meeting, 60 minutes. CoS chairs.
- Monthly: 2nd Thursday MBR, 2 hours. Pre-close for the first six months; moves to post-close as the Finance team matures.
- Quarterly: Half-day OKRs setting; half-day OKRs retrospective. Simple format, 3 Objectives per function.
- Annual: Two-day E-team offsite in early November for the following year.

**Coordination.** No formal cross-functional councils yet. Cross-functional work handled directly between function heads with the CoS mediating.

### Series C, 300 employees — the rhythm matures

**Stage.** Phase 3 (growth through delegation). Control crisis emerging — multiple business lines; multiple regions; data disagreements across functions.

**Rhythm matured.**
- Weekly: Monday 9am E-team meeting, 90 minutes. New COO chairs. CoS runs the agenda prep and action-item tracking.
- Weekly cross-function: Wednesday 3pm BizOps / Analytics / Finance meeting to align the dashboard.
- Monthly: 2nd Thursday MBR, 2.5 hours. Rotating departmental deep-dives.
- Quarterly: Full-day OKRs setting; full-day retrospective. 3–5 Objectives per function; cascaded to team level.
- Mid-quarter check-in: Half-day session in week 6 of each quarter.
- Annual: Three-day E-team offsite with external facilitator. Strategy memo pre-read, financial plan authored before offsite.

**Coordination.** Formal cross-functional councils: pricing council (CRO, CFO, Product, Finance); hiring council (CPO, each function head); systems council (COO, CTO, CFO, Head of Business Systems).

### Series D / growth, 900 employees — the rhythm risks overshoot

**Stage.** Phase 3-to-4 transition (coordination → red-tape risk emerging). Multiple product lines; multi-region; dense executive team.

**Rhythm active-maintenance.**
- Weekly E-team meeting at 90 minutes. CoS and COO actively prune — any topic that could be handled offline moves offline.
- Monthly MBR at 2.5 hours. Deep-dive rotation covers each function on a 3-month rotation plus strategic-topic.
- Quarterly OKRs; the COO actively simplifies — reduces number of Objectives, reduces nesting of KRs, kills any OKR that is "always on" and should be a KPI.
- Annual offsite remains three days. The strategic-pre-read remains CEO-authored, with CoS editorial.
- Governance councils that have outlived their purpose get sunset. The pricing council becomes an email thread. The hiring council becomes a monthly meeting rather than weekly.

**Coordination risk.** The red-tape crisis is actively defended against. Any new committee has to argue for itself; any existing committee has to re-justify annually; the operating cadence is formally reviewed each year with "what should we kill?" as the opening question.

## Common failure modes

- **Treating stage models as prescriptive.** The CEO reads *Scaling Up* and installs the full ten-habit programme on a 40-person company. The company drowns in process. Fix: match the rhythm's depth to the stage.
- **Missing the next crisis.** A Phase 2 company is 12 months from an autonomy crisis; the CEO does not plan for it; the first VP exits over micromanagement. Fix: anticipate the crisis one stage ahead.
- **Over-formalising the Founder-to-Adolescence transition.** The CEO hires a slate of professional executives and installs a dense operating cadence overnight. The founder-era rhythm (fast, informal) is destroyed before the new rhythm is adopted. Fix: install incrementally; keep the founder's culture while adding the structure.
- **Letting Phase 4 process accrete.** The company hits Phase 4; each new initiative adds a committee; nobody kills the old committees. Within 18 months the operating cadence is unreadable. Fix: annual rhythm review with explicit "what gets killed" discipline.
- **Mistaking OKRs for the whole rhythm.** The company adopts OKRs; the executive team believes OKRs are the operating system; the weekly / monthly layer never materialises. Fix: OKRs are *one* layer of a four-layer rhythm.
- **Reading only one book.** The CEO reads *Scaling Up* and declares victory. The CEO reads only *Measure What Matters* and installs OKRs mechanically. The CEO reads only *The Hard Thing About Hard Things* and treats every stage as a wartime moment. Fix: the canon is a mosaic; no single text is the operating system.

## Summary

- Four bodies of work describe the same scaling company — **Greiner's phases** (creativity → direction → delegation → coordination → collaboration), **Adizes's lifecycle** (Infancy → Go-Go → Adolescence → Prime → Aristocracy → Bureaucracy), **Scaling Up / Rockefeller Habits** (People, Strategy, Execution, Cash + the daily / weekly / monthly / quarterly / annual rhythm), and the **OKRs canon** (Grove / Doerr / Wodtke).
- Each body of work frames the same practical reality: a scaling company passes through predictable stages with predictable crises, and the operating patterns that worked in one stage break in the next.
- **Greiner's crises** — leadership → autonomy → control → red-tape — map to the operating decisions the COO / CoS makes. The autonomy crisis forces delegation; the control crisis forces coordination; the red-tape crisis forces simplification.
- **Adizes's Prime is a maintained state, not a destination.** The forces that drag an organisation toward Aristocracy are constant; the COO's job is to counteract them with discipline.
- **Scaling Up / Rockefeller Habits** packages the operating rhythm into a complete installable system. Appropriate for a landing COO at a founder-led company that has no operating rhythm; customised rhythm is appropriate for companies that have evolved their own.
- **OKRs** fit the Phase 2-to-3 transition and the Phase 3 proper. They are a coordinating mechanism, not a budget-target system; the Wodtke "connected" model usually fits scale-ups better than mechanical cascading.
- The **practical rhythm** stays four-layered (weekly / monthly / quarterly / annual, with daily optional). The content and depth change with stage. The disciplines that stay constant: single canonical dashboard, follow-through on decisions, pre-read discipline, retrospective discipline.
