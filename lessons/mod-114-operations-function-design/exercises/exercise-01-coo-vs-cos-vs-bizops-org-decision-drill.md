# Exercise 01 — COO vs. CoS vs. BizOps org decision drill

> Estimated time: **~8 hours** · Related chapter: [01 — Designing the operations function](../01-coo-vs-cos-and-bizops-org-design.md)

## Problem statement

Three Series-A / Series-B scale-ups are each about to make the same consequential structural call — "do we hire a Chief of Staff, a COO, or both?" — against three very different CEO profiles, three very different executive-team shapes, and three very different fundraising-narrative pressures. Each CEO has an informal preference; each lead investor has an opinion; each has a candidate pipeline that includes both CoS-type and COO-type profiles. All three CEOs have asked (independently, through the same board advisor who has engaged you) for an outside operating-perspective memo that applies the chapter 01 framework explicitly, reaches a specific recommendation, and defends that recommendation against the obvious counter-arguments.

The three companies:

- **Arctan Robotics.** Series A, $28M raised 9 months ago, 48 employees. The CEO is an ex-Stanford robotics post-doc and the technical co-founder; the CTO is her former lab mate. The GTM function is one Head of Sales (ex-Boston Dynamics commercial); engineering is 28 people; the operations seat is empty. The CEO is on investor roadshow prep for the Series B 6–9 months out; her lead investor from Lux Capital has told her she "needs a COO" before the next round. Her instinct is that she needs a Chief of Staff — specifically, "someone who can run the operating cadence and offload the stuff I'm bad at" — because she still wants to make product and technology calls herself. She has two finalists: a former McKinsey engagement manager (3 years post-MBA, no operating experience) and a former VP of Operations at a 400-person robotics startup that was acquired (operator, 10+ years).
- **MeshRouter.** Series B, $65M raised 4 months ago, 185 employees. B2B SaaS (network observability for enterprise). The CEO is a repeat founder (second company) with a GTM / sales background; the CTO is a strong engineer and a weak operator; the CRO is new (2 months in, hired on CEO's referral); the CFO was promoted internally from Controller. The CEO explicitly wants a COO, naming the Horowitz framing in his investor conversations. His board has pushed back — "you have never worked with a COO before, and your CRO just landed; adding another executive peer right now will fragment the team." The CEO's hiring process has produced two COO finalists (one ex-operations lead at a public SaaS company, one from a GTM background at a Series-D private company) and one CoS finalist (ex-BCG partner who left consulting 3 years ago and is now a startup operator at a lower-growth company). The CEO is openly leaning toward one of the COO finalists.
- **Thrumline.** Late Series A / early Series B, $34M raised 6 months ago, 95 employees. B2B SaaS (vertical for professional services firms). The CEO is a product-and-strategy founder (co-founder, non-technical, long product background) and the sole founder still in-seat (the technical co-founder exited 18 months ago, replaced by an external CTO). The executive team of 6 (CEO, CTO, VP Sales, VP CS, VP Finance, GC-as-contractor) is "fine" but "nothing is quite running smoothly" per the CEO's own description. The CEO has a Head of People candidate about to accept; she has one CoS candidate (ex-operator with 2 years in a CoS seat at a Series-D company) and no COO candidate in-process. Her instinct is to hire the CoS, grow the Head of People seat, and defer the COO question for 18 months.

For each of the three companies, the board advisor has asked you to produce:

1. A one-page CEO-variable analysis — the four variables from chapter 01 applied specifically.
2. A specific operations-function org-design recommendation for the next 18 months.
3. The counter-argument you expect to hear and your rebuttal.
4. A single-slide "org on page" diagram showing the recommended seats, reporting lines, and timing of each hire.

A fourth deliverable is cross-cutting: a side-by-side table that lets the three CEOs see each other's cases and the reasoning differences.

## Requirements

### Part A — Per-company CEO-variable analysis (A1 Arctan, A2 MeshRouter, A3 Thrumline)

For each of the three companies, author the CEO-variable analysis. Each memo must address:

1. **Variable 1 — the CEO's comparative advantage and the gap.** Where is the CEO strong (product / strategy; commercial; operator)? Where is the gap? Grounded in the specific CEO's described background and the executive-team shape. Not generic.
2. **Variable 2 — willingness to delegate line authority.** Evidence basis: the CEO's own statements, the CEO's history of delegation (where known from the problem statement), the executive-team shape (an executive team that routes everything to the CEO is a leading indicator). Call explicitly: is this CEO ready to be a *chair-and-strategist* CEO, or still an *operating* CEO?
3. **Variable 3 — the stage and shape of the executive team.** Headcount; executive-team size; density; which seats are strong vs. weak vs. empty. Reconciled against the Series-A / Series-B / growth default org designs in chapter 01.
4. **Variable 4 — fundraising-narrative pressure.** What is the lead investor saying? What is the next round's investor conversation going to probe? Is the pressure for a COO a signal about the CEO's gap or a signal about the board's own concern?
5. **The honest-answer paragraph.** Reconcile the four variables into a specific call: hire a CoS first, hire a COO first, or hire both. If "hire a CoS first," name whether the hire is a *strategic-partner CoS* (no COO trajectory) or a *CoS-in-training-for-COO* (18-24-month graduation). If "hire a COO first," name whether the COO is a *commercial COO* (owning GTM) or an *operations COO* (owning internal ops). If "both," name the sequencing and the reporting line.

### Part B — Per-company org design and sequencing (B1 Arctan, B2 MeshRouter, B3 Thrumline)

For each of the three companies, author the 18-month operations-function org design:

1. **The seats recommended for the next 18 months.** Draw from the chapter 01 four-seat architecture — CoS, COO, Head of BizOps, Head of Workplace — plus any that are explicitly deferred.
2. **The reporting lines.** Explicit — reports to CEO; reports to COO; reports to CoS; dotted-line to specific executive. Reconcile with the chapter 01 patterns (CoS reports to CEO; BizOps often to CoS at early stage, to COO at growth; Workplace to COO once one exists, otherwise to CoS or CPO as early fallback).
3. **The decision rights for the lead seat.** For the first hire (the CoS or the COO), produce the three-column decision-rights sketch from [chapter 08](../08-ceo-cos-or-coo-operating-relationship.md): CEO exclusive / CoS or COO unilateral / joint. One line per entry.
4. **The timing.** When does each seat land? Which comes first, which second, which can wait? Tied to specific triggers (headcount, operating-cadence maturity, specific programmes, specific executive-team hires).
5. **The seat the CEO proposed but you are deferring (if any).** Named explicitly with reasoning. The MeshRouter answer in particular should address the CEO's COO-first instinct.

### Part C — Per-company counter-argument and rebuttal (C1 Arctan, C2 MeshRouter, C3 Thrumline)

For each company, name the single strongest counter-argument to your recommendation and author the rebuttal.

- **Arctan.** The expected counter is Lux's "you need a COO before Series B." Your rebuttal must address what the next-round investor conversation will probe, how a strong CoS answers the same probe differently, and what the actual COO trigger would look like.
- **MeshRouter.** The expected counter is the CEO's "I want a COO." Your rebuttal must address the Horowitz framing (hire a COO only when ready to be a *chair-and-strategist* CEO), the executive-team shape (new CRO, promoted CFO), and the alternative CoS-first-with-COO-trajectory path.
- **Thrumline.** The expected counter — this one from the CoS candidate herself — is "if I take this seat with no COO trajectory, I'm stuck." Your rebuttal must address whether the CoS seat here is a terminal CoS seat, a CoS-in-training-for-COO seat, or a CoS-to-VP-Operations stepping stone, and name the honest signal to send at hire.

### Part D — Side-by-side comparison table

A single-page table across the three companies, with columns:

| Company | Stage / headcount | CEO profile | Variable-1 gap | Variable-2 delegation | Variable-3 team shape | Variable-4 pressure | First ops hire | COO trigger | Why same | Why different |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Populate for all three. The point of the table is to make the pattern-recognition visible: three different companies, three different answers, same framework.

### Part E — The "org on page" diagrams

For each company, a single-page org diagram. One box per executive. Named seats; reporting lines; time index (month 0 / month 6 / month 12 / month 18). Diagrams can be rendered in whatever format you choose — ASCII, mermaid, mermaid-rendered image, hand-drawn and attached as image, or a static table representation that reads as an org chart.

### Part F — The common failure mode you are most worried about

Across the three companies, name one common failure mode (from chapter 01's list or from the chapter 08 pattern library) you are most worried about for each. Different failure modes for each; one sentence of mitigation per.

## Starter guidance

- [Chapter 01](../01-coo-vs-cos-and-bizops-org-design.md) is the primary reference. The "COO vs. Chief-of-Staff decision at Series A/B" section's four variables are the lens for Part A.
- [Chapter 02](../02-first-ten-ops-hires-and-comp-benchmarks.md) is referenced for seat triggers and timing in Part B.
- [Chapter 08](../08-ceo-cos-or-coo-operating-relationship.md) is referenced for the decision-rights sketch in Part B.
- The Horowitz framing (hire a COO only when ready to be a chair-and-strategist CEO) is cited in chapters 01 and 08.
- Do not invent facts about the three companies beyond the problem statement. Where the problem statement is silent, state the assumption explicitly ("Assuming Thrumline's executive team can absorb a Head of People landing simultaneously with a CoS, which is tight but defensible given the headcount...").
- Do not invent compensation numbers — [chapter 02](../02-first-ten-ops-hires-and-comp-benchmarks.md) carries them and the problem statement does not require them for this exercise.
- Chief of Staff candidate profiles vary enormously (ex-consultant vs. ex-operator); the recommendation should either accept the specific candidate profile or recommend changing the profile with a reason.
- For Arctan, consider carefully whether an ex-McKinsey EM with no operating experience is the right CoS for a technical founder-CEO at an early-robotics company; the chapter 01 "CoS with no line credibility" failure mode is directly relevant.
- For MeshRouter, the "premature COO" failure mode in chapter 01 is relevant; so is the second-time-founder dynamic (the CEO has done this before and has conviction, which can be an asset or a trap).
- For Thrumline, the "CoS as glorified EA" failure mode and the CoS-to-VP-Operations stepping-stone pattern are both relevant.

## Acceptance criteria

The package is acceptable if:

1. Each per-company CEO-variable analysis (Part A) addresses all four variables explicitly against the specific company's facts — not generic — and reaches a specific recommendation (CoS first with named profile; COO first with GTM-or-ops split; both with sequencing). "It depends" is unacceptable.
2. Arctan's recommendation addresses the CoS-candidate-profile question (ex-consultant vs. ex-operator) with a specific call and reasoning tied to the CEO's variable-1 gap.
3. MeshRouter's recommendation either agrees with the CEO's COO instinct (with reasoning against the board's push-back) or disagrees (with reasoning against the CEO's Horowitz-citation), and names the specific finalist profile that fits — not a generic COO.
4. Thrumline's recommendation names whether the CoS seat is terminal, graduation-track, or stepping-stone, and specifies what signal gets sent to the candidate at hire.
5. Each per-company org design (Part B) names specific seats with specific reporting lines and specific triggers / timing. Vague "we would eventually hire ..." phrasing is unacceptable.
6. Each per-company counter-argument-and-rebuttal (Part C) addresses the strongest counter named in the problem statement, not a weak straw-man, and the rebuttal argues from the chapter 01 framework.
7. The side-by-side comparison table (Part D) is populated for all three companies with distinct rows and shows the pattern-recognition — the same framework producing three different answers.
8. The org-on-page diagrams (Part E) are legible, named, time-indexed, and consistent with the Part B designs.
9. The common-failure-modes section (Part F) names three different failure modes (one per company) drawn from chapter 01 or chapter 08's pattern libraries, with one-sentence mitigations each.
10. Cross-references to chapters 01, 02, and 08 are explicit where the reasoning draws from them; chapter 07's stage-model framing is relevant for the Arctan Series-A-to-B transition and should be cited at least once.
11. No specific compensation numbers are invented; the exercise does not require them.
12. No real-company names are invented as "the finalist we called"; the problem statement's generic descriptions are the operating facts. Nothing is left as `[TBD]` or `[FILL IN]`.
