# 8. The CEO–CoS / CEO–COO operating relationship

> The seat ([chapter 01](./01-coo-vs-cos-and-bizops-org-design.md)) is a job description. The *relationship* — decision rights, calendar, difficult-conversation preparation, executive-team choreography, board-prep partnership, graduation path — is the operating substance. A CoS or COO who has the title but not the relationship is theatre; a CoS or COO who has the relationship but not the title can still move the company.

## Motivation

A CoS or COO hired without an explicit operating relationship with the CEO fails in a specific, predictable pattern. Weeks 1–4 are productive — the CEO is excited, the seat is new, the executive team is curious. Weeks 5–12 degrade — the CEO relitigates the CoS's decisions, the executive team routes around the CoS to reach the CEO directly, the CoS's calendar fills with low-leverage work. By month 6 the CoS is a glorified executive assistant and the CEO is grumbling about the hire. By month 12 the CoS is on their way out or the company has hired a second one.

The failure is not the hire. It is the absence of an explicit, written, revisited operating relationship between CEO and CoS (or CEO and COO). What the CEO decides exclusively; what the CoS can decide unilaterally; what requires joint decision; how the calendar is managed; how difficult conversations get prepared; how the E-team meeting runs; how board prep is split with the CFO; what the graduation path looks like. Each of those is a conversation that most CEO-CoS pairs skip, and skipping each one costs weeks of effectiveness.

This chapter authors the operating relationship. It is partly a conversation framework — a list of things CEO and CoS / COO should discuss and write down — and partly a pattern-library of how those conversations typically resolve. Chapter 09 then builds the executive-team operating rhythm that this chapter's relationship chairs.

## The decision-rights division

The single most important artifact in the CEO–CoS or CEO–COO relationship is a written, agreed, revisited **decision-rights document**. One page, three columns: *CEO exclusive*, *CoS / COO unilateral*, *joint decision*.

### The template

The decision-rights document for a Series-B scale-up CEO–CoS pair, representative:

**CEO exclusive — the CEO decides, usually without consulting:**
- Strategic direction and company narrative.
- Hiring and firing of executive-team members.
- Executive-level compensation decisions (coordinated with CPO and comp committee — see [mod-105](../mod-105-equity-compensation-policy-and-comp-committee/)).
- Fundraising execution and investor-relationship management.
- Capital-structure decisions (option-pool refreshes, debt commitments, M&A).
- Final sign-off on the annual plan and the quarterly OKRs.
- Public positioning and press.

**CoS / COO unilateral — the CoS / COO decides, informs the CEO:**
- Operating-cadence structure (weekly / monthly / quarterly cadence, agendas, pre-reads, meeting mechanics — see [chapter 06](./06-operating-cadence-and-okrs-rhythm.md)).
- E-team meeting agenda and running order.
- Cross-functional programme management decisions within an approved scope.
- Vendor and systems decisions within the approval-authority matrix (see [chapter 05](./05-procurement-operating-model.md)).
- Internal communications tone and timing (announcements, all-hands structure).
- BizOps analytical priorities and work plan.
- Day-to-day operations of the operating function.

**Joint decision — CEO and CoS decide together:**
- CEO calendar and prioritisation (see below).
- Difficult executive-team conversations (coaching plans, performance interventions — see below).
- Executive-team meeting process evolution.
- Any decision touching the executive team's structure or dynamics.
- The CoS's own development plan and graduation trajectory.
- Material board-prep choices (what the pre-read emphasises, what gets a committee track — coordinated with the CFO per [mod-111](../mod-111-corporate-governance-board-operations-and-officer-duties/)).

### The dynamics the division is defending against

- **CEO reversal.** The CEO overturns a CoS decision in front of the executive team. The CoS's authority collapses; the executive team routes around the CoS from then on. Fix: the "CoS unilateral" column lists decisions the CEO commits in advance *not to reverse*; disagreements get discussed privately, with a joint decision for next time.
- **CoS overreach.** The CoS makes a decision that properly belongs to the CEO. The CEO loses trust. Fix: the "CEO exclusive" column is the CoS's checklist of decisions to escalate, not take.
- **Decision drift.** Over time the division becomes stale as the company grows. A decision that was "joint" at Series B becomes "CoS unilateral" at Series D as the CoS earns authority. Fix: formal review of the decision-rights document at each annual planning cycle.

### Scaling the division to a COO

The CoS version above is proximity-based. The COO version is authority-based. For a COO:

- **CEO exclusive** shrinks — some decisions move to joint or to COO unilateral as the CEO delegates line authority.
- **COO unilateral** expands significantly — the COO owns a line (GTM, operations, or both) with real decision authority. Day-to-day decisions in that line do not go through the CEO.
- **Joint** remains important — strategic direction, executive-team structure, major cross-functional calls.

The Horowitz framing (*The Hard Thing About Hard Things*) is that a COO who is reversible by the CEO on operating calls is not a COO; the CEO is still operating and the COO is a messenger. The decision-rights document is where this gets honest. <!-- needs-research: verify the specific Horowitz framing on the COO decision-rights question from *The Hard Thing About Hard Things* and from the a16z operator-focused writing. -->

## The calendar-management and prioritisation partnership

The CEO's calendar is a strategic artifact. It is also, in the absence of discipline, a reactive mess. The CoS's single most-leveraged weekly contribution is often the discipline that keeps the CEO's calendar matching the CEO's stated priorities.

### What the partnership does

**Monthly calendar audit.** Once a month, the CoS and CEO review the prior month's actual calendar against the CEO's stated top-three priorities. Typical finding: the CEO spent 15% of time on the top priority they said was 50% of their focus. The audit is for calibration, not criticism — but it is the signal that triggers a different allocation in the following month.

**Weekly calendar prep.** Each Friday, the CoS reviews the following week's calendar with the CEO. Each meeting gets a 1-sentence purpose statement; the pre-reads are confirmed; the preparation time is diarised. Low-value meetings get flagged for the CEO's "can we delegate this?" decision.

**Inbox and incoming-request triage.** The CoS runs a shared view of the CEO's inbox for anything that can be triaged. Introductions, meeting requests, candidate conversations, external-executive conversations — the CoS triages into categories: *decline politely*, *delegate to X*, *put on CEO's queue with context*, *schedule 15-min for CEO*. The CEO reviews the triage weekly.

**Deep-work protection.** The CEO's week includes blocks of deep-work time — strategy drafting, product thinking, specific-topic preparation. These are diarised and defended by the CoS against the pressure to fill them with meetings. Typical pattern: 2 half-day deep-work blocks per week minimum.

**No-meeting day.** Many CEO–CoS pairs reserve one day per week (often Friday) with no scheduled meetings — space for the CEO to think, write, catch up on strategic topics, and have unplanned conversations with the executive team.

### What breaks

- **CEO over-scheduling.** CEO says yes to every request; calendar fills; nothing of substance gets done. Fix: the CoS has standing authority to decline on the CEO's behalf per a stated-criteria list ("decline sales demos; decline general-audience podcast asks; decline external-advisor requests except from board members").
- **CoS as gatekeeper.** CoS filters access too aggressively; the executive team cannot reach the CEO; the CEO's connection to the company erodes. Fix: executive team always has standing access; the gatekeeper function applies to external, not internal.
- **CoS as scheduler.** The role degrades to pure logistics. Fix: the EA function (if there is one) runs scheduling mechanics; the CoS runs *prioritisation*, which is different work.

## The difficult-conversation partnership

A CEO who is not having the difficult conversations the company needs is a CEO under-performing. The CoS is often the mirror that surfaces which conversations are overdue and the thinking-partner who helps rehearse them.

### Typical difficult conversations

- **Underperforming executive.** A VP who was right for Series A is wrong for Series C. The CEO has avoided the conversation for six months. The CoS has observed the pattern from inside the operating cadence and can name it.
- **Strategic disagreement with a co-founder.** The CTO co-founder and CEO disagree on the technology direction. The disagreement surfaces in product arguments but has never been named directly.
- **Board-director disagreement.** A board member is pushing a position the CEO disagrees with. The CEO is avoiding the direct conversation and routing around through the lead investor.
- **Executive-team conflict.** Two VPs are in open conflict; the executive-team meetings have become dysfunctional; the CEO is hoping it resolves itself.
- **Pay / level / scope decisions for a long-tenured early employee.** The person is beloved; they are also under-levelled for their current role; the CEO cannot bring themselves to have the "you are over your head" conversation.

### How the partnership runs

- **The CoS names the conversation.** The CoS has observational access to the operating cadence that the CEO does not — the VP of X is not performing in the ops review; the two co-founders are not aligned in the strategic topic. Naming the pattern is often the hardest part; the CEO is usually relieved when it's named explicitly.
- **The CoS prepares the conversation with the CEO.** What is the goal of the conversation? What does good look like? What is the walkaway? What is the first thing to say? What are the three or four likely responses, and what is the CEO's planned reaction to each?
- **The CEO has the conversation.** The CoS does not have it; the CEO has it. This matters — the CEO's willingness to have the conversation is where authority sits.
- **The CoS supports the follow-through.** Documentation, next steps, the executive-team adjustments, the comms (if any) land afterward.

### What breaks

- **CoS as proxy.** The CEO delegates the difficult conversation to the CoS. "You go tell them." This destroys authority — both the CoS's authority (they are now the executioner, not the strategic partner) and the CEO's authority (they are visibly avoiding the conversation). Fix: the CEO owns the conversation; the CoS prepares it.
- **CoS enabling avoidance.** The CoS protects the CEO from the discomfort of the conversation by taking on the surrounding work. The conversation never happens. Fix: the CoS's job includes surfacing the conversation and naming the cost of avoiding it.

The Horowitz / Grove / Lencioni canon on this is consistent: an executive team's health depends on the CEO's willingness to have hard conversations directly. The CoS's job is to amplify that willingness, not substitute for it. <!-- needs-research: verify the specific framings from Horowitz's *The Hard Thing About Hard Things*, Grove's *High Output Management*, and Lencioni's *The Five Dysfunctions of a Team* on CEO-direct accountability for difficult conversations. -->

## The executive-team meeting choreography

The CoS owns the executive-team weekly meeting as an operating artifact. The CEO owns the executive-team as a group. The CoS's job is to make the CEO's leadership of the executive team as productive as possible — which is a different thing from running the meeting on the CEO's behalf.

### The division of labour

- **CEO leads the meeting.** The CEO sits in the chair, opens, closes, and makes the final calls on decisions when the group does not reach consensus. The CoS does *not* sit in the chair.
- **CoS runs the meeting mechanics.** Agenda sourcing; pre-read distribution 24 hours in advance; time management inside the meeting; decision-log and action-item capture; follow-through tracking into the next week.
- **CoS prepares the CEO for the meeting.** The CoS and CEO meet (often Friday afternoon or Monday morning before the E-team meeting) to walk the agenda, flag any unresolved topics, decide the CEO's position on contested items.
- **CoS protects the CEO from the meeting degrading.** Standard failure modes — long updates, no decisions, the loudest voice winning — require active moderation. The CoS flags privately when the pattern surfaces and works with the CEO on correction.

### The agenda discipline

Chapter 09 covers the agenda structure in depth. Here the key points for the CEO–CoS relationship:

- **Pre-reads mandatory.** No "I'll walk you through my deck in the room." Agenda items that cannot be pre-read get deferred or restructured.
- **Decisions explicit.** Every item has a type — *inform*, *discuss*, *decide*. The *decide* items get decided; the meeting does not end with a dozen hanging decisions.
- **Action items with owners and dates.** Captured in the meeting; distributed within 24 hours; reviewed at the top of the next meeting.
- **Topics not people.** The meeting is a discussion of topics, not a round-robin update from each executive. Round-robin updates are a 1-on-1 format; they do not belong in E-team.

### What breaks

- **E-team meeting as update theatre.** Each VP gives a 10-minute update; nothing is decided; nobody listens to anybody else's update. Fix: structural redesign — kill the round-robin, substitute dashboard-driven exceptions discussion.
- **CoS running the meeting in the CEO's absence or distraction.** The CEO joins half-attentive; the CoS carries the meeting; the executive team learns that CEO engagement is optional. Fix: when the CEO cannot be present and engaged, cancel the meeting rather than run it as a shadow.
- **CoS taking positions in the meeting.** The CoS has strong opinions; voices them in the meeting; is perceived as acting with CEO's authority on topics where the CEO has not actually taken a position. Fix: the CoS's position-taking is in prep conversations with the CEO; in the meeting the CoS runs mechanics and intervenes only on process.

## The board-preparation partnership

Board preparation is a joint product of CFO (content) and CoS (choreography). The CoS does not author the board pack content — the CFO owns that per the mod-111 boundary. The CoS owns the preparation discipline:

### What the CoS owns

- **Pre-read discipline.** Board pack distributed per the delegation-of-authority / board-rules timing (typically 5–7 days before the meeting — see [mod-111 chapter 02](../mod-111-corporate-governance-board-operations-and-officer-duties/02-board-cadence-and-materials.md)).
- **Agenda choreography.** The sequence of topics; the time allocations; the pre-call with each board member (often the CEO does these, with the CoS scheduling and the CFO briefing).
- **CEO prep.** The CEO's own prep against the pack. The CoS runs a prep session the day before the board meeting — walk the agenda, anticipate questions, decide positions on contested points.
- **Follow-through on board decisions.** Resolutions passed, consents required, action items for the executive team. Captured in the minutes (corporate secretary); distributed into the operating cadence by the CoS.
- **Standing committee coordination.** Audit, Compensation, Nominating / Governance committees each have their own cadence (per mod-111). The CoS coordinates with each committee's chair-of-staff (if any) or the CFO (for Audit) or the CPO (for Compensation).

### What the CFO owns

- The board pack content (financials, KPIs, operating metrics, commentary).
- Investor-relations content (updates to current investors; prep for new-round investor conversations).
- The audit-committee content.
- The year-end audit relationship.

### What the GC / corporate secretary owns

- Board minutes, resolutions, consents (per [mod-111 chapter 03](../mod-111-corporate-governance-board-operations-and-officer-duties/03-corporate-secretary-function.md)).
- The corporate record and the stock-ledger discipline.
- Director indemnification and D&O insurance interface.

### The three-way discipline

CFO + CoS + GC run board prep as a three-way discipline. A failure in any one of the three (late financials, late agenda, late minutes) collapses the discipline. The weekly board-prep check-in in the lead-up to a board meeting is a standing item on the CoS's calendar.

## The CoS-to-COO graduation path

Chapter 01 introduced the CoS-to-COO graduation path as a common pattern. The CEO–CoS relationship is where it either materialises or stalls.

### The signals that the graduation is viable

- **The CoS is operating with real line credibility.** Function heads route decisions through the CoS not because the CEO delegates them to the CoS, but because they trust the CoS's judgment on their own merits.
- **The CoS is making decisions the CEO does not relitigate.** The decision-rights "CoS unilateral" column is populated with substance, and the CEO does not reverse those decisions.
- **The CoS has run at least one major cross-functional programme end-to-end.** A systems migration, a workplace expansion, a pricing rollout, an org restructure. The programme succeeded and the CoS was visibly the owner.
- **The executive team sees the CoS as a peer, not a staff member.** When the CoS speaks in the E-team meeting on a topic within the "CoS unilateral" column, the executives respond substantively, not deferentially.
- **The CoS has demonstrated independent judgment on difficult calls.** Taken a position that disagreed with the CEO's instinct; defended it; was right (or wrong, and acknowledged the learning).

### What CEO and CoS need to agree

- **The target seat.** COO is one target; a functional VP seat (VP of Operations, VP of Business Systems, VP of People) is another. Which one depends on where the gap is and what the CoS's strengths are.
- **The timeline.** Typically 18–24 months from CoS start to graduation decision. Longer timelines degrade — a CoS who is still a CoS at month 36 is a CoS forever.
- **The intervening milestones.** Specific programmes to run, specific functions to shadow, specific executive relationships to deepen. The milestones are check-points; achieving them is not a guarantee of the graduation, and missing them is not a disqualification.
- **The alternative.** What happens if the CoS does not graduate? Three honest options: continue in CoS seat (sometimes right if the CoS and CEO are getting substantial leverage); move to a different company for the next step up; move to a non-executive seat (programme head, head of a function below the C-suite). Each is legitimate; none is a failure if named honestly in advance.

### What breaks

- **No conversation about the graduation.** The CoS assumes graduation is coming; the CEO assumes the CoS is content in the CoS seat. By month 24 the CoS is frustrated and the CEO is confused. Fix: have the conversation at the 12-month mark, and formalise it in the review cycle thereafter.
- **CEO as gatekeeper.** The CEO wants the CoS to graduate but does not create the opportunities (line responsibility, cross-functional programme ownership, executive-team peer visibility). The CoS cannot build the credibility that the graduation requires. Fix: the graduation plan is a two-way commitment; the CEO's commitment is to the opportunities.
- **CoS graduating into a role they cannot do.** A strategic-partner CoS graduates into a COO role; discovers that running a line is a different job; struggles. The CEO's trust erodes; the hire is unwound. Fix: honest conversation about the fit, including the alternative of a smaller line (VP of Operations) as a stepping stone.

## The CEO–COO relationship — different dynamics, same principles

Everything above applies, with adjustments, to the CEO–COO relationship. The specific differences:

- **The decision-rights division is heavier on the COO side.** The COO has line authority; the "COO unilateral" column covers substantial P&L territory. The CEO's reversal privileges are narrower.
- **The calendar partnership is less intensive.** The COO's calendar is their own; the CEO–COO weekly meeting is the primary alignment moment, not daily calendar triage.
- **The difficult-conversation partnership is more peer-to-peer.** The COO is an executive peer; the conversations are collaborations, not CEO-to-staff.
- **The board-preparation partnership is more direct — the COO presents at the board.** The COO has their own board presence (operating results, operating risks); the CEO–COO alignment happens before the board meeting on each other's content.
- **The graduation path for a COO is to President, CEO successor, or an external CEO role.** The internal path (COO → President → CEO successor) is a specific trajectory the CEO and board plan for; the external path is more common. See the Horowitz / Slootman canon on the COO-to-President transition. <!-- needs-research: verify the Horowitz and Slootman framings on the COO-to-President and COO-to-CEO-successor trajectories from *The Hard Thing About Hard Things* and *Amp It Up*. -->

### When the CEO succession trajectory activates

The COO as CEO successor is a particular pattern. The board and the CEO formally identify the COO as the primary succession candidate; the COO's development is explicitly oriented toward that trajectory. This happens in late-stage private companies and pre-IPO companies more often than at Series B / C.

- **The explicit designation.** The board (often through the Nominating / Governance committee) formally identifies the COO as a succession candidate. Not a public announcement; a documented plan.
- **The development programme.** Specific areas the COO is being developed in — investor relations, board management, external-facing executive work, strategic-direction authorship. These are areas typically owned by the CEO; the development programme transitions them over time.
- **The acceleration moments.** Significant moments where the COO stands in for the CEO — a board meeting the CEO cannot attend, an all-hands the CEO delegates, an investor conversation the CEO sits out of. Each is a trust-building moment.
- **The transition timing.** Typically 2–5 years from designation to transition. The board owns the timing.

Not every COO-CEO pair runs this trajectory, and not every COO is a CEO successor. The alternative is a long COO tenure (3–7 years) with no transition, and an eventual external CEO successor or an eventual CEO continuation.

## Concrete example — the Series-B CEO–CoS operating relationship, written down

**Company:** Series-B B2B SaaS, 130 employees, CEO is a product-and-strategy founder, newly-hired CoS from a 7-year management-consulting background.

**Written operating relationship — one page, revisited every 6 months.**

**Decision rights:**
- *CEO exclusive:* Strategic direction, exec-team hiring / firing, exec comp, fundraising, major capital decisions, final OKRs sign-off, public positioning.
- *CoS unilateral:* Operating cadence, E-team meeting mechanics, cross-functional programme scope within approved plan, vendor decisions under $100k, internal comms tone, BizOps analytical priorities.
- *Joint:* CEO calendar, difficult exec-team conversations, exec-team meeting process evolution, CoS development plan.

**Weekly rhythm:**
- Friday 4pm: CEO–CoS 1:1 (60 min). CEO's week in review; next week's priorities; difficult conversations to prep; CoS's own update.
- Monday 8:30am: 30-min pre-E-team prep for the 9am E-team meeting.
- Thursday 3pm: Shadow CEO on a selected external meeting (varies — investor conversation, board-member call, customer executive).

**Monthly rhythm:**
- Last Friday of month: 2-hour calendar audit against stated top-three priorities.
- Mid-month: Review of the current cross-functional programme the CoS is sponsoring.

**Quarterly rhythm:**
- CoS's own development plan reviewed and updated.
- CoS's workload and energy reviewed (burnout prevention — CoS roles are known for burning out at month 18–24; proactive management is in the relationship).
- Decision-rights document reviewed and revised.

**Graduation path (as agreed at hire):**
- Target: COO at month 24 or VP of Operations at month 18 as a stepping stone.
- Intervening milestones: lead the systems-migration programme to completion by month 12; shadow the CRO on three enterprise sales cycles by month 15; co-author the annual plan with the CEO by month 18.
- Alternative if the graduation does not materialise: 12-month extension of CoS seat with expanded BizOps leadership; or external CoS-to-COO move with the CEO's active sponsorship.

**Difficult-conversation partnership:**
- The CoS has standing permission to name a difficult conversation the CEO is avoiding; the CEO has standing permission to decline the surfacing with "not yet" (and a reason).
- Any surfaced conversation gets a 30-min prep session before the CEO holds it.

## Common failure modes

- **No written relationship.** The CEO–CoS pair relies on good intentions and weekly conversation; the discipline drifts; by month 6 the role is a shadow of what it should be. Fix: write the one-page relationship down.
- **CEO as unreliable principal.** The CEO reverses CoS decisions in public, over-schedules the CoS, treats the role as a utility. The CoS cannot build authority or leverage. Fix: honest conversation about whether the CEO actually wants a CoS; sometimes the answer is "no, I want an executive assistant" and the hire is wrong.
- **CoS as unreliable proxy.** The CoS takes positions in the CEO's name that the CEO has not actually endorsed. The executive team learns that the CoS does not reliably reflect the CEO. Fix: the CoS does not take positions in the CEO's name without an explicit authorisation; the CoS speaks for themselves on operating decisions.
- **No calendar discipline.** The partnership does not include active calendar management; the CEO's time is reactive; the CoS cannot create the leverage the role is supposed to create. Fix: the weekly and monthly calendar discipline is non-negotiable.
- **Difficult-conversation avoidance.** The CEO avoids the conversations; the CoS does not surface them; the executive-team dysfunction compounds. Fix: the CoS's job description includes surfacing the conversations.
- **E-team meeting degradation.** The meeting runs as update theatre; nobody decides anything; the CoS does not intervene because the CEO has not asked for intervention. Fix: the CoS has standing authority on meeting mechanics and does intervene.
- **Board-prep breakdown.** CFO authors the pack late; CoS escalates late; GC is surprised by the resulting discussion in the meeting. Fix: the three-way board-prep discipline is a standing weekly check-in in the lead-up to each meeting.
- **Graduation path stalled or absent.** CoS is at the role for 30+ months with no graduation path discussion; frustration builds; CoS exits. Fix: the 12-month conversation is non-negotiable.

## Summary

- The CEO–CoS or CEO–COO relationship is the operating substance, not the title. Author it, write it down, revisit it.
- **Decision rights.** Three columns: *CEO exclusive*, *CoS / COO unilateral*, *joint*. Reviewed annually; revised as the company grows.
- **Calendar and prioritisation.** The CoS's single most-leveraged weekly contribution is calendar discipline — monthly audit, weekly prep, inbox triage, deep-work protection, no-meeting day.
- **Difficult-conversation partnership.** The CoS names the overdue conversation; the CEO owns the conversation; the CoS prepares and supports.
- **E-team meeting choreography.** The CEO leads the meeting; the CoS runs the mechanics; the CoS does not substitute for the CEO.
- **Board-preparation partnership.** Three-way with CFO (content) and GC (corporate record). The CoS owns the choreography and the CEO prep.
- **CoS-to-COO graduation.** 18–24 months is typical; the signals are line credibility, executive-peer status, and demonstrated independent judgment; the alternative paths are explicit.
- **CEO–COO relationship.** Same principles, heavier COO side on decision rights, more peer-to-peer on difficult conversations, direct board-facing on operating results. CEO succession trajectory applies at late stage.
- Chapter 09 authors the executive-team meeting rhythm in depth; this chapter authored the CEO-side relationship that the chapter 09 rhythm chairs.
