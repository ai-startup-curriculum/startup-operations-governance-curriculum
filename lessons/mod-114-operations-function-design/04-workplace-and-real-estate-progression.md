# 4. Workplace and real-estate progression — from remote-first to hub-and-spoke

> A lease is the second-largest capital commitment most scale-ups make, after payroll. Sign it wrong and the company carries a multi-million-dollar mistake for the length of the term. Sign it right — with a tenant-representation firm, a defensible TI package, and a sublease exit built in — and the workplace becomes a durable operating asset instead of a durable liability.

## Motivation

Workplace decisions look small until they aren't. A coworking upgrade at Series A costs $15k a month; a direct short-term lease at late Series A costs several million dollars over the term; a long-term hub lease at Series B costs tens of millions over the term. Each decision commits the company to a physical footprint that outlasts the workforce plan that justified it.

At the same time, workplace is the physical expression of the hybrid / RTO policy the People function owns. Every workplace decision needs to be legible against the policy — a company running a "3 days in office" policy needs a footprint that seats 60–75% of the local workforce on peak days; a company running "remote-first with hubs" needs a very different footprint. Get the policy-to-footprint math wrong and the space is either empty (a $50/sqft-per-year embarrassment) or overflowing (a hybrid-policy failure the workforce complains about).

This chapter maps the workplace progression — remote-first at seed, coworking at Series A, first direct short-term lease at late Series A / Series B, long-term hub lease at Series B, hub-and-spoke at growth — with the tenant-representation firm engagement, the tenant-improvement negotiation, the sublease exit option, and the interaction with the RTO / hybrid policy owned by [mod-108](../mod-108-culture-employee-experience-and-dei/).

## The five-stage workplace progression

### Stage 1 — Remote-first (seed / pre-seed, ~1–15 employees)

Founders work from home; from a garage; from a founder's living room. The first physical space is often a founder's apartment or a rented desk at a coworking space for two or three people. There is no lease, no facilities function, and no workplace budget beyond a laptop-and-tools stipend.

**What this stage buys:** Optionality. The company can shift geography with the CEO's next move, absorb a co-founder's relocation, or add a headcount in any city without a workplace decision. Every early-stage founder should preserve this as long as it holds.

**What this stage costs:** The lack of an in-person space. Product ideation, culture formation, and executive-team coherence are harder in a fully-remote seed team. Many seed-stage teams solve this with periodic in-person offsites (quarterly, week-long) rather than a permanent space.

**When to graduate:** ~15+ employees; a hiring plan that concentrates a critical mass in one geography; a founder's decision to anchor the company in a specific city (often driven by fundraising or a partnership).

### Stage 2 — Coworking (Series A, ~15–50 employees)

The first "office" is typically a coworking membership — WeWork, Industrious, Convene, Regus, Bond Collective, Servcorp, a regional coworking chain, or a specialised startup-focused space (Founders Center, ZeroCircle, or a city-specific brand). Typical cost: **US$500–1,500 per desk per month** in a Tier-1 US city; less in Tier-2 cities; more in enterprise-class private-office coworking (Convene, Industrious's higher tiers). <!-- needs-research: verify per-desk coworking pricing bands across WeWork, Industrious, Convene, Regus, Bond Collective, and Servcorp in major US markets in 2026. -->

**What this stage buys:** Physical space with month-to-month or short-term commitment. Furniture, internet, meeting rooms, coffee, and reception included. No lease exposure, no facilities function, no build-out.

**What this stage costs:** Density limits (most coworking arrangements cap at ~20 desks per company in a shared floor before an enterprise / private-office tier); shared common areas that erode focus; brand dilution ("we're in WeWork" is not "we're in our office"); and cost that becomes uncompetitive above ~30 desks (per-desk economics stop making sense against a direct lease).

**Common failure mode:** Growing to 60 employees in a coworking space, spending US$50k+ per month on desks, and only then starting the lease search. The lease search itself is a 4–9 month process at minimum ([Stage 3](#stage-3--first-direct-short-term-lease-late-series-a--series-b-50100-employees)); start it 6–9 months before you need to move.

**When to graduate:** ~40–50 employees in one location; per-desk economics tipping against direct lease; a hiring plan that will push past 75 employees within 12 months; a management team requiring privacy the coworking layout cannot deliver.

### Stage 3 — First direct short-term lease (late Series A / Series B, ~50–100 employees)

The company signs its first direct lease — typically a **3-year term** on a **3,000–5,000 sqft** space, in a building the tenant-representation firm has vetted, with a modest tenant-improvement (TI) package to build out the space to the company's needs.

**What this stage buys:** Ownership of the workplace experience. The space reflects the company's brand and workflow. The lease term is short enough to be exit-able without catastrophic loss. Cost per desk is meaningfully lower than coworking at this scale.

**What this stage costs:** A real facilities function (see [Stage 5 costs](#stage-5--hub-and-spoke-growth-stage-250-employees-multiple-cities) below); TI capital or amortised TI cost in the rent; a security deposit (typically 1–3 months of rent plus TI overspend); a lease-obligation liability that reflects on the balance sheet (ASC 842 / IFRS 16 right-of-use asset — a coordination point with the CFO).

**Common failure modes:**
- Signing without a tenant-rep firm (see below).
- Sizing to today's headcount rather than the 18-24-month plan.
- Accepting the landlord's standard TI package without negotiating expansion and sublease rights.
- Committing to a longer term (5 years) than the plan can support, in exchange for a rent concession that turns into a stranded asset when the plan changes.

**When to graduate:** Headcount past ~100 in the local market; the current space's growth-capacity exhausted; a strategic anchor decision (hub city with long-term commitment).

### Stage 4 — First long-term hub lease (Series B, ~100–250 employees)

The company signs a **5-year (or longer) lease** on a **10,000–30,000 sqft** hub in the anchor city — a purpose-built build-out, typically in a Class-A or Class-B+ building, with a significant TI package (US$50–150+ per sqft, landlord-funded or landlord-amortised into rent). <!-- needs-research: verify current tenant-improvement allowance ranges in Class-A office buildings in Tier-1 US markets in 2026 — TI packages moved materially through the post-2020 office-market cycle and continue to reset. -->

**What this stage buys:** A durable workplace anchor. Meeting-room capacity for on-site events, board meetings, and customer visits. A visible brand statement. A financial predictability the 3-year lease cannot deliver (long-term rent locked in, TI amortised).

**What this stage costs:** A multi-year commitment sized in tens of millions of dollars over the term. A larger security deposit (often a letter of credit rather than cash; the CFO coordinates). A capital expenditure for furniture, A/V, and finishes that aren't in the landlord's TI package. The obligation shows on the balance sheet as an ASC 842 right-of-use asset and lease liability of comparable size.

**When to graduate:** Multi-city footprint decision — spoke cities where meaningful headcount lives (see [Stage 5](#stage-5--hub-and-spoke-growth-stage-250-employees-multiple-cities)).

### Stage 5 — Hub-and-spoke (growth stage, ~250+ employees, multiple cities)

The company operates a **hub** (primary office in the anchor city) plus **spokes** — smaller offices in cities with meaningful concentrated headcount (typically ~20+ employees to justify a dedicated space). Spokes are often shorter-term leases (3-year) or coworking / enterprise-coworking (Industrious, Convene enterprise) until the local headcount stabilises.

**Common configurations:**
- **Hub + one or two US spokes** — e.g., SF hub + NYC spoke + one Tier-2 spoke (Austin, Boulder, Denver, Nashville).
- **US hub + one or two international spokes** — e.g., SF hub + London spoke + one continental European spoke (see [mod-113](../mod-113-international-expansion-and-global-workforce/)).
- **Two US hubs** — e.g., SF and NYC as co-primary hubs, with smaller spokes in cities where key hires cluster (increasingly common in the post-2020 hybrid-work era).

**What this stage costs:** A workplace function that runs multiple leases, multiple facilities vendors, multiple local RTO / hybrid interpretations, and a workplace-experience programme that maintains a consistent culture across the footprint. The Head of Workplace ([chapter 01](./01-coo-vs-cos-and-bizops-org-design.md), [chapter 02](./02-first-ten-ops-hires-and-comp-benchmarks.md)) becomes a real function with 5–10 direct reports.

**What this stage buys:** Talent access in multiple markets; disaster-recovery resilience (a single-city outage does not shut the company down); a footprint that supports enterprise-customer relationships in multiple regions.

## The tenant-representation firm model

Do not sign a commercial-office lease without a tenant-representation (tenant-rep) firm. This is the single most important workplace-decision discipline, and one of the most-skipped.

### What tenant-rep firms do

A tenant-rep firm represents the tenant (you) in a commercial lease transaction. Their responsibilities:

- **Market survey.** Comprehensive inventory of available spaces in the target market that fit your specification (submarket, size range, class, term, timing).
- **Landlord shortlist.** 3–8 landlord candidates for a formal RFP.
- **RFP and negotiation.** Formal RFP to shortlisted landlords; multiple rounds of counter-offers; comparison matrix across candidates.
- **Economic modelling.** Total lease-obligation modelling — base rent, escalators, operating-expense pass-throughs (CAM — common area maintenance), TI allowance, free-rent periods, and other concessions — translated to a comparable net-effective-rent figure across candidates.
- **Lease-document negotiation.** Alongside the company's real-estate counsel (external, retained under GC oversight), the tenant-rep firm negotiates the business terms of the lease document itself.
- **Post-signing project management.** Coordinating the TI build-out, sometimes with a dedicated project-management arm of the firm.

### Who the firms are

The dominant tenant-representation firms in the US commercial market:

- **CBRE** (Coldwell Banker Richard Ellis) — global, largest by revenue, strong across all US markets.
- **JLL** (Jones Lang LaSalle) — global, strong tech-tenant practice.
- **Cushman & Wakefield** — global, strong in Tier-1 and international.
- **Newmark** — US-strong, expanding globally.
- **Colliers** — global, strong in Tier-2 US and international.
- **Savills** — global, particularly strong in London and international.
- **Avison Young** — mid-market focus.
- **Boutique / regional firms** — many strong regional players (Kidder Mathews in the Pacific Northwest, Kennedy Wilson, CresaPartners as a tenant-only firm that avoids landlord conflicts, and dozens of city-specific specialists).

### How the firms are paid

Tenant-rep firms are paid a **commission by the landlord**, not by the tenant. The commission is typically a percentage of the total lease value (often 4–6% for a first-time deal, with a portion recognised at execution and a portion at rent commencement or over the term). This can create a **conflict of interest** — the firm has an incentive to close a bigger, longer deal — that is worth surfacing explicitly.

**Mitigations:**
- Use a **tenant-only firm** (Cresa is the largest specifically-tenant-focused firm) that does not represent landlords in the same market.
- Ask for the firm's commission structure in writing at engagement.
- Include a **fee arrangement** (flat fee, hourly, hybrid) if the commission-based model does not align.
- Verify that the firm's recommendation would still hold if the commission were structured differently.

### Engagement mechanics

- **Sign an engagement letter.** Defines the firm's role, the scope of the search, the commission arrangement, the exclusivity terms (typically exclusive to that firm for the specific search / market), and the termination terms.
- **Engage 6–9 months before need.** The full search-through-move-in cycle for a direct lease is 6–9 months in most Tier-1 markets; 4–6 months in less-competitive markets; longer for a build-to-suit or a purpose-built build-out.
- **Include your real-estate counsel from day one.** The counsel reviews the LOI, the lease document, and the ancillary agreements (TI construction contract, if separate; broker engagement; letter-of-credit terms).

## Tenant-improvement (TI) negotiation

The TI package is where landlord concessions get delivered. A well-negotiated TI package can be worth hundreds of thousands to millions of dollars over the term; a poorly-negotiated one leaves that money on the table or shifts unexpected out-of-pocket cost to the tenant.

### What TI covers

Tenant improvements are the build-out of the leased space to the tenant's specification — walls, doors, floor coverings, ceiling, lighting, HVAC modifications, electrical, plumbing (where needed), millwork, and finishes. The landlord funds some portion of the TI cost through a **TI allowance** stated as a dollar-per-square-foot number; anything above the allowance the tenant funds directly or amortises into rent.

### Typical TI allowances

The TI allowance depends on market, building class, and lease term. Rough directional ranges in Tier-1 US markets (SF Bay Area, NYC, Boston, Chicago, LA) for Class-A office space in a moderate-market cycle:

- **Short-term lease (3 years):** US$25–50 per sqft.
- **Standard-term lease (5–7 years):** US$50–100 per sqft.
- **Long-term lease (10+ years):** US$100–200+ per sqft.

Tier-2 markets and Class-B buildings run lower; downtown Manhattan Class-A trophy space runs higher; post-2020 tenant-favourable market cycles push allowances up materially. <!-- needs-research: verify current TI allowance ranges in Tier-1 US markets in 2026 — the office market cycle drives significant variance. -->

### Negotiation points on TI

- **Allowance size** — negotiate against comparable-transaction data the tenant-rep firm provides.
- **Turnkey vs. cash allowance** — turnkey means the landlord builds and delivers a completed space to specification; cash allowance means the tenant runs the build-out with the landlord reimbursing. Turnkey shifts construction risk to the landlord; cash allowance gives the tenant more control.
- **Free-rent period** — landlords often offer 1–3 months of free rent at commencement (longer for larger deals or softer markets). This is a straightforward concession; take it.
- **Amortisation of TI overspend into rent** — if the tenant wants a build-out that exceeds the allowance, the landlord will often amortise the overage into the rent at a stated interest rate (often prime + a spread). Compare to the tenant's cost of capital and to a direct capital expenditure.
- **Expansion rights** — right of first offer (ROFO) or right of first refusal (ROFR) on contiguous space that becomes available during the term. Free option, always negotiate for.
- **Contraction rights** — the right to give back a portion of the space at a stated point (often year 3 of a 5-year lease) with a defined penalty. Rare, valuable if the growth plan is uncertain.
- **Early termination option** — the right to terminate the lease at a stated point with a defined penalty (typically unamortised TI + a fee equivalent to some months of rent). Valuable if the growth plan is uncertain; the landlord will price it.
- **Sublease and assignment rights** — the tenant's right to sublease all or a portion of the space, subject to landlord consent (which should be limited to "consent not to be unreasonably withheld"). This is the exit option.

## The sublease exit option

Every long-term lease is signed with a plan; the plan changes. The sublease exit is the mechanic that turns a stranded lease into a manageable liability.

- **Right to sublease.** The lease must include the right to sublease all or a portion of the space, subject to landlord consent not to be unreasonably withheld. Landlords sometimes push for a right of recapture (the landlord's right to take the space back rather than approve the sublease) — negotiate against it where possible.
- **Sub-tenant financial requirements.** The landlord may set a minimum financial-profile requirement for the sub-tenant (creditworthiness, use category, size). Some flexibility, but not unlimited.
- **Landlord approval process.** The lease should define the approval timeline (typically 15–30 days from full submission) and the approval standard (creditworthy sub-tenant using the space for a compatible use).
- **Financial economics.** The tenant remains liable under the primary lease; the sub-tenant pays the sub-lessor (the tenant). If the sub-tenant defaults, the tenant is still on the hook to the landlord. Sublease rent is typically at or below the primary rent in a soft market — a sublease loss is common and is the "cost" of the exit option.
- **Broker-fee obligation.** The tenant typically pays the sublease-broker fee (both sides); model it into the exit cost.

The 2020-onward post-COVID office-market cycle saw an unprecedented volume of sublease space hit the market as tenants exercised the sublease exit. The mechanic that made those exits possible was negotiated at lease signing years earlier. If a lease you are signing today does not include a workable sublease right, you are pre-committing to carry the space for the full term regardless of future business need. <!-- needs-research: verify the specific dynamics of the 2020–2025 US office sublease market and the typical sublease-loss experience for tenants that exercised the option. -->

## Interaction with the return-to-office / hybrid-work policy

The RTO / hybrid-work policy owned by People / Culture (see [mod-108](../mod-108-culture-employee-experience-and-dei/)) sets the demand pattern; workplace sets the supply. The two must agree.

### Common policy patterns and their workplace implications

- **Fully remote.** No dedicated office space per employee. Small hub or coworking presence for periodic gatherings and executive-team meetings. Workplace footprint driven by event-and-visit density, not employee count.
- **Remote-first with hubs.** Employees can work from anywhere; hubs exist in a few cities for in-person collaboration and gathering. Workplace footprint sized to peak visit-density (typically 40–60% of local-city employees on peak days).
- **Hybrid-anchored (2–3 days in office).** Employees are expected in office 2–3 days per week, coordinated to specific days (typically Tuesday-Wednesday-Thursday). Workplace footprint sized to accommodate 60–80% of local-city employees on peak days.
- **Office-first (4–5 days in office).** Employees are expected in office 4–5 days per week. Workplace footprint sized to accommodate 100% of local-city employees on any given day.

### The sizing math

For a hybrid-anchored policy at, say, 65% peak-day occupancy target:
- Local headcount = 200.
- Peak-day attendance ≈ 130 (200 × 65%).
- Desk-sharing ratio of 0.75 (each desk supports 1.33 employees over the week) implies 175 desks.
- Meeting-room capacity, phone-booth capacity, common-area capacity size against peak attendance, not headcount.

The mistake is sizing to headcount (100% occupancy) for a hybrid policy — the space is chronically half-empty and cost-per-attended-day is triple what it should be. The opposite mistake is sizing to average attendance (say, 55%) — peak days overflow, hybrid policy compliance erodes, and employees start avoiding the office entirely.

**Get the policy first, then size the space.** Any workplace decision that runs ahead of the policy will be wrong.

### Policy changes drive space changes

An RTO policy change (from remote-first to hybrid-anchored, for example) increases required peak capacity and typically requires either an expansion (ROFO on adjacent space, or a new lease) or a redesign (reducing meeting-room density in favour of individual-desk density). A hybrid-to-remote-first move often triggers a sublease exit ([above](#the-sublease-exit-option)). Every policy change has a workplace consequence; the two functions coordinate ahead of the change, not after.

## Concrete example — Series-B SaaS going from coworking to first long-term hub lease

**Company:** Series-B B2B SaaS, 130 employees, primary geography San Francisco, 90 in-Bay-Area, hybrid-anchored policy (Tuesday–Thursday in office, 3 days). Currently in a 40-desk WeWork private-floor arrangement at ~US$65k/month, growing to 200 employees over the next 24 months (~150 in Bay Area).

**Trigger.** Coworking capacity exhausted; per-desk economics no longer competitive; management team needs privacy; hybrid-anchored policy peak days overflowing the coworking.

**Search timeline.**
- Month 0: engage Cresa (tenant-only tenant-rep firm) and JLL for a competitive engagement conversation; select Cresa; sign engagement letter.
- Month 1–2: submarket survey (SoMa, Financial District, Mission Bay); short-list of 12 buildings.
- Month 2–3: RFP to 6 landlords; site tours; TI planning conversations with 2 architects on shortlist.
- Month 3–4: LOI negotiation with 3 finalists; final selection; real-estate counsel engagement to negotiate the lease document.
- Month 4–6: lease-document negotiation; execution.
- Month 6–9: TI build-out; furniture and A/V procurement (RFP-managed by Workplace with GC and CFO oversight).
- Month 9–10: soft move-in; hypercare (Workplace on-site for the first 2 weeks); Rippling-managed access badging; IT-managed cabling / meeting-room A/V.

**Lease terms.**
- 5-year term with a landlord-priced 4-year early-termination option.
- 22,000 sqft on a single floor of a Class-A SoMa building (peak-capacity math: 150 Bay Area employees × 65% peak = 100 peak-day attendees; 0.75 desk-share ratio implies 130 desks; plus meeting rooms and common area).
- Base rent US$85/sqft/year, 3% annual escalator.
- TI allowance US$100/sqft (landlord funded), plus 2 months free rent.
- ROFO on contiguous 8,000 sqft on the same floor.
- Sublease and assignment rights, consent not to be unreasonably withheld.
- Letter of credit for security deposit (CFO negotiated the LC facility with the company's bank).

**Cost.** Base rent Year 1 ≈ US$1.87M; total lease obligation over the 5-year term ≈ US$9.9M gross. TI overspend (custom finishes, custom A/V, security integration) US$0.6M funded by the company directly. ASC 842 right-of-use asset ≈ US$9.5M on the balance sheet at commencement.

**Post-signing operating model.** Workplace hire converted from fractional to full-time before signing; hires a facilities coordinator and a workplace-experience coordinator within 90 days of move-in; runs the on-site services (reception, mail, food, meeting-room A/V) with a mix of in-house and vendor.

## Common failure modes

- **No tenant-rep firm.** Company signs directly with a landlord's leasing agent; the agent represents the landlord, not the tenant; the tenant leaves five- to seven-figure concessions on the table.
- **Signing to today's headcount.** 100-employee company signs 15,000 sqft for its current size; 24 months later it is 300 employees and cannot fit, and the ROFO on adjacent space was not negotiated.
- **Committing to a 10-year term at Series A.** A 10-year lease against an early-stage plan is a bet the company will exist in its current form for a decade. The rent concession is not worth the optionality lost.
- **No sublease rights.** The company changes plan and cannot exit; carries a stranded lease for years; the CFO writes it down each quarter.
- **Space designed against average attendance.** Peak-day capacity is chronically insufficient; the hybrid policy erodes; the space is empty on non-peak days and overflowing on peak days.
- **TI over-build.** The tenant builds a signature space with custom finishes that cost 2x the TI allowance; the amortised overage burdens rent for the term; the space becomes hard to sublease because the finishes are company-specific.
- **Workplace under-invested.** Space is signed; no dedicated facilities function; on-site services degrade; the workforce blames Workplace / HR for a facilities problem that was under-staffed from the start.
- **Policy and footprint disagree.** Company announces a hybrid-anchored policy but has a remote-first-sized footprint; employees show up to peak days and cannot find a desk; policy compliance collapses.

## Summary

- The workplace progression: **remote-first (seed) → coworking (Series A) → first direct short-term lease (late A / B) → long-term hub lease (Series B) → hub-and-spoke (growth).** Each stage has a trigger; skipping a stage is expensive.
- **Coworking cost is US$500–1,500 per desk per month** in Tier-1 US markets; per-desk economics tip against direct lease at ~40–50 desks.
- **First direct short-term lease** is typically 3-year, 3,000–5,000 sqft. **Long-term hub lease** is typically 5-year (or longer), 10,000–30,000 sqft with a TI package US$50–150+ per sqft.
- **Tenant-representation firms (CBRE, JLL, Cushman & Wakefield, Newmark, Colliers, Savills, Cresa)** represent the tenant in the transaction; are paid by the landlord (surface the commission arrangement); should be engaged 6–9 months before need.
- **TI negotiation** touches allowance size, turnkey vs. cash, free-rent, amortisation, expansion / contraction rights, early-termination options, and sublease rights.
- **Sublease exit** is the mechanic that turns a stranded lease into a manageable liability. Negotiate the sublease right at signing.
- The **RTO / hybrid policy** ([mod-108](../mod-108-culture-employee-experience-and-dei/)) drives space demand; workplace supplies against **peak-day** attendance, not average. Every policy change has a workplace consequence.
