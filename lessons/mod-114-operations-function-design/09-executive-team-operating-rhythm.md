# 9. Executive-team operating rhythm — the E-team meeting, 1:1s, offsite, 360, onboarding, conflict management

> The executive team is the single most-leveraged group in the company. Fifteen people (CEO plus 8–12 direct reports plus the CoS) make or ratify most of the decisions the company's thousand employees then execute. The team's own operating rhythm — weekly meeting, monthly 1:1s, quarterly offsite, annual 360, onboarding programme — is the mechanism by which the team becomes more than the sum of its executives.

## Motivation

A dysfunctional executive team is diagnosable by week 3 of observing it. The weekly meeting runs long and ends without decisions. Two VPs do not speak to each other outside the meeting room. The CEO has a one-on-one with each executive but never discusses the executives' relationships with each other. The quarterly offsite is a two-day event the whole team dreads, consisting of slide-presentations and strained team-building exercises. The 360 feedback cycle (if it exists at all) produces anodyne scores that reveal nothing. New executives land unprepared and discover six months in that they inherited a political landscape no one briefed them on.

The alternative — a well-run executive-team rhythm — is the thing a Lencioni-trained team coach or a strong CEO can install, and it is a direct competitive advantage. Executive teams that run well out-execute executive teams that have the same talent but no rhythm, and the delta is observable in the operating results.

This chapter authors the rhythm: the weekly meeting agenda and mechanics, the 1:1 cadence between CEO and each executive, the quarterly offsite, the annual 360, the executive-onboarding programme (the 100-day plan), the conflict-management pattern (Lencioni / Horowitz canon), and the interaction with the compensation-committee-owned executive-performance review (see [mod-105](../mod-105-equity-compensation-policy-and-comp-committee/)).

## The E-team weekly meeting

The E-team weekly meeting is the operating heart of the executive team. Running it well is the CoS's single most-visible deliverable.

### Agenda structure

A standard 90-minute agenda (adjust to 60 at Series B, 120 at growth):

- **Open — 5 minutes.** CEO frame. One sentence: "the thing on my mind this week." Not a speech; a pulse.
- **KPIs / dashboard — 15 minutes.** Pre-read via the canonical BizOps dashboard. In-meeting: discussion of yellow / red exceptions only; greens get no air time. The function owner of each exception speaks to the driver and the plan.
- **Decisions — 30 minutes.** 3–5 decisions on the agenda, pre-sourced from the executive team through the week via a shared decisions-queue. Each decision has an owner, a pre-read, a recommendation, and a timeboxed discussion. The group endorses, modifies, or defers with a next step. Decisions-of-the-week get logged by the CoS.
- **Cross-functional blockers — 15 minutes.** Issues that span two or more functions and need the room. The CoS surfaces from the operating-cadence tracking.
- **Strategic topic / deep-dive (rotating) — 20 minutes.** One topic per week — a competitive update, a customer-segment deep-dive, a new initiative review. Not a decision; an alignment conversation. Chairs rotate through the executive team.
- **Close — 5 minutes.** Three questions in sequence: (1) What did we decide today? (2) What are the top three actions coming out of this meeting? (3) What is the single most important thing we did not discuss?

### Running mechanics

- **Pre-reads mandatory, distributed 24 hours in advance.** No pre-read, no agenda spot — the item moves to the next week.
- **Phones away, laptops closed except for shared agenda / dashboard.** The executive team's weekly is the moment the team is in the room with each other. Multitasking signals that this is not the moment.
- **Starts on time, ends on time.** The CEO models this; the CoS enforces it. Overrun is a signal the agenda is wrong.
- **The CoS captures the decision log and action-item log live.** Shared visibility; posted within 24 hours; reviewed at the top of the next meeting.
- **One meeting-norm artifact.** Written down, posted in the meeting room (physical or virtual), reviewed quarterly. Includes: how we handle disagreement, how we handle interruption, how we commit to decisions, how we handle attendance.

### The "topic not people" discipline

The common failure mode is the round-robin update format — each executive speaks for N minutes on their function. This is a 1-on-1 format masquerading as a team meeting. Fix: round-robin updates belong in the CEO–executive 1-on-1 (below). The E-team meeting discusses *topics*, not *updates*.

### Attendance

- **Default attendees:** CEO, CoS (as meeting runner), each functional executive (CRO, CTO, CPO, CFO, CPO-People, GC, Head of Product). The COO if the seat exists.
- **Non-attendees invited by topic:** Head of BizOps for KPI-heavy weeks; Head of Business Systems or Head of Procurement for systems or vendor topics; subject-matter leaders for specific deep-dives.
- **Guests not permitted by default.** Observers are a 1-off ask, approved by the CEO.

### Common failure modes of the E-team weekly

- **Update theatre.** Each executive reports; nothing is decided; nobody listens. Fix: redesign to the topic-and-decision structure.
- **The loudest voice wins.** One or two executives dominate; the rest disengage. Fix: the CoS runs a round of "I need to hear from X and Y specifically on this topic" during contested items.
- **CEO as decision bottleneck.** Every decision gets kicked to the CEO; the meeting ends with "I'll think about it." Fix: Horowitz framing — decisions in the room, with the CEO's judgment but not with the CEO's permission.
- **Rolling the decision forward.** Same decision discussed four weeks in a row with no resolution. Fix: cap at two revisits; if unresolved after two, the CEO decides unilaterally with the information available.
- **Meeting as substitute for 1:1s.** Executives raise individual-function issues in the group; the group becomes a 1-on-1 with an audience. Fix: enforce the topic-not-people discipline; redirect individual-function issues to the 1-on-1.

## The CEO–executive 1:1 cadence

Separate from the E-team meeting, the CEO holds a weekly 1:1 with each executive. The 1:1 is a different artifact.

### What the 1:1 is for

- **Functional update from the executive.** The 10-minute update that would be round-robin in the E-team meeting lives here instead.
- **Decisions that are the executive's and the CEO's exclusively.** Comp decisions for the executive's team; function-specific strategic calls; hiring decisions for the executive's direct reports.
- **The executive's own development, satisfaction, concerns.** The quieter conversation that does not belong in the group.
- **The executive's view of the executive team.** The 1:1 is where the CEO learns which executives are getting on with whom, who is frustrated by whom, who is building and who is defending.
- **The CEO's feedback to the executive.** Explicit, specific, actionable.

### Cadence

- **Weekly, 45–60 minutes.** Standing. Only skipped for genuine conflict (travel, specific coverage days).
- **Written agenda from the executive, shared the day before.** The executive drives the 1:1; the CEO brings their own additions.
- **Running action-item list.** Both sides maintain. Followed through week over week.
- **Quarterly deeper conversation.** One 1:1 per quarter is extended (90 minutes) and covers broader topics — the executive's year-over-year development, the executive's view of their function and of the company, succession planning for the executive's seat.

### What the 1:1 is not for

- Decisions that properly belong in the E-team. Cross-functional calls, strategic-direction calls, decisions that affect other executives' functions.
- Grievances about other executives. Those belong in a mediated conversation (next section).
- Status updates on execution the CEO should be able to see from the operating cadence. If the CEO is learning about operating results in 1:1s instead of in the weekly operating review, the operating review is broken.

### The 1:1 and the operating cadence

The CEO's 1:1s with each executive integrate with the chapter 06 operating cadence. The 1:1 is the function-specific layer; the operating cadence is the cross-functional layer. Both are needed. 1:1s without the cross-functional layer produce executives aligned bilaterally with the CEO but not with each other; the cross-functional layer without 1:1s produces executives who are coordinated but under-coached.

## The quarterly executive-team offsite

Once a quarter, the executive team meets for an offsite — one or two days, off-site location, structured programme, often with external facilitation at growth stage.

### Purposes

The quarterly offsite serves three distinct purposes that compound each other:

1. **Strategic alignment.** A longer-form conversation on strategy than the weekly meeting can accommodate — competitive positioning, customer-segment evolution, product-roadmap trade-offs, capital-allocation choices.
2. **OKRs retrospective and setting.** The quarterly OKRs retrospective (last quarter) and setting (next quarter) from [chapter 06](./06-operating-cadence-and-okrs-rhythm.md). Executive-level, not function-level — the function-level OKRs setting runs separately in the quarter's final weeks.
3. **Team health.** The relationship-level conversation that the weekly meeting cannot carry — how is the team working, what is going well, what is not, what commitments do we make to each other.

### Agenda template (2-day offsite)

**Day 1 — strategy and OKRs.**
- Morning: strategic topic deep-dive. One or two topics; pre-read authored by CEO or by the executive sponsoring the topic.
- Afternoon: OKRs retrospective (scoring prior quarter) + OKRs setting (next quarter).
- Evening: team dinner. Social, not working. (If virtual: cancel the social; replace with a shorter day.)

**Day 2 — team health and planning.**
- Morning: team-health conversation. Structured — Lencioni's team-health exercises or an equivalent framework. Facilitated by a coach at growth stage.
- Afternoon: planning for next quarter — the cross-functional programmes, the executive-team priorities, the risks the team is accepting.
- Close: commitments to each other. Each executive names one thing they commit to doing differently in the coming quarter, and one thing they are counting on from a specific peer.

### Facilitation

- **At Series B / C:** The CEO or CoS runs the offsite. Light structure.
- **At growth stage:** External facilitator for Day 2 (team health). A Lencioni-trained team coach or a specialist executive-team facilitator (common names: The Table Group — Lencioni's own firm; ghSMART; executive-coaching firms with team-practice depth). <!-- needs-research: verify the current practitioner landscape for executive-team facilitation at growth stage; The Table Group and ghSMART are commonly cited but the market is broader. -->

### Common failure modes

- **Offsite as working session with no team-health content.** Two days of planning and no conversation about how the team is working. Team dysfunction compounds. Fix: non-negotiable Day 2 morning on team health.
- **Offsite as team-building theatre.** Trust falls and ropes courses. Executives disengage. Fix: team health is structured conversation, not activity.
- **Offsite with no follow-through.** The commitments made on Day 2 afternoon are forgotten by Week 2. Fix: the CoS puts them on the Monday 9am agenda for the first four weeks post-offsite.
- **Offsite with the wrong people in the room.** CEO brings their coach; adds a board member; invites a candidate; the dynamic shifts. Fix: offsite is the executive team only, except by rare and specific exception.

## The annual executive 360

Once a year, each executive goes through a 360-degree feedback cycle. The 360 is the formal mechanism by which executive-level feedback — from the CEO, from peers, from direct reports, and from the executive themselves — is collected, synthesised, and converted into a development plan.

### Who runs it

- **At Series B/C:** The CPO (Chief People Officer) runs the 360 process; the CEO is the delivering interlocutor for each executive's results; sometimes an external executive coach runs the collection and the delivery.
- **At growth stage:** External executive coach runs each individual 360 (collection, synthesis, delivery). The CPO owns the aggregate programme design. The compensation committee's performance review (see [mod-105](../mod-105-equity-compensation-policy-and-comp-committee/)) uses the 360 as one input among several.

### What the 360 covers

- **Performance against objectives.** Did the executive achieve what they committed to? OKRs scores, operational results, qualitative judgments.
- **Leadership of their function.** Hiring quality, team-building, direct-report development, retention, culture.
- **Peer effectiveness.** How does the executive work with peers? Trust, candour, cross-functional partnership, decision quality in the group.
- **Direct-report experience.** How is the executive experienced by their directs? Managerial quality, feedback discipline, growth support.
- **Self-assessment.** The executive's own view of their year.

### How it integrates

The 360 feeds into:

- **The executive's development plan.** One or two specific areas; concrete coaching or development actions over the following 6–12 months.
- **The CEO's own executive-development planning.** Patterns across executives (the whole team is weak in one dimension; a specific executive is a flight risk) become the CEO's agenda.
- **The compensation committee's executive-performance review** (see [mod-105 chapter 06](../mod-105-equity-compensation-policy-and-comp-committee/) — the exact chapter number depends on mod-105's chapter list; cross-reference confirms the comp committee's role in the annual executive-performance review). <!-- needs-research: confirm the specific chapter path in mod-105 for the comp committee's annual executive-performance review process. -->
- **The succession-planning conversation.** The board's succession planning (CEO succession in particular) uses the 360 as one input.

### Common failure modes

- **360 scores without feedback.** Numerical scores alone, no qualitative commentary; the executive learns nothing actionable. Fix: structured qualitative input (open-ended questions on each dimension) with the numerical scores.
- **360 feedback diluted into harmlessness.** The synthesis removes everything specific; the executive gets anodyne feedback. Fix: coach-run delivery; the coach's job is to hold the specifics.
- **360 as judgment, not development.** The executive treats the 360 as a performance evaluation; defensiveness compounds; no development follows. Fix: separate the 360 from the comp-committee performance review; the 360 is development; the comp review is accountability.
- **360 feedback feeds back to the wrong people.** Direct reports fear retaliation; the feedback is sanitised; the 360 misses the leadership-quality dimension. Fix: anonymity discipline; small-N direct-report groups aggregated with care.

## The executive-onboarding programme (100-day plan)

New executives land with context deficits that materially slow their effectiveness. The 100-day plan is the deliberate onboarding programme that compresses the learning curve.

### Pre-start (weeks −4 to 0)

- **Executive-briefing packet.** Company history, current strategy, current board composition, financial state, operating-cadence calendar, executive-team dynamic (frank), direct-report team details, key cross-functional partners.
- **Pre-start conversations.** Introductory 1:1s with CEO, 2–3 key peers, 1–2 board members. Not full briefings; relationship establishment.
- **Access and tooling.** HRIS onboarding, laptop, SSO, calendars and distribution lists. The IT and People functions coordinate.

### Days 1–30 — listening

- **Listening tour.** 1:1 with every direct report, every executive peer, every key cross-functional partner, and selected individual contributors. 30–45 minute conversations. Open-ended questions: what works, what does not, what should the new executive know, what should they not get surprised by.
- **Operating-cadence immersion.** Attendance at the E-team weekly, the function-level weekly, the MBR, and any in-flight programme reviews. Observer mode primarily.
- **No major decisions.** The executive makes no major directional calls in the first 30 days.

### Days 30–60 — synthesising

- **Synthesis document.** The executive authors a 5–10 page synthesis — what they have heard, what they have observed, what they intend to do, what they are watching. Shared with the CEO and the executive team.
- **Early action.** Low-risk, high-visibility actions that build credibility — unblocking a decision the function has been stuck on, re-opening a conversation that has been avoided, making a specific hire.
- **CEO 1:1 cadence.** Weekly 1:1 established, with explicit framing: the first 90 days are a learning cycle, not a performance window.

### Days 60–100 — committing

- **Function strategy / plan.** By day 100 the executive publishes their function's plan for the next 12 months — priorities, KPIs, hiring, resource ask. Reviewed with the CEO; integrated with the overall operating plan.
- **Executive-team integration.** By day 100 the executive is a full participant in the E-team meeting — contributing to topics, holding decisions, carrying their own cross-functional work.
- **Direct-report assessment.** The executive makes their initial calls on their inherited team — who stays, who needs development, who may need to move. These are directional, not immediate — formal personnel decisions typically wait until past day 100.

### What breaks

- **No briefing packet.** The executive discovers the operating cadence in month 2. 60 days of learning compressed into the executive's own time. Fix: the CoS or CPO authors the briefing packet before the executive starts.
- **Early-pressure decisions.** The executive is pushed into major decisions in the first 30 days (a hire, a reorg, a strategic call). The decisions are made with inadequate information and often reverse later. Fix: enforce the "no major decisions in 30 days" discipline against the pressure.
- **No executive-team integration plan.** The new executive is left to figure out the team dynamics alone. 90 days in, they are still peripheral. Fix: the CEO's job (with the CoS's support) is active integration — introduce the executive, hand off specific cross-functional work, create visibility.
- **Honeymoon too long.** The executive is given 180 days of grace; underperformance becomes entrenched; by month 9 the executive is a problem no one wants to confront. Fix: the 100-day plan has a 100-day review that is honest.

## Conflict management — the Lencioni / Horowitz canon

Executive-team conflict is inevitable. The question is whether it is productive or corrosive. Patrick Lencioni's *The Five Dysfunctions of a Team* (2002) and *The Advantage* (2012), together with Ben Horowitz's writing on the hard moments of leadership, provide the practitioner framework.

### Lencioni's five dysfunctions

Lencioni's model is a pyramid; each dysfunction builds on the one below:

1. **Absence of trust.** Executives do not show vulnerability to each other. Fix: structured vulnerability — Lencioni's "personal histories" exercise, where each executive shares specific personal history with the team.
2. **Fear of conflict.** Without trust, executives avoid open disagreement. The meetings have surface harmony and underlying disagreement that leaks later. Fix: structured conflict — the facilitator forces the disagreement to surface; the team practices disagreeing productively.
3. **Lack of commitment.** Without open disagreement, decisions are not real commitments — people leave the meeting with silent reservations. Fix: explicit commitment — "we have decided X; I will commit to supporting X; even if I disagree, I will not undermine it."
4. **Avoidance of accountability.** Without commitment, executives do not hold each other accountable for the commitments. Fix: peer accountability — the executives call out each other's slips, not just the CEO calling them out.
5. **Inattention to results.** Without accountability, personal and functional priorities displace team results. Fix: shared operating metrics — the team's success is measured by the operating-metric package, not by individual-function success.

The Lencioni practice is dense; a Lencioni-trained team coach can run the full programme over 6–12 months. The 5-minute version: trust, conflict, commitment, accountability, results — each is a precondition for the next.

### Horowitz on hard conversations

Ben Horowitz's writing — *The Hard Thing About Hard Things* (2014) and the a16z writing — emphasises the CEO's responsibility for the hardest conversations. The CEO who avoids the direct conversation (the exec who needs to leave, the co-founder who needs to be renegotiated, the strategic call that will alienate some of the team) is not sparing the team; they are prolonging the dysfunction.

The through-line: the CEO's willingness to be the direct interlocutor on hard conversations is the organisation's single most-leveraged behaviour. Delegation of the hard conversation (through HR, through legal, through the CoS) destroys the trust Lencioni's pyramid is built on.

### The practical pattern

- **Trust-building is explicit, not implicit.** The quarterly offsite's Day 2 structured conversation is the trust-building. "The team will build trust organically through working together" is a wish, not a plan.
- **Conflict is practised.** When an executive disagrees in the meeting, the CoS or CEO explicitly makes space: "Say more." "What is the specific concern?" "What would make you comfortable with this decision?" Not debate for debate's sake — structured disagreement that leads to better decisions.
- **Commitment is verbal and visible.** After a contested decision, the CEO explicitly asks each executive: "Can you commit?" Silence is not consent. Reservations get surfaced before commitment.
- **Accountability is peer, not CEO-only.** When an executive misses a commitment, the first conversation is from a peer, not from the CEO. The CEO backs the pattern.
- **Results are shared.** The executive team's bonus / equity-refresh / promotion architecture rewards team results, not just function results. See [mod-106](../mod-106-compensation-architecture-and-total-rewards/) for the compensation architecture that supports this.

## Interaction with the compensation-committee executive-performance review

The compensation committee owns the formal annual executive-performance review per [mod-105](../mod-105-equity-compensation-policy-and-comp-committee/). The executive-team rhythm this chapter authors feeds into it:

- **The 360 output** is one input to the comp committee's review. Not the only input (operating results, business judgment, strategic contributions are others); but a formal one.
- **The CEO's own annual review** is a comp-committee-led process (independent directors assess the CEO; the CEO is not a party). The CoS supports the CEO in preparing for the review.
- **The executive-team compensation decisions** (base, bonus, equity refresh) land through the comp committee annually. The CEO's recommendations, informed by the operating-cadence results and the 360 feedback, go to the committee.

The chapter 09 rhythm is the *management* side of the executive team; the comp committee's review is the *governance* side. They coordinate but do not merge.

## Concrete example — the Series-C executive-team rhythm

**Company:** Series-C B2B SaaS, 350 employees, executive team of 9 (CEO + CTO + CRO + Head of Product + CPO-People + CFO + GC + Head of Marketing + COO).

**Weekly rhythm.**
- **Monday 9:00–10:30.** E-team weekly. COO chairs. Standard agenda — open, dashboard exceptions, 3 decisions, blockers, rotating topic, close.
- **CEO–executive 1:1s.** Scheduled across Monday afternoon through Thursday — 45-minute weekly with each executive. Fridays reserved for deep-work and strategic topics.
- **Pre-E-team CEO–CoS prep.** Monday 8:30–9:00. Walk the agenda, flag unresolved items.

**Monthly rhythm.**
- **2nd Thursday MBR.** 2.5 hours. Standard MBR structure per [chapter 06](./06-operating-cadence-and-okrs-rhythm.md).
- **CEO monthly review with CoS.** Last Friday of the month. Calendar audit against stated top-three priorities; CoS development plan check-in; difficult-conversation inventory.

**Quarterly rhythm.**
- **Week 12 of quarter — half-day OKRs retrospective** (E-team-only).
- **Week 1 of next quarter — half-day OKRs setting** (E-team-only, carried to all-hands by Week 2).
- **Mid-quarter check-in in Week 6.**
- **Quarterly offsite** (overlaps with OKRs retrospective / setting + team-health segment) — 2 days, growth-stage executive coach runs Day 2 team health segment.

**Annual rhythm.**
- **November annual E-team offsite — 3 days.** CEO strategy memo distributed in October as pre-read. External coach facilitates Day 1 strategic alignment and Day 3 team-formation; CEO / COO author Day 2 operating-plan content.
- **Annual 360s** — collected in October, delivered to each executive by external coach in November, feeds the comp-committee review in December.
- **Comp-committee annual review** — December. Chair of comp committee leads; CPO prepares the pack; CEO's recommendations on each executive's comp decisions are reviewed and ratified.
- **CEO's own annual review** — January. Independent directors run the review; the lead independent director delivers; the CoS supports the CEO in preparing responses to the review.

**Onboarding (any new executive).**
- Briefing packet 4 weeks before start, authored by CoS and CPO.
- Pre-start conversations with CEO, 2–3 peers, 1 board member.
- 30-day listening tour, 60-day synthesis document, 100-day function plan.
- 100-day review with CEO — honest, documented.

**Conflict management.** When two executives are in conflict, the CoS surfaces the pattern; the CEO holds a mediated conversation; the quarterly offsite's team-health segment addresses the aggregate dynamic. External coaching for specific pairs where the conflict is persistent.

## Common failure modes

- **No separation between E-team weekly and 1:1s.** The weekly meeting becomes a serial 1:1 round-robin; the 1:1 becomes a status update. Both are degraded. Fix: topic-not-people in the weekly; function-and-people content in the 1:1.
- **Offsite without team-health content.** Two days of strategy without any conversation about the team itself. Dysfunction compounds. Fix: non-negotiable Day 2 team-health segment.
- **No 360 or an unserious one.** Executives operate with no cross-feedback mechanism; development is unstructured; the comp-committee review flies blind. Fix: external-coach-run 360s at growth stage.
- **Executive onboarding by osmosis.** New executive given a laptop and a wave at the exit. By month 6 they still do not know the dynamic. Fix: the 100-day plan, with CoS and CPO ownership.
- **Conflict avoidance across the entire executive team.** Everyone is polite; nobody disagrees; decisions are unresolved later over Slack. Fix: Lencioni's structured conflict practice; the facilitator forces the disagreement to surface.
- **CEO as every executive's primary interlocutor.** The CEO holds all nine 1:1s; the executives do not hold productive 1:1s with each other; the team is a hub-and-spoke. Fix: cross-executive 1:1s as part of the rhythm (CRO–CPO weekly; CTO–Head of Product weekly; etc.).
- **No integration with comp committee.** The executive-team rhythm and the comp-committee review are disconnected; the committee reviews executives it has no operational visibility into. Fix: the 360 and the CEO's recommendations are formally delivered to the committee as part of the annual cycle.
- **Rhythm never reviewed.** The pattern is set at Series B and never adjusted. By growth stage the rhythm no longer fits the team shape. Fix: annual rhythm review (what should we add / change / kill?) as part of the annual offsite.

## Summary

- **E-team weekly meeting.** 60–90 minutes; open / dashboard exceptions / 3 decisions / blockers / rotating topic / close. Pre-reads mandatory; topics not people; CoS runs mechanics; CEO chairs.
- **CEO–executive 1:1s.** Weekly, 45–60 minutes, executive-driven agenda, written action items, quarterly deeper conversation. Separate from the E-team meeting in purpose and content.
- **Quarterly executive-team offsite.** 2 days; strategic alignment + OKRs retro/setting + team health. External facilitation at growth stage; non-negotiable team-health segment on Day 2.
- **Annual executive 360.** CPO-run programme; external-coach delivery at growth stage; feeds development plans and the comp-committee review.
- **Executive-onboarding programme.** 100-day plan — briefing packet, listening tour, synthesis document, function plan. The CPO and CoS own the structure; the CEO owns the integration.
- **Conflict management (Lencioni / Horowitz canon).** Trust → productive conflict → commitment → accountability → results. CEO owns the direct conversations; the CoS surfaces the overdue ones.
- **Interaction with the compensation committee.** The 360 and the CEO's recommendations are formal inputs to the committee's annual executive-performance review (per [mod-105](../mod-105-equity-compensation-policy-and-comp-committee/)). Management and governance coordinate but do not merge.
- The chapter 06 operating cadence is the *cross-functional* operating layer; this chapter's rhythm is the *executive-team* operating layer. They interlock through the weekly meeting (shared) and through the quarterly offsite (where the strategic + team-health conversations happen together).
