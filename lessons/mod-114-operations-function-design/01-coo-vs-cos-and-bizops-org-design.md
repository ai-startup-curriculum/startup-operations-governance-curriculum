# 1. Designing the operations function — COO, Chief of Staff, BizOps, Workplace

> The operations function is not one seat with different labels. It is at least four seats — COO, Chief of Staff, BizOps, and Workplace — with different remits, different reporting lines, different decision rights, and different hire triggers. Getting the org design right before you fill the seats is the difference between a scale-up that runs and a scale-up that just holds meetings about running.

## Motivation

Ask ten Series-A CEOs "who runs operations?" and you will get ten different answers. Some point at the CFO. Some point at a Chief of Staff hired six weeks ago. Some point at themselves. A few point at nobody and admit that no one owns the operating cadence, the systems roadmap, the workplace footprint, the vendor list, or the executive-team rhythm — those things happen when they happen, run by the last person to touch them.

That answer works until roughly 50–75 employees. Past that, the CEO is spending an increasing fraction of every week on operating machinery the CEO is uniquely wrong to run — vendor-consolidation calls, HRIS migration timelines, workplace lease negotiations, comp-planning process design — and the executive team is missing a peer whose job is to make the org itself run. That is the seat this chapter is about.

This chapter maps the operations function's org design at Series-A / B / growth: **who the seats are, what they own, what they don't own, when they get hired, and how they interlock with the CPO / GC / CFO seats owned by other modules.** Sequencing the first ten hires against comp benchmarks is [chapter 02](./02-first-ten-ops-hires-and-comp-benchmarks.md). The systems, workplace, procurement, and operating-cadence chapters that follow are the *content* the seats designed here will run.

## The four operations seats

The operations function at a scale-up separates into four distinct seats. In a small company one person may hold two or three of them; the seats are still distinct roles with distinct decision rights, and merging them muddies both.

### Seat 1 — Chief of Staff (CoS)

The **Chief of Staff** is an internal-consulting / strategic-partner-to-CEO seat. The CoS does not run a line function. The CoS *serves* the CEO and the executive team by absorbing the work the CEO would otherwise do personally and by running the cross-functional programmes that fall between the executive team's line silos.

Core CoS remit:

- **CEO's operating tempo.** Prioritising the CEO's calendar and preparation; running the CEO's inbox for anything that can be triaged; drafting communications the CEO signs; scheduling deep-work blocks against the pressure to fill them with meetings.
- **Executive-team meeting choreography.** Owns the E-team weekly agenda, the pre-read discipline, the follow-through tracking, and the offsite planning. See [chapter 09](./09-executive-team-operating-rhythm.md).
- **Board-preparation partnership.** Partners with the CFO (who owns the *content* of the pack) on the meeting choreography, the pre-read timing, and the follow-through on board-directed action items. Coordinates with the corporate secretary function ([mod-111 chapter 03](../mod-111-corporate-governance-board-operations-and-officer-duties/03-corporate-secretary-function.md)).
- **Cross-functional special projects.** The strategic-topic deep dives that don't fit inside any one function — pricing model changes, org restructures, category-entry decisions, executive searches, acquisition diligence. The CoS runs the project on the CEO's behalf, sourcing analysts from BizOps and subject-matter input from the line functions.
- **Communications discipline.** All-hands preparation, board-update drafting, investor-update editing, key-hire-announcement rollouts.

The CoS is **not** an executive. The CoS reports to the CEO. The CoS has no P&L, no direct-report headcount beyond a small BizOps pod (if any), and no unilateral decision authority over line functions. The CoS's power is derived from the CEO's — expressed as convening authority, agenda-setting authority, and pre-read authority over what reaches the CEO's desk.

The CoS role has been variously described in the Silicon Valley canon (Tyler Parris's *Chief of Staff: The Strategic Partner Who Will Revolutionize Your Organization*; the White House Chief of Staff literature by Chris Whipple; the Chief of Staff Association's community practice) and in the operator narratives of scaled companies. The consistent through-line is that the seat is defined by proximity to the principal, not by a line remit. <!-- needs-research: verify the Tyler Parris and Chris Whipple citations and confirm the Chief of Staff Association's canonical framing for a private-sector CoS role. -->

### Seat 2 — Chief Operating Officer (COO)

The **COO** is a full operating-leadership seat with P&L and a line remit. The COO is a peer of the CEO and typically a peer to (or supervisor of) other C-suite executives depending on the org design.

Core COO remit — variant by company but drawn from a common vocabulary:

- **Owns the operating rhythm.** Weekly ops review, monthly business review, quarterly OKRs cycle, annual planning — the COO designs, chairs, and enforces the cadence. See [chapter 06](./06-operating-cadence-and-okrs-rhythm.md).
- **Owns the go-to-market operating engine — usually.** In the most common "commercial COO" pattern, the COO owns Sales, Customer Success, Marketing, and Rev-Ops directly, freeing the CEO to work on strategy, capital, product, and executive team. In the alternative "operations COO" pattern, the COO owns internal operations (BizOps, Workplace, IT, Business Systems, sometimes People and Finance) and the CRO owns the commercial engine. Which pattern fits depends on where the CEO's comparative advantage is and where the gap on the executive team sits.
- **Owns cross-functional execution.** Anything that touches multiple functions and needs a single owner — pricing changes, packaging changes, ICP redefinition, category-entry, geographic-expansion — the COO runs.
- **Owns talent decisions in the operating org.** Hires, promotes, and (with the People function) manages performance for the functions in the COO's line.
- **Board-facing.** The COO presents operating results at every board meeting alongside the CEO and CFO; may hold committee memberships depending on structure.

The COO is an executive with independent authority, not a proxy for the CEO. Where the CoS's decisions get reversed by "the CEO says no," the COO's decisions stand unless overturned in an explicit executive-team debate.

Ben Horowitz's *The Hard Thing About Hard Things* frames the COO decision as the CEO's most consequential structural choice — a well-fit COO doubles the CEO's operating capacity; a poorly-fit COO or a premature COO fragments the org. Frank Slootman's *Amp It Up* frames the same seat as the enforcer of operating discipline that lets a CEO stay in the strategic saddle. <!-- needs-research: verify the specific framings from Horowitz's *The Hard Thing About Hard Things* on the COO role and Slootman's *Amp It Up* on the operating-discipline seat. -->

### Seat 3 — Head of BizOps (Business Operations)

**BizOps** is the analytical-and-programme-management arm of the operations function. A BizOps team typically houses four sub-disciplines that are related but distinct:

- **Strategic Finance.** Deep financial modelling, scenario planning, unit-economic analysis, pricing analysis. Coordinated with the CFO's FP&A team — the practical split varies by company (in some orgs Strategic Finance sits inside Finance; in others it sits inside BizOps as an insight-generation function, with FP&A as the systems-of-record function). Owned by the finance/fundraising curriculum for the modelling *methods*; owned here for the *placement* on the org chart.
- **Analytics / Insights.** Business analytics — funnel analysis, retention analysis, ICP analysis, market analysis — as distinct from product analytics (owned by Product) and marketing analytics (owned by Marketing). The BizOps analytics team is the source of truth for the KPIs the executive team looks at.
- **Program Management (PMO).** Cross-functional programme management for major initiatives — a pricing rollout, a systems migration, an org restructure. The PMO owns the plan, the milestones, the risk log, and the weekly programme review.
- **Special Projects.** The projects that don't fit anywhere — an M&A diligence, a category-entry, an executive search, a workplace expansion, a competitive war-room. Whoever on the BizOps bench has the bandwidth and the fit picks it up.

BizOps is typically **2–5 people at Series B** and grows to **10–20 people at growth stage.** The head of BizOps often *is* the Chief of Staff at Series A/B (the seats fuse in a small company); they separate as the org scales and the CoS's remit shifts toward the CEO's personal operating tempo while the head of BizOps runs the analytical-and-programme team.

Reporting line: usually to the CoS at early stages (when the seats fuse); to the COO at growth stages; occasionally to the CFO in a finance-heavy variant. Pick one line, document it, and hold to it — BizOps that reports to no one becomes a floating consulting team the CEO uses as a personal analyst pool.

### Seat 4 — Head of Workplace / Facilities / Real Estate

**Workplace** is a distinct seat from the moment the company holds a physical lease and has more than one office. Before that, the CoS or the head of People runs it as a side responsibility. Once you have (a) a direct lease of >5,000 sqft, (b) two or more physical locations, or (c) an active RTO / hybrid policy the workforce is executing against, Workplace is a full seat with its own remit.

Core Workplace remit:

- **Real estate strategy and lease negotiation.** Site selection, tenant-representation firm management (CBRE, JLL, Cushman & Wakefield, Newmark, Savills, Colliers — see [chapter 04](./04-workplace-and-real-estate-progression.md)), lease negotiation, tenant-improvement (TI) design and construction management, sublease and expansion optionality.
- **Facilities operations.** Day-to-day building operations — reception, mail, meeting-room A/V, security, janitorial, food service. Vendor management for each.
- **Workplace experience.** The physical expression of the culture and hybrid policy the People function owns. Workplace runs the space; People owns the policy.
- **Health, safety, and workplace-security compliance.** OSHA compliance, building fire-code compliance, physical-security programme (badging, camera coverage, incident response). Coordinates with the security function on physical-security posture.
- **Sustainability and workplace-ESG.** Where the company has an ESG commitment, workplace is the site of most of it — energy sourcing, waste, water, commuting.

Workplace typically reports to the COO once one exists, to the CoS in the CoS-only pre-COO era, or (occasionally) to the CFO in a real-estate-heavy company. Reporting to People is a common early-stage default that stops working as soon as the lease exposure crosses a few million dollars — Workplace's decisions are capital decisions and belong with an operator, not with an HR generalist.

## The COO vs. Chief-of-Staff decision at Series A/B

The most common structural question a CEO faces at Series A/B is: **do I hire a Chief of Staff, a COO, or both — and in what order?** The answer turns on three variables.

### Variable 1 — what is the CEO's comparative advantage, and where is the gap?

A **product-and-strategy CEO** — a founder whose comparative advantage is category vision, product decisions, and the top-of-funnel of the executive team — usually has a gap on operating discipline. That CEO's first ops hire is often a **COO** (or a **Chief of Staff who is on track to become COO**) whose job is to install and enforce the operating rhythm.

A **commercial CEO** — a founder whose comparative advantage is sales, GTM, and customer relationships — usually has a gap on internal execution. That CEO's first ops hire is often a **Chief of Staff** whose job is to run the internal operating machinery the CEO does not naturally attend to, plus a strong CFO to backstop the finance function.

An **operator CEO** — a founder who is themselves an operating executive — often does not hire a COO at all in the early years, because that CEO is the COO. The first ops hire is a **Chief of Staff** to absorb the tempo work and a **BizOps analyst** to feed the CEO analytical capacity.

### Variable 2 — what is the CEO's willingness to delegate line authority?

The COO seat requires the CEO to hand over line authority for something — GTM, internal operations, or both. If the CEO is unwilling to do that (which is common; founder-CEOs often intellectually accept the need for a COO and behaviourally struggle to delegate), the COO will fail regardless of fit. In that case the CoS seat is the honest answer: a CoS derives authority from the CEO's, so a CoS whose decisions get overturned still functions; a COO whose decisions get overturned does not.

The Horowitz framing is: hire a COO only when you are ready to be a *chair-and-strategist* CEO instead of an *operating* CEO. If you are not ready, hire a CoS and revisit the COO question in twelve months.

### Variable 3 — what is the stage and shape of the executive team?

- **Series A, executive team of 3–5 (CEO, CTO, CRO or Head of Sales, Head of People or Head of Finance).** A COO is usually premature — the org is small enough that the CEO can and should be running it, and the executive team is not yet dense enough to warrant a COO layer. **CoS is the right first hire.**
- **Series B, executive team of 5–8, headcount 75–150.** COO becomes viable and often correct. **CoS-to-COO conversion** is one common path (the CoS spent 18 months earning line credibility and moves into a COO seat with GTM or internal-ops); **external COO hire** is another. **Ops hire a COO, hire a CoS underneath the COO, or both** — the pattern depends on the CEO's variable 1/2 answers above.
- **Series C+, executive team of 8–12, headcount 250+.** COO is standard. **CoS** is a distinct seat serving the CEO. **BizOps** is a distinct team of 5–15 reporting to the COO (or to the CoS, or to a BizOps head who reports to the COO). **Workplace** is a distinct seat.

### Variable 4 — what does the fundraising narrative require?

A CEO whose board is asking "who is going to run this thing when you are on the road for the next round?" is being told to hire a COO. A CEO whose board is asking "who is going to make you five hours a week more productive?" is being told to hire a CoS. The narrative pressure is a signal about how the board sees the CEO's gap, and the pressure is worth taking seriously even if you disagree with the diagnosis — the fundraising conversation will keep returning to it until the seat is filled.

## The Chief-of-Staff-to-COO graduation path

A common and often-correct pattern is to hire a strong Chief of Staff at Series A with the explicit understanding that the seat is a **COO-in-training** role. Over 12–24 months the CoS:

- Runs the operating rhythm end-to-end (weekly ops review, monthly business review, quarterly OKRs — see [chapter 06](./06-operating-cadence-and-okrs-rhythm.md)).
- Runs at least one major cross-functional programme to completion — a systems migration, a workplace expansion, a pricing rollout.
- Builds and manages a small BizOps pod (2–4 analysts).
- Sits in on executive-team meetings as a participant (not just a scribe) and demonstrates decision-making judgement on cross-functional trade-offs.
- Represents the CEO in specific external interactions — a partner meeting, a customer escalation, a candidate conversation.

At the end of that window the CEO and the CoS make an honest call: is the CoS ready to take a P&L or an operating line? If yes, the CoS graduates into a COO seat (or a functional-VP seat — Head of Operations, VP People, VP Business Systems — as a stepping stone). If no, the CoS either continues as CoS at a higher scope, moves to a different function that fits better, or exits with a clean transition.

The graduation-path model is common enough to be a hiring norm — many CoS candidates take the role precisely because it is understood as a bridge to a COO or VP seat. The corollary is that a CoS role that is *not* a bridge should say so in the JD, so the candidate is not silently misaligned with the seat.

## Coordination with CPO, GC, and CFO

The operations function does not exist alone. It coordinates with three peer C-suite functions owned by other modules of this track and by adjacent tracks:

### Coordination with the Chief People Officer (CPO / VP People)

The **CPO owns the People function** — hiring machinery, onboarding, performance, compensation architecture, culture and employee experience, DEI, employee relations, workforce planning. Everything the workforce experiences as *how the company treats me* is the CPO's product.

The COO / CoS coordinates with the CPO on:

- **Workforce planning as an input to operating cadence.** The COO's quarterly plan and the CPO's headcount plan must agree; the CFO holds the numbers.
- **Workplace policy interface.** The CPO owns the RTO / hybrid policy; Workplace owns the physical space that operationalises it. Policy changes trigger space changes and vice versa.
- **Comp and equity architecture handoff.** Executive-comp decisions and the compensation-committee interaction sit at the CPO / CFO / comp-committee boundary — see [mod-105](../mod-105-equity-compensation-policy-and-comp-committee/) and [mod-106](../mod-106-compensation-architecture-and-total-rewards/).
- **Performance-management calendar interface.** The performance / promotion cycle owned by the CPO ([mod-107](../mod-107-performance-promotion-and-offboarding/)) must interlock with the OKRs cycle owned by operations — the two calendars agree on the quarterly / annual anchors.

This module does **not** own the People function or its content. It owns the *interface* between People and Operations.

### Coordination with the General Counsel (GC)

The **GC owns the legal function** — commercial contracts, IP, corporate governance, employment-law, privacy, disputes, and the corporate-secretary role. See [mod-109](../mod-109-commercial-contracts-ip-and-legal-ops/), [mod-110](../mod-110-privacy-data-governance-and-sector-compliance/), and [mod-111](../mod-111-corporate-governance-board-operations-and-officer-duties/).

The COO / CoS coordinates with the GC on:

- **Systems selection with a legal / privacy / security review dependency.** Every material systems purchase in this module ([chapter 03](./03-systems-selection-meta-framework.md)) has a legal review step — DPA, sub-processor register, SCC / IDTA / DPF, security addendum, MSA.
- **Vendor and procurement contracting.** Procurement ([chapter 05](./05-procurement-operating-model.md)) runs the commercial motion; the GC owns the contract-quality standard.
- **Workplace lease negotiation.** Real-estate counsel (external, retained under GC oversight) handles the lease document; Workplace runs the commercial negotiation with the tenant-rep firm.
- **Board-preparation partnership.** The CoS coordinates with the corporate secretary function ([mod-111 chapter 03](../mod-111-corporate-governance-board-operations-and-officer-duties/03-corporate-secretary-function.md)) on meeting choreography.

### Coordination with the Chief Financial Officer (CFO)

The **CFO owns the finance function** — accounting, controllership, FP&A, treasury, tax, financial systems, month-end close, audit, and fundraising execution. That is owned by the `startup-finance-fundraising-curriculum` track, not by this module.

The COO / CoS coordinates with the CFO on:

- **Operating cadence and the close cycle.** The monthly business review lands after the monthly close — the COO's cadence and the CFO's close cycle must be sequenced so that the MBR uses closed-book numbers. See [chapter 06](./06-operating-cadence-and-okrs-rhythm.md) and the finance-curriculum monthly-close chapter. <!-- needs-research: confirm the exact chapter path in startup-finance-fundraising-curriculum for the monthly close cycle chapter (referenced in the module spec as mod-111). -->
- **Systems selection interface.** ERP, GL, AP, expense-management, and procurement-adjacent finance systems are the CFO's call, with the COO / Head of Business Systems as a stakeholder on the integration side. Non-finance systems (HRIS, CLM, board portal, IT-asset management, workplace management) are the COO's call, with the CFO as a stakeholder on the cost and TCO side.
- **Board-pack authorship split.** CFO owns the pack content; the CoS owns the meeting choreography around it ([mod-111 chapter 02](../mod-111-corporate-governance-board-operations-and-officer-duties/02-board-cadence-and-materials.md)).
- **Strategic Finance placement.** Whether Strategic Finance sits inside Finance or inside BizOps is a company-specific call — the two seats must agree on it and hold to a clear line.

## Example org designs by stage

Three representative org designs, drawn from the pattern-recognition of Series A / B / growth companies:

### Series A, ~35 employees

- **CEO** — running the operating rhythm personally with a weekly staff meeting and an ad-hoc close-of-quarter review.
- **CTO / VP Product** — line functions.
- **VP Sales** — line function.
- **VP People** — HR of one to a small team.
- **VP Finance** or fractional CFO — finance of one plus outsourced controller.
- **No dedicated ops seat yet.** The CEO's EA handles calendar and travel; the CFO handles vendor contracts and systems purchases by exception.

Trigger to hire the first ops seat: headcount past ~40, executive team crossing 5 direct reports, or CEO reporting that they are spending >50% of week on operating machinery.

### Series B, ~120 employees

- **CEO**
- **CTO** and **VP Product** — line functions.
- **CRO** — line function, GTM.
- **CPO / VP People** — People function team of 4–6.
- **CFO** — Finance team of 5–8 (Controller, FP&A, AP/AR, Tax).
- **GC** or **Head of Legal** — Legal team of 2–3.
- **Chief of Staff** (new) — reporting to CEO; runs operating rhythm; runs one BizOps analyst; runs cross-functional programmes.
- **Head of Workplace** (contract or fractional) — running the coworking-to-first-lease transition.

Total ops function: 2–3 people (CoS + 1–2 BizOps analysts + fractional Workplace).

### Growth stage, ~500 employees, multi-office

- **CEO**
- **COO** — full operating remit; GTM or internal ops depending on split with the CRO.
- **CTO / CPO / CRO / CFO / CPO-People / GC** — the executive team.
- **Chief of Staff to the CEO** — distinct from BizOps, reports to CEO.
- **Head of BizOps** — reports to COO; team of 8–15 (Strategic Finance, Analytics, PMO, Special Projects).
- **Head of Workplace** — reports to COO; team of 5–10 (real estate, facilities, workplace experience, workplace security). Multi-office footprint.
- **Head of Business Systems** — reports to COO or CTO depending on the org's IT / Business Systems split; team of 5–10.
- **Head of Procurement** — reports to COO or CFO; team of 3–5.

Total ops function: 25–40 people across BizOps, Workplace, Business Systems, and Procurement, with the COO as the C-suite representative and the CoS as the CEO's direct partner.

## Common failure modes

- **CoS as glorified EA.** The seat is filled with someone whose actual work is calendar management and travel booking. The strategic partnership never develops; the CEO does not gain leverage. Fix: hire the CoS at a senior level with an operating-project remit and pair with a separate EA.
- **CoS with no line credibility.** The CoS is a former consultant with no operating experience. Line functions ignore CoS-run programmes because "you have never done this job." Fix: hire CoS candidates with operating experience in at least one relevant function, or partner a strategy-heavy CoS with a line executive as programme co-owners.
- **Premature COO.** A COO is hired at Series A into a 40-person org where the CEO is not ready to hand over authority. The COO's decisions get overturned, the executive team learns to route around the COO, and the seat degrades. Fix: hire a CoS instead until the COO trigger is real.
- **Fused CoS + BizOps head + Workplace at 200 people.** The seats stay fused too long, the CoS is doing four different jobs, and none of them get done well. Fix: split the seats at the trigger points above.
- **Workplace reporting to People forever.** Workplace stays under the CPO after the lease crosses $5M / year, and capital decisions get made by an HR generalist without commercial-real-estate intuition. Fix: move Workplace to the COO once the lease exposure is meaningful.
- **BizOps with no reporting line.** BizOps floats between the CEO, COO, and CFO as a shared team, and nobody owns its priorities. Fix: pick a line, document it, and hold to it.

## Summary

- The operations function separates into four distinct seats — **Chief of Staff, COO, Head of BizOps, Head of Workplace** — with different remits, reporting lines, and hire triggers.
- **CoS** is a strategic-partner-to-CEO seat with no line authority. **COO** is a full operating-leadership seat with P&L. The choice between them at Series A/B turns on the CEO's comparative advantage, willingness to delegate, executive-team shape, and fundraising-narrative pressure.
- **BizOps** houses Strategic Finance, Analytics, Program Management, and Special Projects — typically 2–5 people at Series B, 10–20 at growth stage. Reports to CoS at early stage; to COO at growth.
- **Workplace** becomes a distinct seat once the company holds a direct lease of >5,000 sqft, has two or more locations, or is running an active RTO / hybrid policy. Reports to COO once one exists.
- The CoS-to-COO graduation path is common and often correct — treat the CoS role explicitly as a bridge (or explicitly not as one) at hire.
- Coordination with the **CPO** (workforce planning, policy interface), the **GC** (legal review of systems / procurement / real estate), and the **CFO** (close cycle / systems interface / board-pack authorship) is where operations either amplifies or fragments the executive team. Draw the coordination lines explicitly.
