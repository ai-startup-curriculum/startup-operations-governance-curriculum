# 4. HIPAA compliance for healthtech — Covered Entity vs. Business Associate, the BAA, Security Rule, and the HITECH breach clock

> HIPAA is not a privacy statute of the same shape as GDPR or the CCPA. It is a sector regime that attaches the moment the corporation touches Protected Health Information on behalf of a hospital, a health plan, or a clearinghouse — and once it attaches, the Business Associate Agreement, the Security Rule, the Privacy Rule, and the HITECH breach clock all run on their own calendars, on top of everything else the corporation already owes under GDPR, CCPA, and the mod-109 commercial-contract stack.

## Motivation

A general-purpose Series-B SaaS corporation does not need to read 45 C.F.R. The moment the same corporation closes a deal with a hospital system, a health plan, a telehealth platform, or a medical-device OEM that hands it Protected Health Information (PHI) to process on the customer's behalf, its regulatory topology changes overnight. The customer is a Covered Entity under HIPAA; the corporation becomes a Business Associate; the Office for Civil Rights at the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS OCR) is now the enforcement authority sitting over the relationship; a Business Associate Agreement under 45 C.F.R. § 164.504(e) has to be countersigned before PHI flows; the Security Rule administrative, physical, and technical safeguards at 45 C.F.R. §§ 164.308 / 164.310 / 164.312 become controls the corporation has to be able to demonstrate on audit; the Privacy Rule's minimum-necessary standard at § 164.502(b) becomes a product-engineering constraint; and the HITECH Breach Notification Rule at 45 C.F.R. §§ 164.400–414 drops a 60-day individual-notification clock on the corporation from the moment it discovers an unsecured-PHI breach.

None of that displaces the GDPR obligations the corporation already carries for its EEA customers ([chapter 01](./01-gdpr-compliance-programme.md)) or the CCPA / state-privacy configuration it runs for its US consumer surface ([chapter 03](./03-ccpa-cpra-and-us-state-privacy-patchwork.md)). HIPAA sits *on top of* those regimes for the healthtech subset of the customer base — and the layered analysis is itself a core deliverable, because a dual-scope SaaS that processes EEA PHI under a BAA simultaneously owes GDPR Article 28 processor duties, the § 1798.145(c) HIPAA carve-out has interpretive edges for non-PHI consumer data that the same entity handles, and the DPA authored under [mod-109 chapter 01](../mod-109-commercial-contracts-ip-and-legal-ops/01-customer-contract-suite-msa-sla-dpa-security-aup.md) has to hold both commitments in one contract vehicle without contradiction.

This chapter owns the HIPAA regulatory substance for healthtech-adjacent SaaS: the Covered Entity vs. Business Associate boundary, the BAA content list, the Security Rule, the Privacy Rule, the HITECH breach-notification clock, the audit-trail and PHI-access-logging discipline, and the layered HIPAA-on-top-of-GDPR / CCPA posture. Security-engineering controls behind the Security Rule defer to `security-learning` and `ai-infra-security-solutions`; the internal AI acceptable-use policy that governs employees handling PHI defers to [mod-108](../mod-108-culture-employee-experience-and-dei/); the cyber-insurance tower that pays for a PHI-breach response defers to [mod-112 chapter 03](../mod-112-enterprise-risk-insurance-and-compliance/03-cyber-insurance-and-incident-response-coordination.md); the board-level reporting of the HIPAA compliance programme defers to [mod-111](../mod-111-corporate-governance-board-operations-and-officer-duties/).

## Core concepts

### The Covered Entity vs. Business Associate boundary under 45 C.F.R. § 160.103

HIPAA attaches to two populations of regulated persons, and only those two.

**The three Covered Entity types.** Under 45 C.F.R. § 160.103, a Covered Entity is one of:

1. A **health plan** — an individual or group plan that provides or pays the cost of medical care. This includes HMOs, Medicare, Medicaid, and employer-sponsored group health plans above the small-plan threshold.
2. A **healthcare clearinghouse** — an entity that translates health-information transactions between standard and non-standard formats.
3. A **healthcare provider** that transmits health information in electronic form in connection with a HIPAA Transaction — claim submission, eligibility verification, referral authorisation, remittance advice, and the other transactions listed at 45 C.F.R. Part 162.

A healthcare provider that transmits only on paper or only by fax is technically not a Covered Entity, but in practice the electronic-transaction test is met by nearly every operating provider.

**The Business Associate definition.** A Business Associate is, also under § 160.103, a person or entity that creates, receives, maintains, or transmits PHI on behalf of a Covered Entity for a function regulated by HIPAA — or that provides specified services to a Covered Entity involving the use or disclosure of PHI (legal, actuarial, accounting, consulting, data aggregation, management, administrative, accreditation, or financial). The corporation that sells a SaaS product to a hospital system and processes PHI inside the product on behalf of the hospital is a Business Associate by definition. There is no opt-out.

**The sub-Business Associate chain.** HITECH amended § 160.103 so that subcontractors of a Business Associate that create, receive, maintain, or transmit PHI on behalf of the Business Associate are themselves Business Associates (so-called sub-Business Associates). This generates a flow-down chain:

> Covered Entity → Business Associate → sub-Business Associate → sub-sub-Business Associate

Each tier owes the full suite of Business Associate obligations under the Privacy Rule, the Security Rule, and the Breach Notification Rule, and each tier must sign a BAA with the tier above it.

**The conduit exception.** Under HHS guidance interpreting § 160.103, entities that merely transport PHI without accessing it other than on a random or infrequent basis — the US Postal Service, UPS, a courier service, or an ISP acting purely as a packet-switching transport — are *not* Business Associates. The conduit exception does **not** cover cloud-storage or SaaS providers that persist PHI at rest, even if the provider is encrypted and does not actually read the data. HHS's 2016 Cloud Computing Guidance makes this explicit: a cloud-service provider that stores PHI is a Business Associate and must sign a BAA, zero-knowledge encryption notwithstanding. The practitioner rule is that if PHI persists on the corporation's systems for more than a transient moment, the corporation is a Business Associate — treating itself as a conduit is a losing audit position.

### The Business Associate Agreement — required content under 45 C.F.R. § 164.504(e)(2)

The BAA is the contract that papers the Business Associate relationship and operationalises the flow-down of HIPAA obligations. 45 C.F.R. § 164.504(e)(2) enumerates the required content. The BAA must:

- Establish the permitted and required **uses and disclosures of PHI** by the Business Associate — typically limited to the services described in the underlying services agreement plus the Business Associate's own management, administration, and legal responsibilities.
- Prohibit the Business Associate from using or further disclosing PHI other than as permitted by the BAA or as required by law.
- Require the Business Associate to implement **appropriate safeguards** (the Security Rule administrative / physical / technical safeguards for electronic PHI, and reasonable safeguards for all PHI under the Privacy Rule).
- Require the Business Associate to **report to the Covered Entity** any use or disclosure of PHI not provided for by the BAA, any security incident, and any breach of unsecured PHI under § 164.410 — within a timeframe the BAA specifies (the regulation says "without unreasonable delay," practitioner-canon BAAs land between five and fifteen calendar days from discovery, with the outer bound of the Business Associate's own § 164.410 60-day clock to the Covered Entity).
- Require the Business Associate to ensure that any **subcontractor** that creates, receives, maintains, or transmits PHI on behalf of the Business Associate agrees to the same restrictions and conditions — the sub-BAA flow-down.
- Require the Business Associate to make PHI available to the individual to meet the Covered Entity's § 164.524 **access** obligations, to make PHI available for **amendment** under § 164.526, and to make an **accounting of disclosures** available under § 164.528.
- Require the Business Associate to make its internal practices, books, and records relating to the use and disclosure of PHI available to **HHS OCR** for the Covered Entity's compliance determination.
- At termination, require the Business Associate to **return or destroy** all PHI, or if return / destruction is infeasible, to extend the BAA's protections to the retained PHI and limit further use to the purposes that make return / destruction infeasible.
- Authorise the Covered Entity to **terminate the BAA** if the Business Associate materially breaches it and the breach is not cured (or the BAA is not terminated) within a reasonable period.

HHS publishes a **sample BAA** that implements the § 164.504(e)(2) list; the sample is not a safe-harbour (the parties are responsible for drafting a BAA that matches their actual processing) but it is a useful starting template, particularly for a healthtech first signing a BAA with a sophisticated hospital-system customer who will produce their own counter-template.

The BAA is a schedule to the DPA, not a substitute. The mod-109 DPA ([mod-109 chapter 01](../mod-109-commercial-contracts-ip-and-legal-ops/01-customer-contract-suite-msa-sla-dpa-security-aup.md)) still governs GDPR and CCPA processor obligations; the BAA is added as a healthcare-specific schedule and the DPA / BAA interaction is called out explicitly — PHI is processed under the BAA, non-PHI EEA personal data flows through the DPA's Article 28 SCC stack, and the DPA states which document controls on the PHI subset (the BAA, because it is the statutory instrument). The order-of-precedence clause in the MSA must list the BAA.

### The Security Rule under 45 C.F.R. Part 164 Subpart C

The Security Rule governs the security of **electronic PHI (ePHI)** — the subset of PHI maintained or transmitted in electronic form. Covered Entities and Business Associates owe the same substantive Security Rule obligations after HITECH. The rule is organised into three categories of safeguards.

**Administrative safeguards at § 164.308** — the programme-level controls. The required elements:

- A documented **security management process** — risk analysis, risk management plan, sanction policy, and information-system-activity review.
- **Assigned security responsibility** — a named Security Officer.
- **Workforce security** — authorisation, clearance, and termination procedures.
- **Information access management** — role-based access, access authorisation, access modification.
- **Security awareness and training** — periodic security updates, log-in monitoring, password management.
- A **security-incident procedure**.
- A **contingency plan** — data-backup plan, disaster-recovery plan, emergency-mode-operation plan, testing and revision, applications-and-data criticality analysis.
- A **periodic evaluation** of security posture against the rule.
- The **Business Associate contract requirement** that loops back to § 164.504(e).

**Physical safeguards at § 164.310** — the physical-environment controls. The required elements:

- **Facility access controls** — contingency operations, facility security plan, access-control-and-validation procedures, maintenance records.
- **Workstation use** standards and **workstation security** controls.
- **Device-and-media controls** — disposal, media re-use, accountability, data backup and storage.

For a cloud-hosted healthtech, the physical safeguards largely inherit from the cloud provider's own HIPAA-aligned attestations (AWS, Azure, and GCP all publish HIPAA-eligibility matrices and sign BAAs for the covered services); the corporation's own physical safeguards narrow to its office environment, endpoint fleet, and any on-premises devices.

**Technical safeguards at § 164.312** — the system-level controls. The required elements:

- **Access control** — unique user identification, emergency-access procedure, automatic logoff, encryption-and-decryption.
- **Audit controls** — hardware, software, and / or procedural mechanisms that record and examine activity in systems that contain or use ePHI.
- **Integrity** — protection against improper alteration or destruction of ePHI.
- **Person-or-entity authentication**.
- **Transmission security** — integrity controls and encryption during transmission.

**"Required" vs. "addressable" implementation specifications.** The implementation specifications under each standard are classified as either required or addressable. A required specification must be implemented as stated. An addressable specification is not optional in the colloquial sense — the Business Associate must assess whether the specification is a reasonable and appropriate safeguard in its environment, and either (i) implement it, (ii) implement an equivalent alternative measure if the stated specification is not reasonable and appropriate, or (iii) document why neither is reasonable and appropriate and that the standard is otherwise met. "We chose not to do this" is not a valid position; "we implemented an equivalent alternative and documented the rationale" is. Encryption of ePHI at rest is addressable, but the practitioner default is to implement it — the HITECH breach-notification safe-harbour for encrypted PHI (discussed below) makes implementation close to mandatory in effect.

Security-engineering depth — how to actually implement MFA, how to configure the cloud-provider HIPAA-eligible services, how to run the audit-log pipeline, how to architect encryption-at-rest with HSM-backed keys — belongs to `security-learning` and `ai-infra-security-solutions`. This chapter is the regulatory map; the engineering controls plug into it.

### The Privacy Rule under 45 C.F.R. Part 164 Subpart E

The Privacy Rule governs the **use and disclosure** of PHI, regardless of whether electronic or on paper. Business Associates are directly subject to a subset of the Privacy Rule (the sections HITECH extended to Business Associates, including the BAA content list and the uses-and-disclosures limits that flow through the BAA); Covered Entities are subject to the full rule.

**Minimum-necessary standard at § 164.502(b).** When using or disclosing PHI or when requesting PHI from another Covered Entity, the Business Associate must make reasonable efforts to limit PHI to the *minimum necessary* to accomplish the intended purpose of the use, disclosure, or request. The practical implication for product engineering is that query interfaces, dashboards, and data exports should default to the minimum fields required — not "SELECT *" over the PHI store — and role-based access should be enforced so a billing user does not see clinical notes. The minimum-necessary standard has an important **TPO exception**: it does not apply to disclosures to or requests by a healthcare provider for *treatment*, to the individual, pursuant to an individual's authorisation, required by law, or as otherwise exempted under § 164.502(b)(2). The exception recognises that treatment decisions require the clinician to see the clinical picture.

**Permitted uses and disclosures without authorisation.** Under § 164.502(a) and the operational sections that follow, PHI may be used or disclosed without an individual authorisation for a defined set of purposes:

- **Treatment, Payment, and Healthcare Operations (TPO)** — the core clinical and administrative use case.
- The individual's own request.
- **Public-health activities** under § 164.512(b) — disease reporting, vaccine registries, FDA adverse-event reporting.
- As **required by law** under § 164.512(a).
- A defined set of other purposes — law enforcement, judicial proceedings, workers' compensation, research subject to IRB waiver.

Anything outside these categories requires an individual **HIPAA authorisation** under § 164.508 — which has specific required content and is much narrower than a generic consent.

**Notice of Privacy Practices at § 164.520.** Covered Entities (not Business Associates) must provide a Notice of Privacy Practices describing the uses and disclosures they may make of PHI, the individual's rights, and the Covered Entity's obligations. The Notice must be provided at first service delivery, posted prominently, and updated for material changes.

**Patient-access rights under § 164.524.** Individuals have a right of access to their PHI in a designated record set. The Covered Entity must respond within **30 days** of the request, with a one-time **30-day extension** on written notice of the reason. The fee is limited to a reasonable, cost-based fee for labour, supplies, and postage. The Business Associate's role here is to make the PHI available to the Covered Entity so the Covered Entity can meet the § 164.524 clock — the BAA requires this under § 164.504(e)(2).

**Amendment rights under § 164.526.** Individuals can request amendment of PHI they believe is inaccurate or incomplete; the Covered Entity must respond within 60 days (with a 30-day extension). The Business Associate assists under the BAA.

**Accounting of disclosures under § 164.528.** Individuals have a right to an accounting of disclosures made in the six years preceding the request, with exceptions (TPO disclosures, disclosures to the individual, disclosures incident to an otherwise permitted use). The Business Associate must track disclosures that would be in scope and surface them to the Covered Entity.

### HITECH Breach Notification Rule under 45 C.F.R. §§ 164.400–414

HITECH (2009) added a federal breach-notification obligation for unsecured PHI, codified at 45 C.F.R. §§ 164.400–414. The rule has four moving parts: the definition of a breach, the four-factor risk-of-compromise assessment, the notification obligations with their calendars, and the Business Associate's own notice to the Covered Entity.

**Definition of breach at § 164.402.** A breach is the acquisition, access, use, or disclosure of PHI in a manner not permitted by the Privacy Rule which compromises the security or privacy of the PHI. **Unsecured PHI** is PHI not rendered unusable, unreadable, or indecipherable through a technology or methodology specified by HHS — in practice, PHI that is not encrypted to the HHS-specified NIST standards or destroyed per the HHS guidance.

Encrypted-at-rest PHI that is lost with the encryption keys secured is generally **not** a reportable breach under HITECH, which is why the Security Rule's "addressable" encryption specification is treated as effectively mandatory by the practitioner community.

**The four-factor risk-of-compromise assessment at § 164.402(2).** An impermissible use or disclosure of PHI is presumed to be a breach unless the Business Associate (or Covered Entity) demonstrates there is a low probability that the PHI has been compromised. The four factors:

1. The nature and extent of the PHI involved, including types of identifiers and the likelihood of re-identification.
2. The unauthorised person who used the PHI or to whom the disclosure was made.
3. Whether the PHI was actually acquired or viewed.
4. The extent to which the risk to the PHI has been mitigated.

The analysis is documented contemporaneously; "we decided this was not a breach" without the four-factor memo is not a defensible posture on audit.

**Individual notice under § 164.404.** Covered Entities must notify each affected individual without unreasonable delay and **in no case later than 60 calendar days** after discovery of the breach. Discovery occurs on the first day the breach is known to the Covered Entity (or a workforce member other than the person who committed the breach) or, by exercising reasonable diligence, would have been known. Notice must be in plain language, delivered by first-class mail (or email if the individual has agreed), and must include the elements enumerated at § 164.404(c) — what happened, types of information involved, steps the individual should take, what the Covered Entity is doing, contact information.

**HHS notice under § 164.408.** For breaches affecting **500 or more** individuals, notice to HHS OCR is **concurrent** with individual notice (and in no case later than 60 days after discovery), submitted through the HHS breach-reporting portal. For breaches affecting **fewer than 500** individuals, notice to HHS is an **annual rollup**, submitted no later than 60 days after the end of the calendar year in which the breaches were discovered.

**Media notice under § 164.406.** For breaches affecting **more than 500 residents of a State or jurisdiction**, the Covered Entity must notify prominent media outlets serving the State or jurisdiction without unreasonable delay and in no case later than 60 days after discovery. This is a *per-jurisdiction* threshold — a breach affecting 2,000 individuals spread across 50 states does not necessarily trigger media notice in any one state, while a breach affecting 501 individuals all in California does.

**Business Associate's own notice to the Covered Entity under § 164.410.** A Business Associate that discovers a breach of unsecured PHI must notify the Covered Entity without unreasonable delay and in no case later than **60 calendar days** after discovery, with the information the Covered Entity needs to meet its § 164.404 obligations (identification of each individual whose PHI was involved, and any other available information the Covered Entity is required to include).

The BAA typically shortens the 60-day outer bound to a much faster commitment — five to fifteen days is common — so the Covered Entity has runway to complete its own analysis and send individual notice inside the Covered Entity's 60-day clock measured from the Covered Entity's own discovery.

### Audit-trail and PHI-access-logging under § 164.312(b)

The Security Rule's **audit controls standard** at § 164.312(b) requires hardware, software, and / or procedural mechanisms that record and examine activity in information systems that contain or use ePHI. The companion administrative safeguard — the **information-system-activity review** at § 164.308(a)(1)(ii)(D) — requires regular review of records of information-system activity (audit logs, access reports, security-incident tracking reports). Together, these are the HIPAA foundation for the PHI-access-logging discipline.

The practitioner implementation is a tamper-resistant audit log that records (at minimum) the user identity, the PHI record accessed, the action taken, and the timestamp, with a documented review cadence, alerting on anomalous patterns, and retention aligned to the § 164.316(b)(2) requirement.

**Retention under § 164.316(b)(2).** Documentation required by the Security Rule — policies and procedures, risk analyses, risk management plans, audit-control records, workforce-security records, and the like — must be retained for **six years from the date of its creation or the date when it last was in effect, whichever is later**. The six-year retention is the HIPAA default and overrides shorter operational retention policies for records in scope.

Audit-log engineering — how to build an append-only log pipeline, how to correlate application-layer access with database-layer queries, how to alert on exfiltration patterns — defers to `security-learning` and the SOC 2 Common Criteria mapping authored in [chapter 07](./07-soc2-iso27001-audit-readiness-and-compliance-automation-stack.md). The audit-trail controls that satisfy HIPAA § 164.312(b) overlap substantially with the SOC 2 Common Criteria for logging and monitoring (CC7.2, CC7.3), and a well-run programme runs one control that satisfies both frameworks rather than two parallel controls.

### Layered analysis — HIPAA on top of GDPR / CCPA / the mod-109 DPA

HIPAA does not displace GDPR or the US state-privacy regime. The layered posture is a three-way overlay.

**HIPAA + GDPR.** Where the Covered Entity or Business Associate processes personal data of EEA data subjects (an EU-based hospital system customer, an EU resident receiving telehealth from a US-based provider), the processing is simultaneously subject to HIPAA (because it is PHI processed on behalf of a Covered Entity) and GDPR (because it is EEA personal data).

PHI is almost always **Article 9 special-category data** (health data) under GDPR, which requires an Article 9(2) condition on top of the Article 6 lawful basis — typically Art. 9(2)(h) (provision of healthcare) in the clinical context, with the member-state-law overlay. The GDPR Article 32 TOMs, Article 33 breach notification (72 hours to the supervisory authority, not 60 days to individuals), and Chapter V transfer mechanisms all run alongside HIPAA. The breach-response playbook ([chapter 05](./05-breach-response-playbook.md)) runs both clocks in parallel from a single incident, because the 72-hour GDPR supervisory-authority notice fires long before the HIPAA 60-day individual-notice clock and the two trigger different deliverables.

**HIPAA + CCPA / CPRA.** California Civil Code § 1798.145(c) contains a **HIPAA carve-out**: PHI collected by a Covered Entity or Business Associate governed by HIPAA is not subject to the CCPA to the extent it is treated as PHI under HIPAA.

The carve-out has interpretive edges. A single corporation that operates both a Covered Entity / Business Associate function (processing PHI under a BAA) *and* a non-covered consumer function (processing non-PHI consumer data on a direct-to-consumer product line) must segregate the two streams; the CCPA still applies to the non-PHI consumer stream. The practitioner posture is a data-flow map that labels each category as PHI (HIPAA-governed) or non-PHI (CCPA-governed) with explicit audit of edge cases — de-identified data derived from PHI, PHI shared with the individual's authorisation into a non-covered context, employer-sponsored wellness data that may or may not be HIPAA PHI depending on the plan structure.

**HIPAA + the mod-109 DPA structure.** The DPA ([mod-109 chapter 01](../mod-109-commercial-contracts-ip-and-legal-ops/01-customer-contract-suite-msa-sla-dpa-security-aup.md)) is the vehicle that carries Article 28 processor obligations, CCPA service-provider language, international-transfer mechanisms, and the TOMs schedule. The BAA is executed as a schedule to the DPA (or as a separate exhibit incorporated by reference in the MSA); the order-of-precedence clause in the MSA lists the BAA above the DPA on the PHI subset. The DPA's TOM schedule is cross-referenced from the Security Addendum and must be consistent with the Security Rule administrative / physical / technical safeguards — the two documents name the same controls under different headings, and they cannot contradict.

**The dual-scope posture.** A SaaS that serves EEA hospital systems, California consumer customers, and US hospital systems simultaneously runs all three regimes off one privacy programme, one data-flow inventory, one TOMs schedule, and one incident-response playbook, with the regulatory-specific obligations pulled out into regime-specific runbooks — not three parallel programmes.

## Concrete example: Northgate Health, a Series-B telehealth-infrastructure SaaS

Northgate Health is a Series-B SaaS corporation that sells a telehealth-scheduling and clinical-messaging platform to US hospital systems. Mercy Valley Health, a 400-bed hospital system in Ohio, is Northgate's first signed enterprise customer. Mercy Valley is clearly a Covered Entity (healthcare provider transmitting health information electronically in connection with HIPAA Transactions). Northgate processes PHI (patient names, appointment times, chief complaints, clinical-messaging content) inside its platform on Mercy Valley's behalf — Northgate is unambiguously a Business Associate under § 160.103. The AWS region Northgate runs on is HIPAA-eligible and AWS has a BAA with Northgate; AWS is Northgate's sub-Business Associate. The transactional-email provider Northgate uses for appointment reminders receives PHI and is also a sub-Business Associate; the observability vendor Northgate sends application logs to has been configured to redact PHI from log payloads and operates under the conduit interpretation for the redacted log stream — but counsel has flagged this as a thin position and the posture is being re-evaluated.

**The BAA execution.** Mercy Valley's procurement team sends Northgate the hospital system's own BAA template, which runs to 14 pages and incorporates all of § 164.504(e)(2) plus several hospital-specific hardening clauses (a five-day breach-notification clock to Mercy Valley rather than the regulatory outer bound of 60 days, a prohibition on using PHI for product-improvement or model-training purposes even in de-identified form, a carve-out of the DPA's audit-rights compromise so Mercy Valley reserves an on-site audit right notwithstanding Northgate's SOC 2 Type II). Northgate's legal team negotiates the five-day clock to ten days (counsel's playbook position; aligned to internal incident-response capability), concedes the no-training clause on PHI (Northgate has no model-training programme on customer PHI anyway), and accepts the on-site audit right conditioned on Mercy Valley giving 30 days' notice and limiting the audit to the systems that handle Mercy Valley's PHI. The BAA is signed as Exhibit C to the MSA, with the DPA as Exhibit B; the MSA's order-of-precedence clause is updated to read "Order Form > BAA (on PHI) > DPA (on non-PHI personal data) > MSA > SLA > Security Addendum > AUP."

**The Security Rule implementation.** Northgate's security programme, run by a named Security Officer (the VP of Engineering plus a security lead reporting to her), implements the § 164.308 administrative safeguards: a documented risk analysis updated annually, a risk-management plan with named control owners, workforce-security onboarding / offboarding tied to the HRIS, security-awareness training at hire and annually (including a PHI-specific module), a contingency plan with quarterly disaster-recovery drills, and a quarterly security-posture review feeding into the board-reporting cadence set up in [mod-111](../mod-111-corporate-governance-board-operations-and-officer-duties/). The § 164.310 physical safeguards inherit from AWS for the production environment, with Northgate's office and endpoint fleet covered by an MDM-managed laptop programme and a clean-desk policy. The § 164.312 technical safeguards run on unique per-user SSO identities (no shared accounts), 15-minute automatic session timeout, AES-256 encryption at rest with AWS KMS-managed keys, TLS 1.3 in transit, an append-only audit log streaming from every PHI-touching service to a centralised log store with 7-year retention (one year of hot storage, six years of cold archive), and a quarterly information-system-activity review that samples audit-log records against user access-authorisation records. Encryption of PHI at rest is nominally addressable; Northgate implements it as effectively required because the HITECH breach-notification safe harbour for encrypted PHI is close to a business-continuity requirement for a Series-B.

**The Breach Notification Rule runbook.** Northgate's incident-response playbook includes a dedicated HIPAA path. On detection of a potential impermissible use or disclosure of PHI, the Security Officer opens a HIPAA incident ticket and convenes the incident-response team (Security Officer, GC or outside privacy counsel, VP Engineering, Customer Success lead for the affected customer). The team runs the four-factor § 164.402(2) risk-of-compromise analysis within 48 hours and documents the analysis in a privileged memo. If the four-factor analysis concludes the incident is a breach, the clock starts: Northgate must notify Mercy Valley under the BAA within ten days (the negotiated clock), and Mercy Valley's own 60-day individual-notice clock starts on Mercy Valley's discovery (which will typically track Northgate's notice). The runbook separately tracks the GDPR Article 33 72-hour clock in case any affected data subject is EEA-based (Mercy Valley has a small set of EEA-visiting-patient records), the state breach-notification clocks for Ohio and any other state whose residents are affected, the SEC Item 1.05 four-business-day clock (deferred — Northgate is not yet public, but the runbook is pre-built against IPO timing), and the cyber-insurance carrier-notification clock under the policy stood up in [mod-112 chapter 03](../mod-112-enterprise-risk-insurance-and-compliance/03-cyber-insurance-and-incident-response-coordination.md). If the breach affects 500 or more individuals in a single state, the runbook triggers HHS OCR concurrent notice and prominent-media notice in that state; if under 500, HHS notice goes into the annual rollup. The runbook is rehearsed semi-annually in a tabletop exercise with outside privacy counsel participating, and the exercise outputs are reported to the audit / AI-risk sub-committee at the quarterly board cycle.

The combined posture — a signed BAA with every Covered Entity customer, a Security Rule implementation that produces audit-trail artefacts on request, a HITECH breach-notification runbook with a 48-hour four-factor memo and parallel GDPR / state / SEC / cyber-insurance clocks — is what a hospital-system procurement team, an HHS OCR investigator, and a Series-C technical-diligence reviewer all expect to see on first ask.

## Summary

- HIPAA attaches to two populations under 45 C.F.R. § 160.103 — Covered Entities (health plans, healthcare clearinghouses, healthcare providers transmitting electronically in connection with a HIPAA Transaction) and Business Associates (anyone who creates / receives / maintains / transmits PHI on their behalf). A SaaS that processes PHI for a Covered-Entity customer is a Business Associate by definition; sub-Business Associate flow-down runs to every downstream processor. The conduit exception is narrow and does not cover cloud-storage or SaaS providers that persist PHI.
- The BAA under 45 C.F.R. § 164.504(e)(2) is the required instrument: permitted uses and disclosures, prohibition on unlawful use, Security Rule safeguards, breach reporting to the Covered Entity, subcontractor flow-down, individual-access / amendment / accounting assistance, HHS audit access, and return / destroy on termination. The BAA is a schedule to the DPA, not a substitute; the MSA order-of-precedence clause names the BAA explicitly.
- The Security Rule at 45 C.F.R. Part 164 Subpart C runs across administrative safeguards (§ 164.308 — risk analysis, workforce security, training, contingency planning), physical safeguards (§ 164.310 — facility, workstation, device / media controls), and technical safeguards (§ 164.312 — access control, audit controls, integrity, authentication, transmission security). "Required" specifications must be implemented; "addressable" specifications must be implemented or an equivalent documented, not skipped.
- The Privacy Rule at 45 C.F.R. Part 164 Subpart E imposes the minimum-necessary standard at § 164.502(b) (with the TPO exception), permits uses and disclosures for Treatment / Payment / Healthcare Operations and the enumerated public-interest categories, requires the Notice of Privacy Practices under § 164.520, and gives individuals access (§ 164.524, 30-day clock extendable by 30), amendment (§ 164.526), and accounting-of-disclosures (§ 164.528) rights that the Business Associate supports through the BAA.
- The HITECH Breach Notification Rule at 45 C.F.R. §§ 164.400–414 runs a 60-day individual-notice clock from discovery of a breach of unsecured PHI, with HHS OCR notice concurrent for 500+ affected residents of a state or jurisdiction and annual rollup for breaches under 500, prominent media notice for 500+ in a state, and the four-factor § 164.402(2) risk-of-compromise analysis that must be documented contemporaneously. A Business Associate owes its own § 164.410 60-day notice to the Covered Entity; BAAs typically shorten this to five to fifteen days.
- Encrypted PHI (to HHS-specified NIST standards, with keys secured) is generally outside the Breach Notification Rule's "unsecured PHI" definition — the safe harbour that makes the Security Rule's addressable encryption specification effectively mandatory in practice.
- The audit-controls standard at § 164.312(b) plus the information-system-activity-review requirement at § 164.308(a)(1)(ii)(D) are the PHI-access-logging foundation; HIPAA documentation retention under § 164.316(b)(2) runs six years from creation or last-effective date. Audit-trail controls overlap substantially with SOC 2 Common Criteria (CC7.2, CC7.3); run one control programme, not two.
- HIPAA layers on top of GDPR (PHI is almost always Article 9 special-category data; Art. 9(2)(h) healthcare condition typically applies; Article 32 TOMs and Article 33 72-hour notice run alongside HIPAA), on top of CCPA / CPRA (the § 1798.145(c) HIPAA carve-out protects PHI handled as a Covered Entity or BA but not the non-PHI consumer surface of the same entity), and on top of the mod-109 DPA structure (BAA as a schedule, with the DPA carrying non-PHI processor obligations and the DPA's TOMs schedule consistent with the Security Rule safeguards).
- A healthtech corporation that serves hospital systems, EEA patients, and California consumers simultaneously runs one privacy programme with one data-flow inventory and one TOMs schedule, with regime-specific runbooks pulled out — not three parallel compliance programmes.
- Security-engineering implementation of the Security Rule defers to `security-learning` and `ai-infra-security-solutions`; the cyber-insurance tower that pays for a PHI-breach response defers to [mod-112 chapter 03](../mod-112-enterprise-risk-insurance-and-compliance/03-cyber-insurance-and-incident-response-coordination.md); the internal AI acceptable-use policy for employees handling PHI defers to [mod-108](../mod-108-culture-employee-experience-and-dei/); the board-level reporting of the HIPAA compliance programme defers to [mod-111](../mod-111-corporate-governance-board-operations-and-officer-duties/).
