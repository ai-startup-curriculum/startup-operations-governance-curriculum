# Exercise 05 — Breach-response playbook authoring

> Estimated time: **~12 hours** · Related chapter: [07 — The incident-response and breach-notification playbook](../07-incident-response-and-breach-notification-playbook.md) (with HIPAA Breach Notification Rule substance from [chapter 04](../04-hipaa-compliance-for-healthtech.md) and GDPR Article 33 / 34 substance from [chapter 01](../01-gdpr-compliance-programme.md))

## Problem statement

Northrun Platforms Inc. is a Series-C Delaware C-corp selling a workforce-identity-and-access management product into mid-market and enterprise. The product manages customer-organisation employee identities, authentication artefacts (SAML / OIDC tokens, session identifiers), and the audit log of every authentication and authorisation event across the customer's downstream SaaS and on-prem application portfolio. Northrun is 440 FTE, closed a $140M Series C eleven months ago, books ARR of approximately $84M, runs ~1,900 customer tenants across the US, EU, UK, Canada, and Australia (with ~180 EU-resident tenants including seven German Bundesanstalt-regulated financial institutions and two French CNIL-notification-threshold customers), serves three New York Department of Financial Services Covered Entity customers under 23 N.Y.C.R.R. Part 500, serves eleven HIPAA Covered Entity customers under a signed BAA, and is listed under neither Form S-1 nor Form 10 — Northrun is a private company and is not subject to SEC Item 1.05 of Form 8-K on its own cybersecurity-incident disclosure. Approximately 23 of Northrun's customers are public reporting companies subject to Item 1.05 and will depend on Northrun's breach-notification timing to meet their own four-business-day disclosure clock.

Northrun's current breach-response posture is immature given the stage. The incident-response team runs out of the security-engineering organisation; the privacy-office interface is ad-hoc (the general counsel + one in-house privacy counsel split responsibility with no defined breach-response role); the forensic-investigation protocol is the IR team's standing runbook and has not been reviewed by outside counsel for privilege posture; the cyber-insurance tower (primary + two excess layers, aggregating to a limit the CFO will confirm) <!-- needs-research: confirm Northrun's current tower limit, retention amount, and panel-counsel list; chapter 07 names the tower as the forcing function on the IR-counsel-of-record and the forensic-vendor selection. --> has panel-counsel and panel-forensic-vendor requirements the IR team has not practised against; the HIPAA Breach Notification Rule 60-day clock has been presumed but never time-tested; the GDPR Article 33 72-hour clock has never been tested at all; the 50-state breach-notification matrix has never been assembled. The CISO has flagged the breach-playbook gap on the risk register for three quarters running and the Series-D diligence team has asked, in a preliminary data-request, for "a written breach-response playbook with the regulatory-notification matrix, a sample scenario walk-through, and the forensic-vendor-and-counsel-of-record engagement letters."

Three scenarios are the test cases the playbook must handle. **Scenario X** — a targeted phishing compromise of a Northrun on-call engineer with production access; the compromised credential was used to query the customer-audit-log table for approximately 46 hours before the SOC detected the anomalous access; the exfiltration volume (if any) is uncertain; the customer-set exposed includes four of the German financial-institution customers and one HIPAA Covered Entity. **Scenario Y** — a misconfiguration in the customer-onboarding API that exposed 11,000 US-customer administrator names, email addresses, and encrypted-at-rest password hashes (bcrypt, salt per record) to the public internet via an unauthenticated endpoint for approximately 3 hours before discovery; no evidence of enumeration; the customer set is cross-state and includes California, Illinois, Massachusetts, and New York residents. **Scenario Z** — a sub-processor (the primary email-delivery vendor) suffered its own breach announced to Northrun 11 days after the sub-processor's discovery; the breach exposed Northrun customer-administrator names and email addresses passed through the sub-processor for transactional emails; approximately 320,000 records in scope spanning the US, EU, UK, and Australia customer base.

The general counsel has ten weeks to walk the board governance subcommittee through the playbook, the three scenario walk-throughs, and the panel-counsel / panel-forensic-vendor engagement artefacts. She owns the end-to-end package; the CISO owns the IR-team-side runbook and the SOC detection-and-containment posture; the chief privacy officer (one headcount, reports to the general counsel) owns the regulator-notification matrix; the CFO owns the cyber-insurance-tower-coordination mechanics; outside IR counsel is being selected from the cyber-insurance panel. Author the full package.

## Requirements

### Part A — Incident-classification ladder and severity taxonomy

Author `incident-classification-ladder.md`. The chapter 07 framing — event / incident / breach — is the operational decomposition every downstream clock reads from. Cover:

1. **The three-tier ladder.** Define "event" (a signal of potential concern, not yet confirmed to be an incident; low-and-medium alerts, failed login anomalies, log-integrity warnings), "incident" (a confirmed unauthorised event affecting the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of Northrun systems or data; chapter 07's operational definition), and "breach" (an incident meeting the regulatory definition of personal-data breach under GDPR Art. 4(12), of PHI breach under 45 C.F.R. § 164.402, of a covered security event under NYDFS Part 500, of a notifiable event under a named state breach-notification statute, or of a material cybersecurity incident under SEC Item 1.05 for customers who are reporting companies). Name the escalation triggers from event → incident → breach and the owners at each tier.
2. **The severity taxonomy.** The chapter 07 severity levels (Sev-0 / Sev-1 / Sev-2 / Sev-3 / Sev-4 or an equivalent named hierarchy) with the specific operational treatment per level — the paging discipline, the exec-notification discipline, the customer-notification posture, the trading-window and insider-information posture if applicable (Northrun is private so Section 10b-5 is not directly relevant but the posture discipline applies to the Series-D fundraise timing).
3. **The initial-assessment decision-tree.** The decision-tree the IR commander runs at the moment of escalation from event → incident — the first-30-minute questions (what data is in scope, what systems are affected, what customers are affected, is personal data potentially implicated, is PHI potentially implicated, is NPI potentially implicated, is the sub-processor chain in scope). The decision-tree is engineered so the privacy-office interface activates at the earliest moment personal data is implicated, not at the moment the SOC declares breach.
4. **The "clock-start" convention per regime.** The specific moment at which each regulatory clock starts: GDPR Art. 33 "awareness" (the moment the controller has a reasonable degree of certainty that a security incident resulting in the exposure of personal data has occurred — EDPB Guidelines 9/2022 on personal data breach notification, superseding WP250 rev.01); HIPAA Breach Notification Rule "discovery" at 45 C.F.R. § 164.404(a)(2) (the first day the breach is known, or by exercising reasonable diligence would have been known); NYDFS Part 500.17(a) "determined" (the moment the Covered Entity determines that a cybersecurity event has occurred); SEC Item 1.05 "material" (the moment the reporting company determines materiality, four business days from that determination); state breach-notification clocks (the authoritative-source step is the 50-state matrix in Part B). <!-- needs-research: confirm the current EDPB Guidelines 9/2022 endorsement status and any 2025–2026 supervisory-authority supplementary guidance on "awareness." -->

1. **The GDPR Article 33 / 34 row.** The 72-hour clock to the supervisory authority (Art. 33(1)) and the "without undue delay" clock to data subjects where the breach is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons (Art. 34). Name the lead supervisory authority determination (the main establishment under Art. 56 — the Dublin establishment that Northrun's EU operations run through, if established, makes the Irish DPC the lead authority; otherwise the authorities of each affected member state are directly competent). The Art. 33(1) content obligations and the Art. 33(4) phased-notification mechanic where the information is not available within 72 hours. <!-- needs-research: confirm Northrun's current main-establishment status under Article 56 and the current Irish DPC vs. multi-authority posture before relying on the Irish DPC as lead supervisory authority. -->
2. **The HIPAA Breach Notification Rule row.** The 60-day clock to affected individuals from discovery under 45 C.F.R. § 164.404(b), the 60-day clock to HHS for breaches affecting ≥ 500 individuals under § 164.408(b), the annual-rollup to HHS for breaches affecting < 500 individuals under § 164.408(c), the media-notification obligation for breaches affecting > 500 individuals in a single state or jurisdiction under § 164.406. The Business-Associate 60-day clock to the Covered Entity under § 164.410(b) (with Northrun's chapter-04 commitment to a faster 24–48-hour clock from Exercise 04 Part B).
3. **The 50-state breach-notification matrix.** A table with one row per US state and territory, with columns for (statute citation, applicability threshold, consumer-notification clock, regulator-notification-threshold, regulator-notification-clock, attorney-general or other regulator identified, content-of-notice requirements, substitute-notice threshold, encryption safe-harbour applicability, private-right-of-action). <!-- needs-research: confirm the current list of state breach-notification statutes and effective dates; several states have amended or enacted statutes in 2024–2026 with varying notification clocks, regulator-notification thresholds, and content requirements. --> Flag the states with regulator-notification clocks shorter than the general consumer-notification clock (including, at time of writing, Florida's FIPA notification to the Department of Legal Affairs, California's notification to the AG for breaches affecting > 500 residents, and New York's notification to the AG, Department of State, and Division of State Police).
4. **The NYDFS Part 500.17(a) row.** The 72-hour clock to the Department from the determination that a cybersecurity event has occurred, applicable where Northrun is itself a Covered Entity (Northrun is not directly covered; its three NYDFS-Covered-Entity customers are) and where Northrun's Third-Party Service Provider Security Policy flow-down obligations under § 500.11 require Northrun to notify the Covered-Entity customers on a faster-than-statutory clock so those customers can meet their own 72-hour clock to the Department. Name the specific contractual commitment Northrun will make on the Third-Party Service Provider side.
5. **The SEC Item 1.05 of Form 8-K row.** The four-business-day disclosure clock for a "material cybersecurity incident" from the registrant's determination of materiality for SEC-reporting-company customers. Northrun is not a registrant; the 23 reporting-company customers depend on Northrun's breach-notification posture to make their own materiality determination. Name the contractual commitment Northrun will make — the breach-notification clock Northrun offers each reporting-company customer so that customer can meet the four-business-day clock. Reference the SEC's July 2023 Cybersecurity Disclosure Final Rule and the Division of Corporation Finance interpretive guidance. <!-- needs-research: confirm the current SEC Division of Corporation Finance interpretive guidance on Item 1.05 materiality determination and any 2024–2026 updates. -->
6. **The sector-specific overlays row.** The GLBA § 314.5 FTC-notification 30-day clock for the Kestrel-Capital-like bank-partner-service-provider scenario (not directly applicable to Northrun but reference the pattern from Exercise 04 Part C). The specific-state regulator-notification clocks named in the state row where the statute is not a general breach-notification statute but a sector overlay.
7. **The international-customer overlay.** The UK DPA 2018 / UK GDPR Article 33 analog; the Canadian PIPEDA Breach of Security Safeguards reporting; the Australian Privacy Act 1988 (Cth) Notifiable Data Breaches scheme; the Brazilian LGPD Article 48 notification. Defer the country-specific depth to [mod-113 — International Expansion & Global Workforce](../../mod-113-international-expansion-and-global-workforce/); the matrix names the rows and the clock by jurisdiction.

### Part C — Forensic-investigation protocol and privilege posture

Author `forensic-investigation-and-privilege-posture.md`. The forensic investigation produces the report that, if discoverable, becomes plaintiffs' primary evidence in the private-right-of-action and the AG-enforcement proceedings that follow. Cover:

1. **The IR-commander-and-counsel-of-record structure.** The outside IR counsel engaged under an engagement letter that names the investigation as attorney-directed and attorney-privileged, with the forensic vendor engaged by outside counsel (not by Northrun directly) under a separate engagement letter that names the vendor as an agent of counsel assisting counsel in the provision of legal advice. The chapter 07 privilege-posture pattern against the *In re Capital One Consumer Data Security Breach Litigation* (E.D. Va. 2020) discoverability precedent — the "but-for" / "ordinary course" / "primary-purpose" tests the court applied and the operative discipline the engagement structure must satisfy. <!-- needs-research: confirm the current state of the attorney-client-privilege and attorney-work-product precedents on forensic-investigation-report discoverability, including any 2024–2026 federal or state decisions refining the primary-purpose test. -->
2. **The engagement-letter templates.** The named-counsel engagement letter (what the general counsel signs with outside IR counsel at incident declaration), the forensic-vendor engagement letter (what outside IR counsel signs with the forensic vendor), and the notice-to-the-cyber-insurer coordination document that the chapter-07 panel-counsel discipline requires. The specific clauses — purpose statement (attorney-directed investigation in connection with provision of legal advice, not an ordinary-course incident response), work-product-ownership and control, deliverable destination (outside counsel, not Northrun directly), communication posture (all substantive communication through outside counsel), retention and destruction posture at engagement termination. Author the operative clause text.
3. **The IR-team-and-counsel interface.** The operational discipline the IR team runs — the SOC continues to operate in its ordinary-course capacity (containment, eradication, recovery; chapter 07's SANS-style IR lifecycle) and does not produce the forensic-investigation report; the forensic vendor runs a separate investigation under counsel's direction; the SOC-side and vendor-side artefacts are kept distinct in the document-management posture. The named discipline: SOC Slack channels carry ordinary-course IR traffic (potentially discoverable); privileged channels (named separately, access controlled) carry counsel-directed investigation traffic.
4. **The dual-track-report pattern.** The common pattern at mature IR-counsel-of-record engagements — the forensic vendor produces two deliverables: a short "technical IR summary" not covered by privilege, delivered to Northrun directly, consumed by the IR team for containment and remediation; and a longer "privileged forensic report" delivered to outside counsel, consumed by counsel for legal advice, and not shared with Northrun directly. Name the pattern Northrun will adopt and the specific discipline at communication boundaries.
5. **The cyber-insurer coordination.** The policy's notice-of-incident obligations (typically immediate notice from incident declaration), the panel-counsel / panel-forensic-vendor selection discipline (the primary carrier will insist on panel counsel at the engagement moment; chapter 07 names the "pre-select from panel" discipline that avoids the engagement-delay failure mode at the clock-start moment), and the coordination with the chapter-07 broker-on-speed-dial posture. Reference [mod-112 chapter 03](../../mod-112-enterprise-risk-insurance-and-compliance/03-cyber-insurance-and-incident-response-coordination.md) for the cyber-insurance-tower-coordination framework and the broker-on-retainer pattern; do not re-author.

### Part D — Scenario X walk-through (phishing compromise of privileged credential)

Author `scenario-x-walkthrough.md`. Walk Scenario X through the end-to-end playbook from detection to post-incident review. Cover:

If the determination is that the breach is not likely to result in a risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons (Art. 33(1) carve-out), the documented Art. 33 non-notification analysis the DPO signs. If the determination is still-uncertain, the Art. 33(4) phased-notification mechanic. Name the specific decision Northrun makes and the governance cadence (who signs the notification; who reviews; who authorises).
4. **The customer-notification posture.** The Third-Party Service Provider Security Policy flow-down notification to the three NYDFS Covered Entity customers (72-hour clock from Northrun's determination), the BAA-flow-down notification to the HIPAA Covered Entity customer (24–48 hours from Northrun's discovery per Exercise 04 Part B), the DPA-flow-down notification to the EU customers (per the processor's assist-the-controller obligation under Article 28(3)(f) and Article 33(2)). Author the customer-notification template Northrun will use, with the specific content a Covered Entity needs to meet its own clock.
5. **The post-incident review.** The chapter-07 post-incident review discipline — root-cause analysis, controls-change identification (what control was missing, what control failed, what control should be added), governance-cadence review (did the playbook work; what is the gap; what is the amendment), lessons-learned-publication (what Northrun will publish to its customers and what it will keep internal), the cyber-insurance-claim-coordination post-incident.

### Part E — Scenario Y walk-through (misconfiguration exposing hashed credentials)

Author `scenario-y-walkthrough.md`. Walk Scenario Y through the same playbook. Cover:

1. **The characterisation question.** Scenario Y is the hard characterisation case — 11,000 customer-administrator names, email addresses, and bcrypt-salt-per-record password hashes were exposed via an unauthenticated public endpoint for three hours. Is this a "breach" under the state statutes' typical definition of "unauthorised acquisition of personal information"? The encryption safe-harbour under most state breach-notification statutes applies where the personal information was encrypted and the encryption key is not reasonably believed to have been accessed — bcrypt-hashed-with-salt may or may not qualify as "encrypted" under a given state's definition. Analyse the question against the specific state-statute definitions.
2. **The 50-state analysis.** Run the applicability analysis across California (Cal. Civ. Code § 1798.82, which defines "encrypted" and historically has treated a one-way hash as not meeting the encryption safe-harbour for the purpose of the acquisition test — the general counsel's conservative posture is to notify), Illinois (815 ILCS 530 and the Biometric Information Privacy Act does not apply here), Massachusetts (M.G.L. c. 93H), and New York (N.Y. Gen. Bus. Law § 899-aa and the SHIELD Act's definition of "private information"). <!-- needs-research: confirm the current statutory definitions of "encrypted" and "personal information" in each of the four states and the current AG enforcement posture on the one-way-hash-plus-salt question; the posture moves and is state-specific. -->
3. **The GDPR characterisation.** If any of the exposed records are EU-resident, the GDPR Art. 4(12) "personal data breach" analysis — a bcrypt-hashed-with-salt password is likely still personal data under Recital 26 (the hash is linkable to a data subject via the record's name and email), the exposure is a "breach of security leading to the accidental or unlawful ... disclosure of ... personal data." The Art. 33(1) "unlikely to result in a risk" analysis — a three-hour exposure with no evidence of enumeration, bcrypt hashing adding cost-of-attack, may or may not clear the "unlikely to result in a risk" threshold.
4. **The decision and the notification posture.** The specific decision Northrun makes — likely a conservative "notify under the state statutes where the general counsel cannot defend the encryption safe-harbour" and "notify under GDPR Art. 33 as a protective filing even where the DPO's Art. 33(1) analysis suggests non-notification is defensible." Author the state-side notification letter template, including the substitute-notice posture where the per-state notification volume approaches the substitute-notice threshold under the state's statute.
5. **The sub-processor-notification-propagation.** The downstream customer notification (Northrun's customers are controllers for their own administrator-identity data; Northrun is the processor); the Art. 33(2) processor-side obligation is to notify the controller "without undue delay" after becoming aware of the personal data breach; the operational timeline Northrun commits to (reference Exercise 01 Part D's sub-processor-change-notification mechanic as the standing-channel the breach notification flows through).

### Part F — Scenario Z walk-through (sub-processor breach with delayed notice)

Name the clock-position-per-regime at the moment of sub-processor notification.
2. **The sub-processor-contract review.** The chapter-07 and Exercise-01-Part-D discipline — the sub-processor is contractually obligated to notify Northrun without undue delay under Northrun's outbound DPA from [mod-109 chapter 01](../../mod-109-commercial-contracts-ip-and-legal-ops/01-customer-contract-suite-msa-sla-dpa-security-aup.md). 3. **The multi-jurisdiction notification.** 320,000 records across the US, EU, UK, and Australia. The matrix-reading from Part B — the EU notification posture (72-hour clock from Northrun's awareness; the sub-processor is a processor and Northrun is a processor on behalf of the customer-controllers, who will each run their own Art. 33 clock against their own supervisory authority); the UK notification posture (parallel UK GDPR Art. 33 with the ICO as the authority); the US state-by-state posture; the Australian Privacy Act Notifiable Data Breaches posture (30-day clock to the OAIC if the breach is likely to result in serious harm). <!-- needs-research: confirm the current OAIC Notifiable Data Breaches threshold and reporting mechanism after the 2024 Privacy Act amendments. -->
4. **The customer-notification coordination at scale.** The operational discipline for 320,000 records across ~1,900 customer tenants — the data-flow map from Exercise 01 Part D is the reference, the customer-notification template is the Exercise 01 Part F cadence surface, the batch-notification-vs-per-tenant-notification decision, the customer-support-team-mobilisation discipline, the trading-window-and-fundraise-timing posture (Series-D timing coordination).
5. **The sub-processor replacement decision.** The post-incident evaluation of the sub-processor — continue under a tightened SLA with specific breach-notification-clock contract terms and specific indemnification terms; replace with an alternative vendor; run parallel until replacement is complete. Reference the [chapter 08](../08-vendor-privacy-risk-assessment-programme.md) vendor-privacy-risk-assessment programme as the operating discipline the replacement decision reads from.
