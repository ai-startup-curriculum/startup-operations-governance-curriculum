# 2. The CCPA / CPRA compliance programme — thresholds, consumer rights, GPC, sensitive PI, and the service-provider flow-down

> CCPA compliance is not "we are not selling data." It is a threshold analysis done on three measurements, a consumer-rights workflow that closes on a 45-day clock, a Global Privacy Control signal honoured server-side on the first request, a sensitive-PI inventory that tracks the twelve named categories, and a service-provider addendum that flows the no-sale / no-share certification down every vendor contract.

## Motivation

A US-headquartered B2B SaaS corporation does not opt in to the California Consumer Privacy Act. The statute reaches it the moment one of three thresholds trips — $25M in annual revenue, personal information on 100,000 California consumers or households, or 50%+ of revenue from the sale or sharing of personal information. Most Series-B SaaS corporations trip the revenue threshold before they notice; many trip the 100,000-consumers threshold on their own marketing databases before counting customer end-users. The California Privacy Rights Act of 2020 then rewired the statute — adding the right to correct, the right to limit use and disclosure of sensitive personal information, "sharing" as a parallel to "selling," the Global Privacy Control universal-opt-out mechanism, and a new regulator (the California Privacy Protection Agency) with rulemaking authority on cybersecurity audits, risk assessments, and automated decision-making technology.

A credible CCPA programme has six running components: a current threshold-analysis memo that re-evaluates annually, a consumer-rights intake and fulfilment workflow that honours the 45-day clock, a GPC signal handler wired into the web platform, a sensitive-PI inventory with a "limit use" flow, a service-provider / contractor contract map with the mandatory § 1798.140(ag) / (ai) language flowed down, and a CPPA risk-assessment / cybersecurity-audit posture that tracks the Agency's evolving regulations. This chapter owns the substance. The structural anatomy of the DPA and the CCPA service-provider addendum as a schedule to the DPA is authored in [mod-109 chapter 01](../mod-109-commercial-contracts-ip-and-legal-ops/01-customer-contract-suite-msa-sla-dpa-security-aup.md); this chapter writes the clauses that schedule carries.

## Core concepts

### Threshold analysis under Cal. Civ. Code § 1798.140(d)

The CCPA applies to a "business" — defined in Cal. Civ. Code § 1798.140(d)(1) as a for-profit legal entity that does business in California, collects consumers' personal information (or has it collected on its behalf), determines the purposes and means of processing, *and* meets one of three thresholds:

- **Annual gross revenues in excess of $25 million** in the preceding calendar year (§ 1798.140(d)(1)(A)). The threshold is on gross revenue of the entire legal entity, not California-sourced revenue. A Series-B SaaS that crosses $25M ARR has tripped the revenue threshold even if only a single-digit percentage of revenue is from California customers.
- **Buys, sells, or shares personal information of 100,000 or more consumers or households** annually (§ 1798.140(d)(1)(B)). "Consumer" is defined at § 1798.140(i) as a California-resident natural person. The threshold counts consumers *or* households, and counts personal information collected from any source — marketing databases, free-tier signups, cookie-collected web visitors, trial accounts — not only paying customers.
- **Derives 50% or more of annual revenue from selling or sharing consumers' personal information** (§ 1798.140(d)(1)(C)). This is the ad-tech / data-broker threshold; a conventional B2B SaaS rarely trips it, but the analysis must be run rather than assumed.

Section 1798.140(d)(2)–(4) extends the definition to entities that control or are controlled by a "business" and share common branding (parent / subsidiary / affiliate), and to joint ventures or partnerships with a 40%-or-more interest held by a business — the corporate-group expansion that pulls affiliates into the regime.

**Minors' data under § 1798.120(c).** The sale or sharing of personal information of a consumer under 16 requires affirmative opt-in — from the consumer if 13–15, from a parent or guardian if under 13. A corporation with knowledge that it processes minors' data operates under the opt-in default regardless of the volume thresholds; the "actual knowledge" standard under § 1798.120(c) is interpreted expansively.

Operationally, the threshold analysis is a short memo refreshed annually by privacy counsel, with the revenue figure pulled from audited financials, the consumer-count figure pulled from the data inventory, and the sale-or-share figure pulled from the RoPA / data-flow map. The memo identifies the first threshold that trips and the trigger date, because downstream obligations (privacy-policy content, consumer-rights workflow, service-provider contracts) begin on that date, not on annual-report publication.

### Consumer-rights workflow — § 1798.100 through § 1798.130

The CPRA-era CCPA grants California consumers seven substantive rights, each tied to a statutory section:

- **Right to know** (§ 1798.100 and § 1798.110) — what categories and specific pieces of personal information the business has collected, the sources, the business or commercial purpose, the categories of third parties with whom it is shared.
- **Right to delete** (§ 1798.105) — subject to the § 1798.105(d) exceptions (complete a transaction, detect security incidents, comply with legal obligation, exercise free speech, enable solely internal uses aligned with consumer expectations, etc.).
- **Right to correct** (§ 1798.106) — introduced by the CPRA; the consumer can request correction of inaccurate personal information, considering the nature and purposes of the processing.
- **Right to opt-out of sale or sharing** (§ 1798.120) — "sharing" was added by the CPRA to reach cross-context behavioural advertising even where no money changes hands.
- **Right to limit use and disclosure of sensitive personal information** (§ 1798.121) — see the sensitive-PI subsection below.
- **Right to data portability** (§ 1798.100(d)) — personal information provided in a readily usable format that allows transmission to another entity without hindrance, where technically feasible.
- **Right to non-discrimination** (§ 1798.125) — a business may not deny goods, charge different prices, or provide a different level of service because the consumer exercised a right, with the narrow § 1798.125(b) "reasonable relation to value" exception for financial incentive programmes.

**The 45-day clock under § 1798.130.** The business responds to a verifiable consumer request "within 45 days of receiving the request." The clock is extendable by an additional 45 days "when reasonably necessary," with notice to the consumer within the first 45 days of the extension and the reason. The right-to-know look-back extends beyond the previous 12 months under CPRA § 1798.130(a)(2)(B) — the consumer may request information collected on or after 1 January 2022 unless doing so proves impossible or would involve disproportionate effort.

The practitioner workflow mirrors the GDPR DSAR workflow in [chapter 01](./01-gdpr-compliance-programme.md):

1. A single intake channel — toll-free number *and* a web form under § 1798.130(a)(1)(A) for a business that operates a website — routed to a tracker with SLA timers tied to the 45-day clock. A B2C business must offer at least two designated methods; a B2B business that collects only commercial-contact personal information may limit to one method per § 1798.130(a)(1)(A) exceptions.
2. Identity verification under the CPPA regulations (California Code of Regulations Title 11 § 7060 and following) proportionate to the sensitivity and value of the request — the standard is matching data points for a right-to-know request, a higher bar (signed declaration under penalty of perjury) for a specific-pieces request on sensitive personal information.
3. Routing to the systems of record for each data category — CRM, support, billing, product database, data warehouse, archives, and backups-in-scope where § 1798.105 deletion applies. The deletion routing must pass through the § 1798.105(d) exception analysis per data store, because a wholesale deletion that erases a record subject to a mandatory retention obligation is itself a violation.
4. A decision log recording the verification outcome, the exception analysis (where applicable), the systems acted on, and the response text.
5. The right-to-know look-back under § 1798.130(a)(2)(B) — personal information collected on or after 1 January 2022 unless disproportionate effort or impossibility is documented.
6. Where the corporation acts as a service provider on a customer's California-consumer data, the request is forwarded to the business under the service-provider contract (see below); the business is the respondent and the service provider assists within the 45-day window the business carries.

### Global Privacy Control and universal-opt-out signals — § 1798.135(b)

Section 1798.135 requires a business that sells or shares personal information to provide a "Do Not Sell or Share My Personal Information" link on its homepage and a separate "Limit the Use of My Sensitive Personal Information" link, *or* a single combined "Your Privacy Choices" link under the CPPA regulations. Section 1798.135(b) permits the business, in the alternative, to recognise an **opt-out preference signal** sent by a platform, technology, or mechanism — the statutory hook for the Global Privacy Control.

The CPPA regulations ([California Code of Regulations, Title 11, § 7025](https://cppa.ca.gov/regulations/pdf/ccpa_statute_eff_20260101.pdf)) detail the signal-handling requirement: the signal must be treated as a valid request to opt out of sale and sharing, must apply to the browser, device, and — if the consumer is logged in and the business can associate the signal with the account — the consumer; the business may not require the consumer to provide additional information beyond what is necessary to effectuate the opt-out; and the business must respond to the signal as soon as feasibly possible, no later than 15 business days after receipt per the regulations. Effective 1 January 2026, § 7025(c)(6) also requires the business to show the consumer that the signal has been received.

The **technical handler design** has four components:

1. **Client-side detection.** The web platform reads the `Sec-GPC: 1` request header on every inbound HTTP request and the `navigator.globalPrivacyControl` property on client-side script. A signal is present when either indicates GPC is enabled.
2. **Server-side enforcement.** On signal detection, the business suppresses sale-and-share signals downstream — no transmission of the request to third-party advertising partners, cookie-based identifiers stripped from analytics beacons, server-to-server event forwards for advertising purposes short-circuited. The suppression is immediate, not queued.
3. **Logged-in-user association.** When the browser carrying the signal is tied to an authenticated account, the opt-out is written to the account's consent record so it persists across devices. The CPPA regulations allow (but do not require) the business to ask the consumer to confirm an account-level opt-out; the practitioner default is to apply the signal account-wide and ask the consumer to confirm only if the association is ambiguous.
4. **Record of consent.** Every GPC signal received is logged (timestamp, IP-derived region, identifier where associable) and the resulting opt-out state recorded in the consent ledger. The log is the audit evidence that the signal was honoured within the statutory window.

The common failure mode is a web stack that reads the signal in the privacy banner but does not propagate it to the server-side tag-management layer; third-party pixels fire before the opt-out is recorded and the regulator treats the outbound share as a violation. The programme is wired so GPC enforcement sits above the tag-management layer, not alongside it.

### Sensitive personal information and the right to limit — §§ 1798.140(ae) and 1798.121

Sensitive personal information is defined at Cal. Civ. Code § 1798.140(ae) as a named list:

- Social Security number, driver's licence number, state identification card number, or passport number.
- Account log-in, financial account, debit card, or credit card number in combination with any required security or access code, password, or credentials allowing access to an account.
- Precise geolocation (defined at § 1798.140(w) as information derived from a device that is used or intended to locate a consumer within a geographic area equal to or less than the area of a circle with a radius of 1,850 feet).
- Racial or ethnic origin, religious or philosophical beliefs, or union membership.
- Contents of a consumer's mail, email, and text messages unless the business is the intended recipient of the communication.
- Genetic data.
- Biometric information processed for the purpose of uniquely identifying a consumer.
- Personal information collected and analysed concerning a consumer's health.
- Personal information collected and analysed concerning a consumer's sex life or sexual orientation.

**Section 1798.121** grants the consumer the right to direct the business to limit the use of sensitive personal information to the uses necessary to perform the services or provide the goods reasonably expected by an average consumer, and a narrow set of § 1798.121(a) permitted purposes (security, fraud prevention, safety, short-term transient use, servicing the account, quality verification). On a valid "limit" request, the business must stop using the SPI for inferences, cross-context behavioural advertising, profiling that produces non-trivial effects, and any purpose outside the § 1798.121(a) list.

The "Limit the Use of My Sensitive Personal Information" link is required at § 1798.135(a)(2), paired with the "Do Not Sell or Share" link or combined under the "Your Privacy Choices" alternative. The opt-out preference signal defined in the CPPA regulations at 11 CCR § 7025 operates on the § 1798.120 sale/share opt-out; the § 1798.121 right to limit SPI is generally exercised through a separate consumer-facing mechanism (the "Limit the Use of My Sensitive Personal Information" link or the combined "Your Privacy Choices" page), and § 1798.135(b) permits — but does not require — a business to recognise an opt-out preference signal as also effecting the limit-SPI right.

Operationally, the sensitive-PI inventory sits inside the broader data inventory and is tagged so the right-to-limit workflow can identify every system to suppress on a valid request. A SaaS corporation that collects none of the twelve categories can state so in its privacy policy and omit the limit-SPI link; most B2B SaaS corporations collect at least one category (log-in credentials, precise geolocation from mobile SDKs, health data in healthtech, biometric authentication in security products) and the inventory discipline is the only way to know.

Two common mis-scopes are worth calling out. First, the § 1798.140(ae) log-in-credentials category requires the combination of account log-in *with* password or security code — a bare email address collected for account creation does not trigger the category by itself. Second, the "health" and "sex-life / sexual-orientation" categories under § 1798.140(ae)(2)(A) are routed through the general permitted-purposes list at 11 CCR § 7027(m), and the inventory discipline should tag both categories so a regulation update does not require re-discovery.

### Service providers, contractors, and third parties — § 1798.140(ag), (ai), (ah)

The CCPA distinguishes three recipient classes, and the distinction drives the contract language and the sale / share characterisation:

- **Service provider** under § 1798.140(ag) — a person that processes personal information on behalf of a business and to which the business discloses personal information for a business purpose pursuant to a written contract containing the mandatory terms. The archetype is a SaaS vendor processing customer data on the business's instruction.
- **Contractor** under § 1798.140(ai) — a person to whom the business makes available a consumer's personal information for a business purpose pursuant to a written contract containing the same mandatory terms. The distinction between service provider and contractor is largely formal; both categories require the same contract architecture, and the practical difference is that "contractor" captures recipients whose role does not involve processing-on-behalf-of in the strict service-provider sense.
- **Third party** under § 1798.140(ai) (the statute uses the same subdivision with different cross-references) — any other recipient. A transfer to a third party is a "sale" or "share" under §§ 1798.140(ad) and (ah), triggering the opt-out right under § 1798.120, unless an exception applies.

The CCPA's regulatory move is that disclosures to a service provider or contractor under a compliant contract are *not* sales or shares — the service-provider / contractor contract is the mechanism by which a business routes data to a vendor without triggering the opt-out machinery.

**Mandatory contractual terms under § 1798.140(ag)(1) and (ai)(1).** The contract must:

1. Prohibit the recipient from selling or sharing the personal information.
2. Prohibit the recipient from retaining, using, or disclosing the personal information for any purpose other than the business purposes specified in the contract (including processing for a commercial purpose other than the business purposes specified, or outside of the direct business relationship between the recipient and the business).
3. Prohibit the recipient from combining the personal information with personal information received from other sources, except as permitted by the regulations for specific business purposes.
4. Require the recipient to comply with the applicable obligations under the CCPA and provide the same level of privacy protection as the CCPA requires of businesses.
5. Grant the business the right to take reasonable and appropriate steps to ensure that the recipient uses the personal information in a manner consistent with the business's CCPA obligations.
6. Require the recipient to notify the business if it makes a determination that it can no longer meet its obligations under the CCPA.
7. Grant the business the right, upon notice, to take reasonable and appropriate steps to stop and remediate unauthorised use of personal information.

The **sub-processor flow-down** requires the service provider or contractor, when engaging its own sub-service-providers or sub-contractors, to pass the same mandatory terms down the chain under § 1798.140(ag)(2) / (ai)(2). The chain of contracts from business to service provider to sub-service-provider carries the no-sale / no-share certification unbroken.

**The no-sale / no-share certification.** The practitioner draft includes an express service-provider certification — a signed statement that the recipient does not sell or share personal information, does not retain or use it outside the contract, and will flow the obligations down to sub-processors. This is the clause an enterprise customer's procurement team looks for first in the DPA; the structural placement of the clause as a schedule to the DPA is authored in [mod-109 chapter 01](../mod-109-commercial-contracts-ip-and-legal-ops/01-customer-contract-suite-msa-sla-dpa-security-aup.md).

**Audit-right posture.** The § 1798.140(ag)(1)(E) "reasonable and appropriate steps to ensure" right is the CCPA analogue of the GDPR Article 28(3)(h) audit right. The operational compromise is the same as under the DPA — the service provider offers its SOC 2 Type II report plus a right to on-site audit triggered by a specific incident or materially insufficient report. The CCPA does not mandate a specific audit methodology; the regulation requires that the steps be "reasonable and appropriate," which the CPPA has interpreted to include documented vendor-management procedures and periodic attestation review.

**Common contract failures.** Three failure patterns repeatedly show up in enterprise diligence reviews. First, a corporation that describes a recipient as a "service provider" in its privacy policy but whose vendor contract lacks the seven mandatory terms — the recipient defaults to "third party" status and every disclosure to that recipient is a sale or share. Second, a contract that carries the no-sale / no-share language but omits the sub-processor flow-down, so the first downstream contract breaks the chain. Third, a contract that permits the recipient to "combine with other data" for "service-improvement purposes" without the § 1798.140(ag)(3) exception language — a direct violation that voids the service-provider characterisation. The practitioner remediation is to template the mandatory-terms schedule in the CLM stack and refuse to execute a vendor DPA that strips any of the seven terms.

### CPPA risk assessments, cybersecurity audits, and ADMT regulations

The California Privacy Protection Agency, established under § 1798.199.10 and following, has rulemaking authority over several categories of CCPA regulation. Three Agency rulemakings are load-bearing for the compliance programme:

- **Cybersecurity audits** under § 1798.185(a)(14) — the Agency is directed to issue regulations requiring businesses whose processing presents significant risk to consumers' privacy or security to perform an annual cybersecurity audit, including defining the scope and the independence requirement for the auditor. The [final regulations](https://cppa.ca.gov/announcements/2025/20250923.html) were approved by the California Office of Administrative Law on 23 September 2025 and took effect 1 January 2026, with compliance phased in by business size (the largest tier first) and the audit performed by a qualified independent auditor; businesses certify completion annually to the Agency rather than filing the audit report itself.
- **Risk assessments** under § 1798.185(a)(15) — the Agency is directed to require businesses whose processing presents significant risk to submit risk assessments to the Agency on a regular basis, with specified contents and submission schedules. The risk-assessment regulation parallels the GDPR Article 35 DPIA in function but with Agency submission rather than DPA-held documentation. The final regulations adopted on 23 September 2025 require applicable businesses to begin conducting risk assessments from 1 January 2026, with a first attestation and summary submission to the Agency due by 1 April 2028 (annually thereafter).
- **Automated decision-making technology (ADMT)** under § 1798.185(a)(16) — the Agency is directed to issue regulations governing access and opt-out rights with respect to a business's use of automated decision-making technology, including profiling. The ADMT regulations parallel GDPR Article 22 in function. The final rules were adopted alongside the cybersecurity-audit and risk-assessment regulations on 23 September 2025 and took effect 1 January 2026; ADMT-specific compliance obligations for businesses that use ADMT to make significant decisions begin 1 January 2027.

The practitioner posture is to track CPPA board-meeting agendas and public-comment deadlines, to pre-stage a risk-assessment template that can be adapted to the final regulation text, and to brief the audit committee ([mod-111](../mod-111-corporate-governance-board-operations-and-officer-duties/)) on the Agency's rulemaking cadence so cybersecurity-audit capacity is budgeted before the rule bites.

The ADMT regulation is the item most likely to reshape the engineering roadmap. If the final rule tracks the draft structure, any in-product automated decision that produces legal or similarly significant effects (hiring screens, credit and underwriting, access control, content moderation at scale, workplace monitoring) will trigger a pre-use notice, an opt-out right, and an access-to-logic right analogous to GDPR Article 22. The sequencing implication is that an AI-in-the-loop feature in the backlog today should be evaluated against the draft ADMT rule rather than against the general CCPA baseline; see [chapter 09](./09-ai-vendor-and-ai-model-provider-governance-regime.md) for the ADMT and GDPR Article 22 crosswalk.

### Interaction with the mod-109 DPA

The CCPA service-provider addendum is a structural schedule to the Data Processing Addendum. The DPA's architecture — Annex I scope of processing, Annex II technical and organisational measures, sub-processor list, international-transfer stack, breach-notification commitment — is authored in [mod-109 chapter 01](../mod-109-commercial-contracts-ip-and-legal-ops/01-customer-contract-suite-msa-sla-dpa-security-aup.md); the CCPA addendum is the schedule that carries the § 1798.140(ag) / (ai) mandatory terms and the no-sale / no-share certification.

The structural split is deliberate. The DPA's architecture rarely changes; the CCPA-specific schedule changes whenever the Agency updates the service-provider regulations or the mandatory-terms list. By placing the CCPA content in a schedule rather than in the DPA body, the corporation can update the schedule on notice when the regulations move without re-papering the DPA with every enterprise customer. [Chapter 03](./03-us-state-privacy-landscape-and-rolling-registration.md) treats the parallel schedules for Colorado, Virginia, Connecticut, and the other US state statutes; the DPA ends up with a schedule stack that mirrors the US state map.

## Concrete example

Vectorline Analytics — the Series-B US-headquartered B2B SaaS introduced in [chapter 01](./01-gdpr-compliance-programme.md) — runs its CCPA threshold analysis in the same 90-day sprint as its GDPR programme. The findings and the build:

**Threshold analysis.** Vectorline's audited FY revenue is $38M, tripping the $25M revenue threshold under § 1798.140(d)(1)(A). The consumer-count analysis finds 182,000 California-resident records across the marketing database (free-tier signups, newsletter subscribers, event-attendee lists) and the customer end-user database — the 100,000 threshold also trips. The sale-or-share analysis finds that the marketing stack forwards hashed email identifiers to advertising partners for lookalike-audience modelling; under the CPRA definition of "sharing," this is a share. Vectorline is a "business" under § 1798.140(d) on three independent grounds, with the trigger date fixed at the first day of the calendar year in which revenue crossed $25M.

**Consumer-rights workflow.** A single `privacy@vectorline.com` intake plus an in-product "Your Privacy Choices" link route to the DSAR tracker that already handles GDPR requests. The tracker carries parallel SLA timers — 30 days for GDPR Article 12(3), 45 days for CCPA § 1798.130 — and a routing map covering the right-to-know, right-to-delete, right-to-correct, opt-out-of-sale-or-share, and limit-SPI flows. Identity verification is proportionate to the request sensitivity. For requests on customer end-user data where Vectorline is the service provider, the tracker forwards the request to the customer business under the service-provider contract.

**GPC handler.** The web platform reads `Sec-GPC: 1` at the edge-proxy layer and writes the opt-out state into a cookie and (for authenticated sessions) the user's consent record. The tag-management layer consults the consent record before firing any third-party pixel; the marketing stack's hashed-email forwarding to advertising partners is suppressed on signal detection. A nightly audit job samples GPC-signalled sessions and verifies no outbound shares occurred; the audit log is the evidence for the CPPA.

**Sensitive-PI inventory.** The inventory finds three SPI categories in scope: account log-in credentials (customer end-user authentication), precise geolocation (optional mobile-SDK feature on the customer-facing app), and health data (one healthtech customer tenant whose processing instruction carries HIPAA-adjacent data). The "Limit the Use of My Sensitive Personal Information" link is added to the "Your Privacy Choices" page; on a valid limit request, the mobile-SDK geolocation is suppressed for the consumer and the customer-tenant healthtech flow routes through the § 1798.121(a) exception for providing the goods or services expected.

**Service-provider / contractor contract map.** Vectorline's vendor inventory lists 47 sub-processors. The CCPA schedule to Vectorline's standard DPA (authored structurally in [mod-109 chapter 01](../mod-109-commercial-contracts-ip-and-legal-ops/01-customer-contract-suite-msa-sla-dpa-security-aup.md) and populated with the § 1798.140(ag) / (ai) language here) is flowed down to every sub-processor; the three that cannot sign the schedule are re-scoped to non-personal-information processing or replaced. The inventory is published at a public URL with a 30-day change-notification mechanic that honours both GDPR Article 28(2) and the CCPA sub-processor flow-down.

**CPPA regulatory tracking.** The VP of Privacy subscribes to CPPA board-meeting notices and public-comment windows, briefs the audit committee ([mod-111](../mod-111-corporate-governance-board-operations-and-officer-duties/)) quarterly on the state of the cybersecurity-audit, risk-assessment, and ADMT rulemakings, and pre-stages a risk-assessment template keyed to the latest Agency draft. The template is updated on each draft iteration so Vectorline is not starting from scratch when the final rule publishes.

At the end of the sprint, Vectorline's privacy counsel can produce the threshold memo, the consumer-rights workflow with closed-loop timers on the 45-day clock, the GPC handler audit log, the sensitive-PI inventory with limit-SPI routing, the service-provider contract map with the § 1798.140(ag) / (ai) language flowed down, and the CPPA regulatory tracker — the full CCPA programme the next enterprise diligence cycle will ask for.

## Summary

- CCPA / CPRA applicability rests on a three-prong threshold under Cal. Civ. Code § 1798.140(d) — $25M in annual gross revenue, 100,000 California consumers or households, or 50%+ of revenue from selling or sharing personal information — with the § 1798.120(c) opt-in overlay for minors' data. The threshold analysis is an annual memo, refreshed from audited financials and the data inventory.
- The consumer-rights workflow covers the right to know (§ 1798.100 / § 1798.110), delete (§ 1798.105), correct (§ 1798.106), opt-out of sale or sharing (§ 1798.120), limit use and disclosure of sensitive PI (§ 1798.121), portability (§ 1798.100(d)), and non-discrimination (§ 1798.125), with a 45-day response clock under § 1798.130 extendable by 45 more days with notice.
- The Global Privacy Control signal under § 1798.135(b) and the CPPA regulations at California Code of Regulations Title 11 §§ 7025–7026 must be honoured server-side, propagated above the tag-management layer, and logged as a record of consent. A GPC handler that reads the signal in the banner but does not suppress downstream shares is the common failure mode.
- Sensitive personal information under § 1798.140(ae) is a defined list of twelve categories — SSN / driver's licence / passport / state ID, financial account with access credentials, precise geolocation, racial or ethnic origin, religious or philosophical beliefs, union membership, contents of non-addressed mail / email / texts, genetic data, biometric for unique identification, health, and sex-life / sexual-orientation. The § 1798.121 right to limit routes to a narrow § 1798.121(a) permitted-purpose set.
- Service-provider (§ 1798.140(ag)), contractor (§ 1798.140(ai)), and third-party distinctions drive the contract architecture. Disclosures under a compliant service-provider or contractor contract are not sales or shares; the mandatory-terms list, the sub-processor flow-down, and the no-sale / no-share certification are the contract's load-bearing clauses.
- CPPA rulemaking on cybersecurity audits, risk assessments, and automated decision-making technology under § 1798.185(a)(14)–(16) is the volatile tier of the programme. Track the Agency's rulemaking cadence, pre-stage templates against the latest draft, and brief the audit committee quarterly.
- The CCPA service-provider addendum is a schedule to the DPA. The DPA's architecture is authored structurally in [mod-109 chapter 01](../mod-109-commercial-contracts-ip-and-legal-ops/01-customer-contract-suite-msa-sla-dpa-security-aup.md); this chapter owns the substantive CCPA content the schedule carries. [Chapter 03](./03-us-state-privacy-landscape-and-rolling-registration.md) extends the schedule stack to the rest of the US state map.
