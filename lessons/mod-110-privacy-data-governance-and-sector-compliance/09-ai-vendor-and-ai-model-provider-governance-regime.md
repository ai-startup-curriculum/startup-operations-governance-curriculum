# 9. The AI-vendor / AI-model-provider governance regime — input classification, training-on-inputs prohibition, model-provenance, EU AI Act GPAI, and the model-risk-committee interface

> The AI-vendor governance regime is the privacy, contractual, and oversight posture that has to be standing before the first production feature routes a customer prompt through a foundation-model API. Input-data classification drives the vendor-DPA flow-down; the training-on-inputs prohibition closes the leakage channel; model-provenance disclosure makes the outbound customer commitment honest; the EU AI Act GPAI addendum captures Articles 51/53/55 pass-through; and the model-risk committee is the gate for high-risk use cases. What this regime does *not* own is the AI-safety-technical substance — that belongs to the chief-AI-officer and head-of-AI-governance tracks.

## Motivation

A Series-B B2B SaaS corporation ships its first AI-in-the-loop feature in a quarter where three things happen in parallel: a product-manager writes a spec that routes customer support tickets through a foundation-model classifier; an enterprise prospect's security-review questionnaire adds a page of AI-specific due-diligence questions that did not exist eighteen months earlier; and EU AI Act Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 moves another operationalisation milestone past its effective date. None of the three events is independently a crisis. All three together mean the corporation needs a standing AI-vendor governance regime — a posture, not a one-off checklist — before the feature ships, because the first customer DSAR against the AI feature, the first enterprise procurement team that asks for the model-provenance disclosure, and the first regulator inquiry about GPAI pass-through will not accept "we are working on it" as a response.

This chapter is the AI overlay on top of the general vendor-privacy-risk programme in [chapter 08](./08-vendor-privacy-risk-programme.md). The structural anatomy of the AI-vendor contract vehicle and the AI-DPA itself — clause-by-clause — is authored in [mod-109 chapter 06](../mod-109-commercial-contracts-ip-and-legal-ops/06-ai-vendor-contracts-and-ai-dpa-pattern.md); this chapter owns the substance that fills those clauses. The internal-employee AI acceptable-use policy — the posture the corporation takes toward its own employees' use of AI tools — is authored in [mod-108](../mod-108-culture-employee-experience-and-dei/). The AI-risk sub-committee charter that this chapter calls into is authored in [mod-111 chapter 04](../mod-111-board-governance-and-investor-relations/04-ai-risk-sub-committee-and-model-risk-governance.md). The AI-safety-technical substance — evaluation suite design, red-teaming methodology, interpretability work, agentic-system safety — is explicitly out of scope and is deferred to `chief-ai-officer-learning` and `head-of-ai-governance-learning`.

## Core concepts

### Input-data classification — the four classes and why the classification drives everything downstream

The first analytical step before any AI-vendor contract is negotiated or any production AI feature is shipped is to classify the input data that will flow through the model. Four classes cover substantially all the cases; each class carries a different consent posture, lawful-basis posture, allowed-use posture, and downstream contractual posture.

**Class 1 — Customer data.** Personal data or confidential business data that belongs to or is controlled by the corporation's enterprise customers and that is routed through an AI model in the course of delivering the product. Examples: support-ticket content, uploaded documents, chat transcripts, CRM records, product telemetry. The corporation is the Article 4(8) GDPR processor of this data under its customer-facing DPA (see [mod-109 chapter 01](../mod-109-commercial-contracts-ip-and-legal-ops/01-customer-contract-suite-msa-sla-dpa-security-aup.md)); the foundation-model provider is the corporation's sub-processor. The lawful basis is inherited from the customer's own lawful basis for processing the underlying personal data; the corporation does not get to choose a different basis. The downstream contractual consequence: the model provider's DPA must flow-down the Article 28 processor obligations, must appear on the public sub-processor list, must honour the customer's Article 15–22 rights requests through the corporation, and must be contractually prohibited from any secondary use of the data. This is the class with the heaviest contractual burden and the class that drives the AI-DPA posture.

**Class 2 — Employee data.** Personal data about the corporation's own employees that is routed through an AI model — for example, a résumé screener, a performance-review summariser, an internal-helpdesk assistant, or an onboarding chatbot. The corporation is the Article 4(7) GDPR controller of this data. The lawful-basis analysis runs through [mod-103](../mod-103-people-ops-talent-and-performance/) (people operations) and [mod-104](../mod-104-compensation-equity-and-benefits/) (compensation and benefits); employment-context processing generally cannot rely on Art. 6(1)(a) consent because of the power-imbalance analysis in EDPB Guidelines 05/2020, and typically rests on Art. 6(1)(b) contract (for payroll-adjacent processing) or Art. 6(1)(f) legitimate interests (for most else) with the balancing test documented. Works-council or employee-representative consultation obligations may apply in several EU member states. The downstream contractual posture is still a processor-sub-processor flow-down with the model provider, but the DPIA and consultation burden is heavier because the AI feature is operating on data where the corporation is directly answerable to the employee as a data subject.

**Class 3 — Product data.** Internal operational data that is not customer personal data and not employee personal data — system logs, aggregated telemetry, internal documentation, codebases, bug reports — routed through an AI model for an internal operational purpose. Example: an internal AI assistant that classifies and routes inbound support tickets before a human reviews them; a model that summarises internal engineering tickets for a weekly digest. The data-protection posture is lighter because the data is not personal data in the first place, but three sub-cases revert the analysis to one of the other classes: (i) a log line that embeds a customer identifier reverts to Class 1; (ii) a bug report that pastes in a user prompt reverts to Class 1; (iii) a codebase that embeds secrets reverts to the secrets-handling posture in [mod-112](../mod-112-enterprise-risk-insurance-and-compliance/). The classification must be done at the field level, not at the dataset level, because a single leaked field can reclassify the whole flow.

**Class 4 — Public data.** Data that is either genuinely public (public web pages, public filings, open-source corpora) or has been obtained under an explicit licence that permits the intended AI use. The data-protection posture is lightest but is not zero: the GDPR applies to publicly available personal data (CJEU *Fashion ID* C-40/17 and subsequent clarification), the CCPA applies to personal information that has been made publicly available only under narrow conditions in Cal. Civ. Code § 1798.140(v)(2), and the IP posture on scraped data is separately governed by the copyright regime, the database-sui-generis regime in Europe, the terms-of-use regime of the source site, and (where applicable) the TDM opt-out regime under Article 4 of Directive (EU) 2019/790 — which EU AI Act Article 53(1)(c) now requires GPAI providers placing a model on the EU market to respect, and which national courts (e.g. the Hamburg Higher Regional Court in *Kneschke v. LAION*, December 2024) have construed as requiring the opt-out to be expressed in a machine-readable form. The downstream contractual posture on the AI-vendor side is lighter — the provider does not need the same processor commitments — but the corporation's own IP and anti-scraping posture still has to be clean, and the US fair-use analysis on training-corpus scraping is an active-litigation area that practitioners should re-verify against the most recent dispositive ruling before concluding the posture is settled.

The classification is the first artefact the model-risk committee asks for and the first artefact the AI-DPA negotiation refers to. A corporation that has not done the classification cannot answer the vendor's questions ("what categories of data will you send to the API?") or the customer's questions ("what categories of our data do you route through the model?") without improvising, and improvising at that interface is how the gap between the outbound customer commitment and the inbound vendor commitment opens.

### Training-on-inputs prohibition — the contractual flow-down to the foundation-model provider

The single clause the AI-vendor contract has to get right is the prohibition on the model provider using the corporation's inputs — prompts, RAG context, tool-call arguments, embeddings, system prompts, outputs derived from them — to train, fine-tune, evaluate, or otherwise improve any model the provider sells. The clause structure is authored in [mod-109 chapter 06](../mod-109-commercial-contracts-ip-and-legal-ops/06-ai-vendor-contracts-and-ai-dpa-pattern.md); this chapter owns the substantive posture the clause has to deliver.

The enterprise-tier default across the major model providers is that commercial API traffic is not used to train the provider's models. The specific posture varies by provider, by product tier, and over time, and should be re-verified against the provider's current trust-centre documentation before every contract renewal. The pattern at a general level:

- A commercial / enterprise API tier that commits to no training on customer inputs by default, typically with a short abuse-detection retention window and an exception for data subject to a legal hold.
- An available zero-data-retention election for workloads that cannot tolerate even the abuse-detection window, typically requiring admin configuration and sometimes a separate contract flag.
- Admin-level logging-retention controls (workspace or organisation-level) that let the customer configure how long inference traffic is retained, and whether it is retained at all.
- A consumer / free tier with the opposite default — training on user inputs enabled unless the user opts out — which is why employees prototyping against production data on consumer accounts is the leakage channel that the internal AI acceptable-use policy in [mod-108](../mod-108-culture-employee-experience-and-dei/) has to close.

The specific posture at each major foundation-model provider — Anthropic workspace-admin controls and ZDR election, OpenAI enterprise retention defaults and ZDR availability for qualifying endpoints, Microsoft Azure OpenAI DPA commitments and abuse-monitoring opt-out, Google Vertex AI and Gemini-for-Workspace training commitments, and AWS Bedrock per-model-provider retention defaults — moves between product releases and should be re-read off each provider's current trust-centre documentation at every contract renewal rather than relied on from a prior memo.

The practical posture the corporation adopts: for Class 1 and Class 2 traffic, insist on the no-training commitment, elect zero-data-retention where available, document the elected configuration in the vendor record, and re-verify at renewal. For Class 3 traffic, the same posture is still the default even though the data-protection burden is lighter, because the leakage-into-training-set risk remains. For Class 4 traffic, the posture may be softer if the data is genuinely public, but the IP and competitive-intelligence angle (do not feed competitor-sensitive data into a model that competitors also use) usually recommends the same default.

### Model-provenance disclosure — model identity, training-data governance, and the AI BOM

The second substantive clause: the vendor must disclose which model family and version is serving the corporation's traffic, must disclose the provider's training-data governance posture at a general level (publicly scraped corpora / licensed corpora / synthetic / customer-tenant fine-tuning data), and must disclose known limitations and the provider's own evaluation posture on relevant benchmarks. The clause interlocks with the emerging AI-bill-of-materials pattern.

The AI BOM is the AI analogue of the software BOM authored in [mod-109 chapter 05](../mod-109-commercial-contracts-ip-and-legal-ops/05-open-source-hygiene-and-sbom-programme.md): a machine-readable record of the AI components in the deployed system, their provenance, and their known characteristics. The convergent practice is drawing on three reference patterns:

- **Model Cards** — originally proposed by Mitchell et al. (2019) and now a de-facto convention on the Hugging Face Hub and in Google's model-release documentation. A Model Card captures the intended use, out-of-scope use, training-data summary, evaluation results on standard benchmarks, and known limitations of a specific model artefact.
- **NIST AI 600-1** — the Generative AI Profile published under the NIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0 in July 2024. The profile enumerates the risks specific to generative AI systems (confabulation, data-privacy, dangerous-content, information-integrity, intellectual-property, and others) and recommends the Govern / Map / Measure / Manage functional posture against each. The corporation's AI-vendor record maps the provider's disclosed posture against the NIST AI 600-1 risk categories.
- **The emerging AI-BOM proposals** under CISA's AI security guidance and the ISO/IEC 42001 AI-management-system standard published in December 2023.

The practical posture: the corporation's AI-vendor record includes, for each production model, the model family and version, a link to the provider's current Model Card or equivalent, the provider's training-data-governance disclosure at the level the provider publishes, and a mapping to the NIST AI 600-1 risk categories. The record is reviewed by the model-risk committee at onboarding and at each version bump.

### EU AI Act GPAI addendum — Articles 51, 53, 55 and the pass-through to the deployer

Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, the EU AI Act, entered into force on 1 August 2024 and operationalises in stages. The general-purpose-AI-model obligations in Chapter V (Articles 51–56) are the part of the Act that bites on the corporation's AI-vendor relationships directly, and they became applicable on 2 August 2025 for models placed on the EU market on or after that date; models already on the EU market before 2 August 2025 must come into compliance by 2 August 2027. The Commission's General-Purpose AI Code of Practice — the voluntary instrument that a GPAI provider may adhere to in order to demonstrate compliance with the Chapter V obligations — was published on 10 July 2025 and approved by the Commission on 1 August 2025; the AI Office continues to publish implementing guidance and may still issue further templates (notably for the Article 53(1)(d) training-content summary). Commission enforcement for systemic-risk GPAI providers under Article 101 (fines) begins on 2 August 2026. A separate tier of obligations in Title III bites on high-risk AI *systems* placed on the market or put into service — these are addressed in the use-case-level analysis below.

**Article 51 — GPAI classification and the systemic-risk tier.** Article 51 defines a general-purpose AI model and separates out a sub-class of GPAI models with systemic risk. Article 51(2) sets a cumulative-training-compute threshold (expressed in floating-point operations) above which a model is presumed to have systemic-risk capability; the Commission is empowered to adjust the threshold and to designate models above or below it. The practical consequence for the downstream deployer is that the vendor's self-classification against Article 51 — is it a GPAI model, is it a systemic-risk GPAI model — materially changes the compliance evidence the vendor must hand down and that the deployer must retain.

**Article 53 — GPAI provider obligations.** Every GPAI model provider must (i) prepare and keep up-to-date technical documentation of the model, including the elements enumerated in Annex XI (training process, evaluation, integration guidance, compute and energy use); (ii) make information and documentation available to downstream AI-system providers under Article 53(1)(b) so that the downstream provider can comply with its own obligations; (iii) put in place a policy to respect Union copyright law, including the text-and-data-mining opt-out regime under Article 4(3) of Directive (EU) 2019/790, under Article 53(1)(c); and (iv) publish a sufficiently detailed summary of the content used for training under Article 53(1)(d), on the template that the AI Office is empowered to publish. The downstream deployer's AI-vendor contract has to require the provider to flow these artefacts through on request, because the deployer may be asked for them by its own regulators or enterprise customers.

**Article 55 — systemic-risk GPAI obligations.** A GPAI model with systemic risk under Article 51 carries additional obligations under Article 55: model evaluation under state-of-the-art protocols including adversarial testing (Art. 55(1)(a)); assessment and mitigation of possible systemic risks at Union level (Art. 55(1)(b)); tracking, documenting, and reporting to the AI Office and, where appropriate, to national competent authorities, serious incidents and corrective measures without undue delay (Art. 55(1)(c)); and ensuring an adequate level of cybersecurity protection for the model and its physical infrastructure (Art. 55(1)(d)). The downstream deployer's contractual expectation is that a vendor whose model is classified in the systemic-risk tier can produce, on request, the evidence that Article 55 has been operationalised — the evaluation artefacts, the serious-incident register, the cybersecurity-control attestation. The deployer does not perform the Article 55 work itself but does need to be able to demonstrate that it has selected a provider that does.

### Model-risk committee integration — high-risk use cases and the DPIA / FRIA gate

Not every AI use case is a model-risk-committee matter. The committee's remit — the charter is in [mod-111 chapter 04](../mod-111-board-governance-and-investor-relations/04-ai-risk-sub-committee-and-model-risk-governance.md) — is to gate the subset of use cases where the downstream impact on data subjects, customers, or third parties warrants board-adjacent oversight. Three triggers put a use case in scope.

**Trigger 1 — Annex III of the EU AI Act.** Annex III enumerates the high-risk AI use cases: biometric identification and categorisation of natural persons, management and operation of critical infrastructure, education and vocational training (including access and admissions decisions), employment and worker management (including recruitment, filtering, performance evaluation, and termination decisions), access to and enjoyment of essential private services and essential public services and benefits (including credit scoring and risk-based pricing of life/health insurance), law enforcement, migration/asylum/border control, and administration of justice and democratic processes. A system that falls within one of these use cases is a high-risk AI system; the deployer under Article 26 of the Act has operational-use obligations, including the Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment under Article 27 for certain deployers (public-law bodies, operators of essential services, and operators in specified high-risk categories).

**Trigger 2 — GDPR Article 22 solely-automated-decision.** Article 22 of the GDPR gives a data subject the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing, including profiling, which produces legal effects concerning them or similarly significantly affects them. An AI use case that makes the decision end-to-end — no meaningful human review — and that significantly affects a natural person is in Article 22 scope. The deployer must have a lawful basis for the automated decision (Art. 22(2)(a) contract, 22(2)(b) Union or member-state law, or 22(2)(c) explicit consent), must offer human intervention, and must do a DPIA under Article 35.

**Trigger 3 — sector-specific regulated decisioning.** Hiring decisions under the New York City Local Law 144 bias-audit regime (effective July 2023) and the Colorado AI Act (SB 24-205, signed May 2024, effective 1 February 2026); credit decisioning under the Equal Credit Opportunity Act and Regulation B adverse-action-notice regime; housing decisioning under the Fair Housing Act; healthcare decisioning under the sector-specific rules addressed in [chapter 04](./04-hipaa-compliance-for-healthtech.md). Each regime overlays the general AI Act and GDPR analysis with its own bias-audit, adverse-action-notice, and documentation obligations.

A use case that trips any trigger flows through the model-risk-committee intake. The intake artefacts: the input-data classification from the section above; the DPIA under GDPR Article 35; the FRIA under EU AI Act Article 27 if the deployer is in an Article 27 class; the model-provenance record; the AI-vendor-contract posture; the human-in-the-loop design; the evaluation and monitoring plan (the substance of which is handed off to the AI-safety side, below); the risk-register entry (the register is maintained under [mod-112](../mod-112-enterprise-risk-insurance-and-compliance/)); and the quarterly reporting cadence to the board AI-risk sub-committee. The committee either approves the use case for production, approves with conditions, or returns it for rework. A use case that has not cleared the committee does not ship.

### Boundary with chief-AI-officer-learning and head-of-AI-governance-learning — the AI-safety technical substance

This chapter and this curriculum own the *privacy, contractual, and governance posture* of the corporation's AI-vendor relationships and AI use cases. What they do not own, and what every reader should understand is being explicitly handed off, is the *AI-safety-technical substance*: the design of the evaluation suite that measures a specific model's capability and safety on the deployment's own benchmarks; the red-teaming methodology that adversarially probes the deployed system; the model-interpretability work that reasons about the model's internal representations; the model-level controls that constrain generation at inference time; the agentic-system-safety work that governs tool-use, memory, and autonomy in agent architectures; and the systemic-risk analysis that an Article 55 GPAI provider is obligated to conduct and that a sophisticated deployer may wish to replicate.

That substance lives in the chief-AI-officer and head-of-AI-governance role tracks — specifically in `chief-ai-officer-learning` and `head-of-ai-governance-learning` — and is beyond the scope of a general-counsel / head-of-people / CFO-adjacent curriculum. The boundary is clean: the governance side selects the use case, classifies the inputs, writes the contract, documents the DPIA/FRIA, and gates deployment through the model-risk committee. The AI-safety side evaluates and red-teams the specific model deployment, tunes the model-level controls, and designs the monitoring that feeds back into the governance loop. A corporation that runs only the governance side has a paper posture with no measurement; a corporation that runs only the AI-safety side has measurement with no accountability. The two sides are meant to interlock, and the model-risk-committee intake is the interlock point.

## Concrete example

A fictional Series-B B2B SaaS, **Helioform Logistics** — a 90-person warehouse-management platform selling to mid-market third-party-logistics operators in the UK, Germany, and the US — launches its first customer-facing AI feature: an in-app "Helio Assistant" that answers end-user questions about inventory, routing, and shipment status by retrieving over the customer's own data and generating a natural-language answer through a foundation-model API. The assistant is behind a feature flag with a beta cohort of eight customer tenants scheduled for GA in the next quarter.

**Input-data classification.** The feature routes three categories of data to the model API. Customer prompts (Class 1 — customer data, routed on behalf of the enterprise customer); retrieval context pulled from the customer tenant's warehouse database (Class 1 — customer data); and a system prompt plus a small library of few-shot examples (Class 3 — product data, authored by Helioform, no personal data). The Class 1 inputs are the heavy flow and drive the AI-DPA posture.

**Vendor-DPA posture with the foundation-model provider.** Helioform selects a commercial-tier API from a major foundation-model provider, negotiated through the corporation's standard vendor-contract vehicle (see [mod-109 chapter 03](../mod-109-commercial-contracts-ip-and-legal-ops/03-vendor-contract-suite-and-onboarding-workflow.md)) with the AI-specific clauses from [mod-109 chapter 06](../mod-109-commercial-contracts-ip-and-legal-ops/06-ai-vendor-contracts-and-ai-dpa-pattern.md) layered in. The substantive posture: no-training commitment on API traffic; a zero-data-retention election at the workspace or organisation level where the chosen provider offers one for the endpoint in use (the specific configuration name and availability should be confirmed on the provider's then-current product documentation); the provider listed as a sub-processor on Helioform's public sub-processor list with 30-day notice on new sub-sub-processors; EU-region endpoint for Helioform's UK and German tenants; breach-notification commitment at 24 hours to Helioform (to support Helioform's 72-hour commitment to its enterprise customers under GDPR Article 33).

**EU AI Act GPAI pass-through.** The model the provider is serving is self-classified by the provider as a GPAI model. The provider's classification against Article 51(2) — whether the model is in the systemic-risk tier, presumed under Article 51(2) where the cumulative training compute exceeds 10^25 FLOPs and otherwise capable of designation by the Commission on the Scientific Panel's qualified alert — is read off the provider's current regulatory-disclosure page, and the Article 52 notification obligation on the provider (notify the Commission without undue delay and in any event within two weeks of meeting or being on course to meet the threshold) means the self-classification can change between contract renewals. The vendor contract requires the provider to make the Article 53 Annex XI technical documentation available to Helioform on request, to flow through the Article 53(1)(c) copyright-policy and Article 53(1)(d) training-content-summary artefacts, and — if the model is in the systemic-risk tier — to make the Article 55 evaluation and serious-incident evidence available. Helioform retains those artefacts in its AI-vendor record and can produce them for an enterprise-customer due-diligence request or a supervisory-authority inquiry.

**Article 22 solely-automated-decision analysis.** The Helio Assistant produces natural-language answers that end-users read and act on; the end-user remains the decision-maker on any downstream action (approving a shipment, adjusting an order). The feature is therefore *not* a solely-automated-decision under GDPR Article 22 because the human is in the loop on the operative decision. Helioform documents the analysis in the DPIA for the feature; the DPIA is the artefact the customer's data-protection officer asks for during the AI-security-review. A hypothetical extension of the feature — a future "Helio Auto-Replenish" that automatically generates purchase orders without human review — would trip Article 22 and would re-enter the model-risk committee for a different posture (explicit consent under Art. 22(2)(c), or Art. 22(2)(a) contract basis with explicit disclosure, plus human-review-on-request).

**Model-risk-committee intake.** The Helio Assistant use case is routed through the model-risk-committee intake even though it is not Annex III high-risk and not Article 22 scope, because the input class (Class 1 customer data) and the customer-visible surface put it on the committee's standing agenda. The intake pack: the input-data classification; the DPIA under GDPR Art. 35; the AI-vendor-contract posture summary; the model-provenance record (model family, version, Model Card link, NIST AI 600-1 risk mapping); the evaluation and monitoring plan (authored on the AI-safety side under `head-of-ai-governance-learning` — *not* in this chapter); the human-in-the-loop design; the rollout plan (feature-flag cohort, GA gate); the risk-register entry; and the proposed quarterly reporting cadence to the board AI-risk sub-committee. The committee approves with two conditions: (i) a beta-cohort evaluation checkpoint before GA, with the evaluation substance designed on the AI-safety side; (ii) an abuse-monitoring dashboard with a named operator-on-call. The feature ships to GA the following quarter with both conditions closed.

## Summary

- Input-data classification into the four classes — customer, employee, product, public — is the first analytical step and drives every downstream contractual, lawful-basis, and allowed-use posture.
- The training-on-inputs prohibition is the single most important AI-vendor clause; the enterprise-tier default across major providers is no-training-on-API-traffic, with zero-data-retention available as a stricter election, re-verified at every renewal.
- Model-provenance disclosure captures the model family, version, training-data-governance posture, and known limitations; the AI BOM pattern — anchored on Model Cards, NIST AI 600-1, and emerging ISO/IEC 42001 practice — is the artefact structure.
- EU AI Act Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 Articles 51, 53, and 55 govern the GPAI-provider obligations the vendor owes, and the AI-vendor contract has to require pass-through of the Article 53 Annex XI documentation and, where applicable, the Article 55 systemic-risk evidence.
- Model-risk-committee intake gates high-risk use cases — triggered by EU AI Act Annex III, GDPR Article 22 solely-automated-decisioning, or sector-specific bias-audit regimes — with the DPIA under GDPR Art. 35 and the FRIA under AI Act Art. 27 as the standing artefacts.
- The AI-safety-technical substance — evaluation-suite design, red-teaming methodology, interpretability, agentic-system safety — is explicitly handed off to `chief-ai-officer-learning` and `head-of-ai-governance-learning`; the governance side selects and contracts, the AI-safety side evaluates and red-teams, and the two interlock at the model-risk-committee intake.
- The regime sits on top of the AI-DPA structural anatomy in [mod-109 chapter 06](../mod-109-commercial-contracts-ip-and-legal-ops/06-ai-vendor-contracts-and-ai-dpa-pattern.md), the internal-employee AI AUP in [mod-108](../mod-108-culture-employee-experience-and-dei/), the vendor-privacy-risk programme in [chapter 08](./08-vendor-privacy-risk-programme.md), and the AI-risk sub-committee charter in [mod-111 chapter 04](../mod-111-board-governance-and-investor-relations/04-ai-risk-sub-committee-and-model-risk-governance.md).
- The regime has to be standing *before* the first production AI-in-the-loop feature ships; a corporation that writes it during the first enterprise AI-security-review will be improvising at the interface that most needs not to be improvised.
