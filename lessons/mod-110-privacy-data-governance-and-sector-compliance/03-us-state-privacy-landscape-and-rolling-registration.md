# 3. The US state privacy landscape — rolling-registration programme and the common-baseline configuration

> There is no US federal privacy law. There are twenty-odd state statutes, three sector overlays that bite outside the comprehensive-privacy frame, and roughly one new statute per legislative session. The practitioner response is not twenty compliance programmes — it is one strictest-common-denominator posture, a rolling tracker, and a named owner who updates both on a quarterly cadence.

## Motivation

A Series-B SaaS corporation that solves California (see [chapter 02](./02-ccpa-cpra-compliance-programme.md)) frequently assumes the hard work of US privacy is behind it. It is not. By 2024 the map had already filled in with Virginia, Colorado, Connecticut, Utah, Iowa, Texas, Oregon, Delaware, Montana, Tennessee, and New Jersey; Maryland, Minnesota, Indiana, Kentucky, New Hampshire, Nebraska, and Rhode Island followed in the same wave. Each is a comprehensive-privacy statute with its own citation, its own effective date, its own consumer-rights catalogue, its own sensitive-data rule, and its own universal-opt-out posture. On top of that, Washington's My Health My Data Act, Illinois's BIPA, Illinois's GIPA, and the minors-privacy laws out of California, Utah, Louisiana, Arkansas, and Florida bite on specific data categories or user populations regardless of whether the comprehensive-privacy statute in that state applies.

The naive response — one compliance programme per state — scales nowhere. The right response is a **rolling-registration programme**: pick a strictest-common-denominator posture that discharges every known statute, collapse the per-state detail into a single controller-side configuration, and maintain one living tracker that watches for new statutes, effective-date phase-ins, and enforcement waves. The corporation treats California plus Colorado as the baseline anchors, inherits most of the other states for free, and spends its legal-ops cycles on the narrow deltas that genuinely require per-state engineering. This chapter walks through the map, the dividing lines, and the operating discipline that keeps the programme from fragmenting.

## Core concepts

### The comprehensive-state-privacy map — citation by citation

The comprehensive statutes, with their primary citations and the controller/processor structure they share:

- **California CCPA/CPRA** — Cal. Civ. Code § 1798.100 et seq. (treated in [chapter 02](./02-ccpa-cpra-compliance-programme.md)). The outlier on terminology ("business," "service provider," "contractor") and on the opt-out-plus-limit-use structure for sensitive-personal-information.
- **Virginia VCDPA** — Va. Code § 59.1-575 et seq. Opt-in consent for sensitive-data processing.
- **Colorado CPA** — Colo. Rev. Stat. § 6-1-1301 et seq. with 4 CCR 904-3 (Attorney General regulations). Opt-in consent for sensitive data plus a universal-opt-out-mechanism obligation under the AG rules.
- **Connecticut CTDPA** — Conn. Pub. Act 22-15, codified at Conn. Gen. Stat. § 42-515 et seq. Opt-in for sensitive data and a universal-opt-out obligation.
- **Utah UCPA** — Utah Code § 13-61-101 et seq. The leanest of the state statutes — opt-out posture for sensitive data, no universal-opt-out mandate, narrower consumer rights.
- **Iowa ICDPA** — Iowa Code chapter 715D. Lean, similar in posture to Utah.
- **Texas TDPSA** — Tex. Bus. & Com. Code § 541 et seq. Opt-in for sensitive data and a universal-opt-out obligation; low applicability threshold (processing personal data in Texas, with a small-business carve-out) which materially expands the covered-controller population.
- **Oregon OCPA** — Oregon Consumer Privacy Act, Oregon Revised Statutes chapter 646A (OCPA sections). Opt-in sensitive-data posture, universal-opt-out obligation, and an explicit right to a list of the specific third parties to whom personal data has been disclosed.
- **Delaware DPDPA** — Delaware Personal Data Privacy Act, 6 Del. C. ch. 12D.
- **Montana MCDPA** — Mont. Code Ann. § 30-14-2801 et seq.
- **Tennessee TIPA** — Tenn. Code Ann. § 47-18-3201 et seq. Notable for an affirmative-defence safe harbour for controllers who maintain a written privacy programme that reasonably conforms to NIST Privacy Framework.
- **New Jersey NJDPA** — N.J. Stat. § 56:8-166.4 et seq.
- **Maryland MODPA** — Md. Code, Com. Law § 14-4601 et seq. The strictest of the second-wave statutes on data-minimisation and on sensitive-data processing.
- **Minnesota Consumer Data Privacy Act** — Minn. Stat. ch. 325O.
- **Indiana Consumer Data Protection Act** — Ind. Code § 24-15.
- **Kentucky Consumer Data Protection Act** — Ky. Rev. Stat. § 367.3611 et seq.
- **New Hampshire SB 255** — N.H. Rev. Stat. ch. 507-H.
- **Nebraska Data Privacy Act** — Neb. Rev. Stat. § 87-1101 et seq.
- **Rhode Island Data Transparency and Privacy Protection Act** — R.I. Gen. Laws § 6-48.1 (data-broker) and the comprehensive-privacy provisions enacted in the 2024 session.

<!-- needs-research: confirm current effective date and any phase-in schedule before quoting as canonical for each of the states above; the landscape moves quarterly and several of the 2024-wave statutes have staggered small-business exemptions and processor-provision effective dates --> The corporation should not hard-code effective dates into customer-facing documents; the tracker (below) holds dates and is the source of truth.

All of these statutes share the Virginia template structurally: controller/processor terminology (not California's business/service-provider), a consumer-rights catalogue (access, deletion, correction, portability, opt-out of sale / targeted advertising / profiling), a data-protection-assessment requirement for high-risk processing (which lines up neatly with GDPR DPIAs — see [chapter 01](./01-gdpr-compliance-programme.md)), and controller-processor contract language that mirrors GDPR Article 28 in substance. The deltas across states are in sensitive-data posture, universal-opt-out obligation, the private-right-of-action question (almost universally absent — AG enforcement only), applicability thresholds, and cure-period mechanics.

Applicability thresholds matter. Most states follow some variant of Virginia's "controller that conducts business in the state and processes personal data of 100,000 consumers or 25,000 consumers if deriving more than 25% of revenue from sale of personal data." Texas's TDPSA breaks this pattern by extending to any controller that processes personal data in Texas with only a small-business carve-out — materially widening the covered-controller population. California's CCPA/CPRA uses revenue, consumer-count, and sale/share thresholds that work differently again. A controller that is just over threshold in one state and just under in another should not be making per-state applicability determinations at consumer-request time; the operating default is to treat every US consumer-rights request as in-scope and respond.

Cure periods are the other quiet delta. Several second-wave states (Utah, Iowa, Tennessee) grandfathered permanent cure-period rights — the AG must give the controller notice and a 30- or 60-day cure window before enforcement — while others (California under the CPPA, Texas, Oregon, Delaware) have either sunset their cure periods or granted the AG discretion to withhold them. The practical takeaway is that cure is a backstop, not a strategy; the baseline configuration should be compliant on day one of each statute's effective date, not on day thirty-one.

### The sensitive-data opt-in vs. opt-out dividing line

The single most load-bearing delta across the state map is how sensitive data is processed:

- **Opt-in consent for sensitive-data processing.** Virginia, Colorado, Connecticut, Texas, Oregon, Montana, Delaware, New Jersey, Tennessee, Minnesota, Maryland, New Hampshire, Indiana, Kentucky, Nebraska, and Rhode Island require affirmative opt-in consent before processing sensitive personal data. "Sensitive" tracks a familiar list — racial/ethnic origin, religious beliefs, mental or physical health diagnosis, sexual orientation, citizenship/immigration status, genetic or biometric data processed for the purpose of unique identification, precise geolocation, and personal data of a known child.
- **Opt-out / limit-use for sensitive data.** California and Utah run a different structure. California's CPRA creates the "sensitive personal information" category under Cal. Civ. Code § 1798.121 with a right to **limit use and disclosure** to disclosed business purposes — opt-out, not opt-in. Utah's UCPA likewise runs opt-out for sensitive data.
- **Iowa** sits closer to Utah than Virginia on sensitive-data treatment and should be read on its own terms.

The practical engineering consequence: a controller that treats all sensitive-data processing as opt-in by default satisfies the opt-in states and over-satisfies California and Utah (which are satisfied by opt-out but not harmed by opt-in). A controller that treats sensitive-data processing as opt-out breaches all of the opt-in states. The strictest-common-denominator posture is therefore to **collect opt-in consent for sensitive-data processing globally**, surface the California-specific limit-use link anyway (California requires the link regardless of whether opt-in has been obtained), and configure the data-inventory to flag which processing activities touch sensitive categories.

The terminology drift is worth internalising. California speaks in terms of **business / service-provider / contractor / third party**; every other state speaks in **controller / processor**. Draft internal documentation and customer-facing privacy notices once in controller/processor terms with a California annex mapping the terms through — rewriting everything twice is a legal-ops tax with no upside. The DPA language that flows down to sub-processors (see [mod-109 chapter 01](../mod-109-commercial-contracts-ip-and-legal-ops/01-customer-contract-suite-msa-sla-dpa-security-aup.md)) should likewise be drafted in controller/processor terms with a California service-provider / contractor addendum appended — one contract template, one review cycle, one redline surface across both regimes.

A second dividing line that practitioners frequently miss: **profiling-opt-out scope**. Colorado, Connecticut, Virginia, Oregon, Delaware, Montana, and others extend opt-out rights to "profiling in furtherance of decisions that produce legal or similarly significant effects" — which, read broadly, covers automated credit-adjacent, employment-adjacent, housing-adjacent, and insurance-adjacent decisioning. California's equivalent is being built out through CPPA automated-decision-making regulations. A controller that runs *any* ML-driven ranking that affects consumer-facing offers, pricing, or access must assume profiling-opt-out applies and must wire the opt-out into the ranking stack, not just the ad-tech stack.

### Universal-opt-out-mechanism obligations

A material cluster of states now requires the controller to honour a **universal opt-out mechanism** — a browser-level or device-level signal that declares the user's opt-out of sale / targeted advertising / profiling without the user having to find and click a link on the controller's site. The current scope (subject to <!-- needs-research: confirm which states' universal-opt-out mandates are currently in force vs. phased in --> ongoing phase-in schedules):

- **Colorado** — 4 CCR 904-3 Rule 5 recognises universal opt-outs and the Colorado AG maintains a published list of recognised mechanisms, which has included Global Privacy Control (GPC).
- **Connecticut** — Conn. Gen. Stat. § 42-518 requires honouring a universal opt-out signal.
- **Texas** — TDPSA requires recognition of a universal opt-out from a specified effective date.
- **Montana, New Jersey, Delaware, Oregon** — all carry universal-opt-out obligations in their comprehensive statutes.
- **California** — not a statutory requirement at the CPRA level, but the California Privacy Protection Agency regulations at 11 CCR § 7025 require businesses to process opt-out preference signals, which has effectively made GPC-handling table stakes in California as well.

The engineering spec that discharges all of these in one configuration:

1. **Detect the signal.** Parse the `Sec-GPC: 1` header on every request and honour the equivalent in any other recognised mechanism the Colorado AG lists.
2. **Record the signal per user.** Persist "GPC-asserted-opt-out" as a first-class attribute of the consumer record (keyed by cookie-ID, device-ID, or authenticated user-ID), not a per-session flag.
3. **Apply the signal in the absence of a logged-in session.** The user does not need to be authenticated for the opt-out to attach — the signal is the opt-out. The cookie-banner / CMP configuration must suppress sale, targeted advertising, and profiling cookies on any request that carries the signal, regardless of session state.
4. **Reconcile with authenticated preference centres.** If the same user later logs in and asserts a different preference, the controller needs a documented reconciliation rule (most practitioners treat the authenticated preference as governing for authenticated-session traffic and GPC as governing for anonymous traffic).
5. **Prove it on audit.** The consent-management platform (OneTrust, Osano, Transcend, Didomi, or an in-house equivalent) must produce a per-user audit record showing the signal was received, when, and that the downstream ad-tech and analytics SDKs were suppressed on that request.

Honouring GPC globally — regardless of the user's declared state — is the strictest-common-denominator posture and is cheaper than per-state geolocation-gated logic.

A common failure mode is the **tag-manager bypass**: the CMP correctly suppresses first-party analytics on a GPC-asserted request, but a Google Tag Manager or Segment container loads third-party ad-tech tags via a side channel that does not consult the consent state. The audit question to drive back to engineering is not "does the CMP respect GPC?" — the question is "on a request carrying `Sec-GPC: 1`, which network calls fire, and are any of them to vendors we have categorised as sale-or-sharing recipients?" The answer lives in a browser-network trace, not in the CMP configuration UI.

### Sector overlays that bite outside the comprehensive-privacy frame

The comprehensive statutes have broad exemptions — HIPAA-covered entities, GLBA-covered financial institutions, FCRA-covered activities, and employee / B2B data in several states. The sector overlays fill the gaps and frequently apply to controllers that thought they were out of scope:

- **Washington My Health My Data Act** — RCW 19.373 et seq. Covers "consumer health data" processed by entities that are not HIPAA-covered — wellness apps, mental-health apps, cycle-tracking apps, SDK vendors that ingest health-adjacent attributes from consumer apps.

  Requires opt-in consent for collection and a separate opt-in "authorisation" for sale, plus a prominent "Consumer Health Data Privacy Policy" distinct from the general privacy policy. Carries a private right of action under the Washington Consumer Protection Act (RCW 19.86), which is the material enforcement risk. A Nevada analogue (SB 370) and a Connecticut 2023 amendment extend similar consumer-health-data protections in those jurisdictions.

  The MHMDA definition of "consumer health data" is broad — it covers inferences about an individual's physical or mental health. That reach captures product categories that do not self-identify as health-tech: fitness trackers, meditation and sleep apps, nutrition apps, reproductive health apps, and any analytics or ad-tech SDK embedded in them. A controller should not conclude it is out of scope because "we are not a health app"; the question is whether any processing activity generates a health-adjacent inference.
- **Illinois Biometric Information Privacy Act (BIPA)** — 740 ILCS 14. Requires written informed consent before collecting "biometric identifiers" (fingerprint, voiceprint, retina/iris scan, face geometry) or "biometric information" derived from them, plus a written retention schedule and prohibition on sale. **Private right of action with statutory damages** — $1,000 per negligent violation, $5,000 per intentional or reckless violation. The private right of action is the mechanism behind the material BIPA class-action exposure that has driven settlements across voiceprint, face-geometry, and fingerprint deployments.

  A controller that uses face-geometry for login, voiceprint for authentication, or fingerprint for time-clocks in Illinois needs BIPA-grade written consent, retention policy, and vendor language — regardless of whether the comprehensive-privacy statute applies. BIPA's definition of "biometric identifier" excludes photographs on their face but the Illinois Supreme Court has read "biometric information" to cover templates derived from photographs, so an "it's just a photo" defence will not hold where a face-geometry template is extracted. The practical rule: if any ML model extracts a biometric template, BIPA applies; the comprehensive-privacy statute's biometric opt-in is in addition to, not instead of, BIPA.
- **Nevada SB 370** — data-broker opt-out registry and consumer opt-out of sale of covered information under Nev. Rev. Stat. § 603A. California (Cal. Civ. Code § 1798.99.80 et seq. — DELETE Act) and Vermont (9 V.S.A. § 2446 et seq.) run their own data-broker registration regimes; a controller that qualifies as a data broker in any of these states must register separately from the comprehensive-privacy compliance posture.
- **Illinois Genetic Information Privacy Act (GIPA)** — 410 ILCS 513. Written informed consent for disclosure of genetic testing information, with a private right of action similar in structure to BIPA. Genetic data is also sensitive-personal-data under every comprehensive-privacy statute, but the GIPA written-consent obligation is separate and is the specific hook for Illinois class-action exposure in the direct-to-consumer genetic-testing sector.
- **Minors-privacy laws.** California's Age-Appropriate Design Code (AB 2273, codified at Cal. Civ. Code § 1798.99.28 et seq.) and SB 976 (Protecting Our Kids from Social Media Addiction Act) impose design-level obligations on online products "likely to be accessed by a child" — DPIAs, high-privacy defaults, restrictions on dark patterns. <!-- needs-research: confirm current injunction / appellate posture of the AADC (NetChoice v. Bonta) and SB 976 before relying on specific provisions as currently operative --> Utah's Minor Protection in Social Media Act, Louisiana Act 440, Arkansas SB 396 (Social Media Safety Act), and Florida HB 3 impose age-verification, parental-consent, and feature-restriction obligations on social media platforms and other covered products, each with its own effective date and litigation posture.

  A corporation whose product is plausibly "likely to be accessed by a child" or qualifies as a covered social media platform must track this cluster separately from the comprehensive-privacy map. The federal Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA, 15 U.S.C. § 6501 et seq.) and the FTC's COPPA Rule at 16 C.F.R. Part 312 continue to apply to online services directed to children under 13 and run in parallel with the state minors-privacy regimes, not instead of them.

The DPA (see [mod-109 chapter 01](../mod-109-commercial-contracts-ip-and-legal-ops/01-customer-contract-suite-msa-sla-dpa-security-aup.md)) needs sector-overlay language where relevant — WMHMDA processor terms, BIPA-flow-down, minors-data flags — because the comprehensive-state addenda do not cover these overlays.

The failure mode that drives most sector-overlay litigation is **relying on the comprehensive-privacy exemption and forgetting the overlay**. A controller that is HIPAA-regulated for its covered-entity activities may still have a non-HIPAA wellness-app line of business that is squarely inside WMHMDA; a controller that uses face-geometry only for a narrow authentication flow may still trip BIPA because the written-consent-plus-retention-schedule obligation applies to the collection event, not to the business purpose. The sector overlays are read strictly, and the private-right-of-action states (Illinois, Washington) carry damages exposure that materially dwarfs AG-enforcement states.

### The rolling-registration-plus-common-baseline operating approach

The architectural decision that unlocks everything else is to **pick the strictest-common-denominator posture as the single controller-side configuration** and treat per-state deltas as thin disclosure-and-response-time overlays rather than separate programmes. Concretely:

1. **One privacy-policy template with state-specific disclosure annexes.** The body of the notice describes the processing, purposes, categories, retention, rights, and contact per the Virginia-template structure. State-specific annexes provide the California CCPA/CPRA disclosures (sources, purposes, categories of third parties, limit-use link, Do-Not-Sell/Share link, retention per category), the Colorado universal-opt-out disclosure, the Washington Consumer Health Data Privacy Policy, the BIPA written-policy disclosure, and the minors-privacy disclosures. One document, maintained in one place, rendered by one template system.
2. **One consumer-rights workflow with state-specific response-time overlays.** The intake form accepts verifiable requests from any US consumer; the backend classifies the consumer's state; the response-time clock is set to the shortest applicable statutory response (typically 45 days with a 45-day extension, but confirm per state at request time); the response is drafted against a single rights catalogue (access, deletion, correction, portability, opt-out of sale / targeted advertising / profiling, limit-use for California sensitive data) and trimmed to the rights the consumer's state actually grants. The workflow logs every request and response for the AG's records demand.
3. **One data inventory tagged by applicable statute.** Every processing activity in the RoPA (see [chapter 01](./01-gdpr-compliance-programme.md)) carries a tag set indicating which US state statutes apply, whether sensitive categories are involved, whether consumer-health-data categories are involved, whether biometric identifiers are involved, and whether the user population includes known minors. The tag set drives the policy annex, the consent flow, and the response-time overlay without requiring a per-state inventory.
4. **Strictest-common-denominator defaults.** Opt-in consent for sensitive-data processing globally (satisfies Virginia et al., over-satisfies California/Utah). GPC honoured globally for sale, targeted advertising, and profiling (satisfies the universal-opt-out states and California regulatory expectations). DPIA-equivalent data-protection assessments for high-risk processing globally (satisfies Virginia et al. and aligns with GDPR). Controller-processor contract language built on the Virginia-template clauses with a California service-provider addendum (see [mod-109 chapter 01](../mod-109-commercial-contracts-ip-and-legal-ops/01-customer-contract-suite-msa-sla-dpa-security-aup.md)).
5. **Treat new statutes as deltas against the baseline, not greenfield programmes.** When a new state enacts a comprehensive statute, the question the tracker asks is not "do we need a new programme?" but "does this statute introduce any obligation the baseline does not already discharge?" Most of the 2024 wave answered that question with "no" — the deltas were applicability thresholds, cure-period mechanics, and small disclosure nuances.

The anti-pattern this avoids is **per-state engineering spawn** — separate consent flows, separate privacy-policy versions, separate rights intake per state. That architecture breaks on the first new statute and does not scale across a Series-B's engineering bandwidth.

A second anti-pattern worth naming is **"we'll fix it when enforcement starts."** The comprehensive-privacy statutes are AG-enforced and the first wave of AG activity tends to target obvious misses — missing privacy-policy disclosures, missing universal-opt-out handling, missing data-protection assessments, missing processor contracts. California's Attorney General and the California Privacy Protection Agency have both issued enforcement advisories, Colorado's AG runs active sweeps, and Texas's AG has made consumer-privacy enforcement a stated priority. The baseline configuration is cheap to maintain; the enforcement-letter response is not.

### The watch-list discipline

The baseline is only durable if it is maintained. The watch-list discipline:

- **One rolling tracker.** A living spreadsheet or a OneTrust / Osano / Securiti regulatory-tracker module listing every US state comprehensive statute and every sector overlay, with columns for: citation, effective date, applicability thresholds, consumer rights, sensitive-data posture, universal-opt-out obligation, cure period, AG regulations status, notable enforcement actions, and the baseline-delta assessment (does our current configuration already discharge this?).
- **Quarterly legal-ops review cadence.** The privacy counsel or legal-ops lead walks the tracker every quarter — new statutes enacted since last review, new effective dates passed, new AG regulations, new enforcement actions, new litigation on injunction-posture statutes (AADC, SB 976, state social media laws). The output is a short memo for the board / audit committee.
- **A named owner.** Privacy counsel or the legal-ops lead carries the tracker on the operating RACI. Shared ownership becomes no ownership; the named owner is accountable for the quarterly review happening and for the baseline being updated when deltas are found.
- **Named change-triggers that force an off-cycle update.** (i) A new comprehensive-privacy statute is signed into law. (ii) A state AG issues new regulations or guidance (notably Colorado AG under 4 CCR 904-3, California CPPA under the CCPA regulations, Texas AG, Oregon AG). (iii) A CPPA enforcement action lands against a comparator controller. (iv) A state-AG enforcement wave against a specific practice (data-broker registration failures, Shine-the-Light requests, sensitive-data opt-in failures). (v) A BIPA, WMHMDA, or GIPA class-action settlement that re-prices the practice at issue. (vi) An injunction decision moving an AADC-style or minors-social-media statute from pre-enforcement limbo to operative.
- **Documentation the AG expects to see on records demand.** Several state AGs now issue CID-style records demands that ask for the privacy notice, the consumer-rights intake log, the data-protection assessments, the universal-opt-out configuration evidence, and the controller-processor contracts with sub-processors. The tracker and the baseline configuration both feed a single evidence package that the privacy office can deliver without re-engineering.
- **Separation of concerns against sibling disciplines.** The baseline configuration is a privacy-and-legal artefact. The security-engineering controls that support it (encryption, access control, logging, incident response) belong to the security-engineering learning path, not to this chapter. The internal AI acceptable-use policy that governs employee use of consumer personal data in prompts and model training belongs to [mod-108](../mod-108-ai-governance-model-risk-and-responsible-ai/). The international privacy regimes beyond GDPR — Brazilian LGPD, Canadian PIPEDA / Law 25, Indian DPDP, Chinese PIPL, Australian Privacy Act reform — belong to [mod-113](../mod-113-international-privacy-and-cross-border-data/). This chapter is strictly the US state map.

The watch-list discipline is the connective tissue between the baseline configuration and the board / audit committee compliance roll-up in [chapter 11 of mod-111](../mod-111-compliance-reporting-audit-and-internal-controls/) (quarterly privacy metrics are one of the standing items on the audit committee or AI-risk sub-committee's agenda). This chapter owns the state-map and the tracker; mod-111 owns how the tracker's output is reported upward.

## Concrete example: Inverloch Analytics' strictest-common-denominator posture

Inverloch Analytics, a Series-B US-headquartered SaaS corporation selling a product analytics platform to B2B customers, has customers in 38 US states and a growing SMB segment that pushes it over the applicability thresholds of most comprehensive-privacy statutes. Its data-inventory flags two processing activities that touch sensitive categories (an optional precise-geolocation feature for in-app session replay; an optional face-blur ML feature that briefly processes face geometry before discarding it) and no HIPAA, GLBA, or FCRA activity. It is not a social media platform and its product is not directed at minors, but the terms of use set a floor age of 16.

**Baseline configuration.**

- One privacy-policy template rendered on inverloch.example/privacy, with annexes for California, Colorado, Washington (because the face-blur feature plausibly implicates WMHMDA's consumer-health-data definition in edge cases), and Illinois (because the face-blur feature implicates BIPA unless a clean exclusion applies). The template is versioned in the CMS, every published version is retained, and each version carries a diff summary for the audit trail.
- Opt-in consent collected globally for the precise-geolocation feature and for the face-blur ML feature, with a separate written BIPA-grade consent for Illinois users on face-blur and a documented retention schedule attached.
- GPC honoured globally. The consent-management platform suppresses targeted-advertising and analytics cookies on any request carrying `Sec-GPC: 1`, and the preference is persisted to the authenticated user record on login.
- One consumer-rights intake at privacy@inverloch.example and a webform; backend classifies state at request time, sets the response clock to the shortest applicable window, and responds against a single rights catalogue trimmed per state.
- One data-protection-assessment template (modelled on GDPR DPIAs under [chapter 01](./01-gdpr-compliance-programme.md)) covers Virginia-template DPA requirements, California cybersecurity-audit / risk-assessment requirements, and the Colorado AG DPA requirements. New product features that touch sensitive data, minors, automated decisioning affecting consumers, or new sub-processors route through the template before launch.
- Controller-processor DPA language built on Virginia-template clauses with a California service-provider addendum, incorporated by reference into the mod-109 DPA (see [mod-109 chapter 01](../mod-109-commercial-contracts-ip-and-legal-ops/01-customer-contract-suite-msa-sla-dpa-security-aup.md)).
- A data-inventory in Securiti tagged by processing activity, sensitive-category flag, minors flag, biometric flag, and consumer-health-data flag. Every tag is wired to drive downstream behaviour — the consent flow, the policy annex, the DPA module included in the customer contract, and the response-time overlay for the consumer-rights workflow.

**Rolling tracker.** A single spreadsheet in the legal-ops Notion. 21 rows (one per statute in scope, including the sector overlays). Columns: citation, effective date (with <!-- needs-research --> markers where staged), applicability threshold, consumer rights, sensitive-data posture, universal-opt-out, cure period, AG regs status, enforcement notes, baseline-delta. The data-protection counsel (one FTE) owns the tracker and walks it every quarter on the first Monday of the quarter; a two-page memo goes to the audit committee as part of the standing quarterly compliance roll-up in [mod-111](../mod-111-compliance-reporting-audit-and-internal-controls/).

**Off-cycle update in practice.** In Q3, a new state enacts a comprehensive-privacy statute. The data-protection counsel adds a row to the tracker within two weeks of signing. The baseline-delta assessment concludes that the new statute is Virginia-template with a universal-opt-out obligation and opt-in sensitive-data posture — fully discharged by the baseline — and the only required change is to add the state to the California/Colorado-style disclosure annex. Engineering ships a copy change; no new consent flow is built; no new data-inventory tags are introduced. The memo to the audit committee closes the row as "baseline sufficient, cosmetic disclosure update shipped." This is what the rolling-registration-plus-common-baseline approach delivers and why it is worth the upfront investment in a strictest-common-denominator posture.

**Enforcement-letter rehearsal.** The data-protection counsel also runs an annual tabletop against a simulated AG records demand. The exercise walks through delivering, within a stated response window, the privacy notice history (with version diffs), the consumer-rights intake log (with response-time measurements per state), the data-protection-assessment register, the universal-opt-out configuration evidence (a network trace showing suppression on `Sec-GPC: 1`), the sub-processor list with executed contracts, and the DPA repository. If the exercise finds a gap — the response-time measurements are not actually logged, or the network trace cannot be produced on demand — the gap is logged as a finding and routed to engineering with an SLA. The tabletop is cheap insurance; a real AG letter is not.

**Board-level visibility.** The audit committee sees the privacy programme as a single line on the quarterly compliance dashboard (see [mod-111](../mod-111-compliance-reporting-audit-and-internal-controls/)): "US state privacy — baseline sufficient across N statutes, M in flight, K enforcement developments noted this quarter, next quarterly review scheduled." That one-line summary is backed by the tracker and the annual tabletop. If the summary flips to "baseline delta identified — remediation in flight," the audit committee gets the full memo and a remediation ETA. This is the governance payoff of the rolling-registration approach — one accountable owner, one living artefact, one durable reporting line.

### Interaction with mod-111 reporting and with the California baseline

The output of the tracker and the baseline configuration feeds two upstream destinations. The first is the **compliance reporting roll-up to the audit committee or AI-risk sub-committee**, handled in [mod-111](../mod-111-compliance-reporting-audit-and-internal-controls/). The committee does not want a per-state status report; the committee wants one line — "US state privacy baseline sufficient, N statutes tracked, any deltas identified" — backed by the memo and the tracker on request. The chapter here owns producing the tracker; mod-111 owns the reporting line.

The second upstream destination is the **California baseline programme** in [chapter 02](./02-ccpa-cpra-compliance-programme.md). California is the baseline anchor because (i) the CPRA / CPPA regulations are the most prescriptive of the US state regimes on operational mechanics (opt-out preference signals, cybersecurity audits, risk assessments, automated-decisionmaking regulations), and (ii) a controller that is CPRA-compliant and Colorado-CPA-compliant has already built most of the operational artefacts the rest of the map expects to see. The rolling-registration programme consumes the California baseline and extends it; it does not duplicate it.

## Summary

- There is no US federal privacy law. There are twenty-odd state comprehensive-privacy statutes (California, Virginia, Colorado, Connecticut, Utah, Iowa, Texas, Oregon, Delaware, Montana, Tennessee, New Jersey, Maryland, Minnesota, Indiana, Kentucky, New Hampshire, Nebraska, Rhode Island, and successors) plus a sector-overlay layer (Washington My Health My Data Act, Illinois BIPA, Illinois GIPA, Nevada SB 370 data-broker opt-out) plus a minors-privacy cluster (California AADC and SB 976, Utah, Louisiana Act 440, Arkansas SB 396, Florida HB 3).
- The comprehensive statutes are structurally a Virginia-template family — controller/processor terminology, access/deletion/correction/portability/opt-out rights, data-protection assessments for high-risk processing, controller-processor contract requirements — with California as the terminology outlier (business / service-provider / contractor) and the opt-out-plus-limit-use outlier on sensitive data.
- The load-bearing delta is sensitive-data posture — opt-in in Virginia, Colorado, Connecticut, Texas, Oregon, Montana, Delaware, New Jersey, Tennessee, Minnesota, Maryland, New Hampshire, Indiana, Kentucky, Nebraska, Rhode Island; opt-out / limit-use in California and Utah. The strictest-common-denominator posture is opt-in globally.
- Universal-opt-out mechanisms (Global Privacy Control chief among them) must be honoured in Colorado, Connecticut, Texas, Montana, New Jersey, Delaware, Oregon, and effectively California under CPPA regulations. Honouring GPC globally is cheaper than geolocation-gated logic and discharges all of them.
- Sector overlays bite outside the comprehensive-privacy frame. Washington MHMDA (RCW 19.373) covers non-HIPAA consumer health data with a private right of action under the state Consumer Protection Act. Illinois BIPA (740 ILCS 14) and GIPA (410 ILCS 513) carry private rights of action with statutory damages that have driven the material class-action exposure.
- Minors-privacy laws apply regardless of the comprehensive-privacy frame and carry live injunction posture that must be tracked separately; <!-- needs-research --> flag the current status before relying on any specific provision as operative.
- The operating approach is rolling-registration-plus-common-baseline — one privacy-policy template with state-specific disclosure annexes, one consumer-rights workflow with state-specific response-time overlays, one data inventory tagged by applicable statute, and strictest-common-denominator defaults (opt-in sensitive, GPC global, DPAs for high-risk processing).
- The watch-list discipline is one tracker, a quarterly cadence, a named owner (privacy counsel / legal-ops lead), and documented change-triggers (new statute, new AG regulations, enforcement waves, BIPA/WMHMDA/GIPA settlements, injunction decisions on minors-privacy laws).
- Effective dates and enforcement developments move quarterly — never hard-code dates into customer-facing documents; keep the tracker as the source of truth and flag <!-- needs-research: ... --> where specific dates are in flux.
- California (see [chapter 02](./02-ccpa-cpra-compliance-programme.md)) and Colorado (via the AG rules at 4 CCR 904-3) are the baseline anchors. If those two are configured correctly, the rest of the US map falls out as a disclosure-and-tagging exercise rather than a per-state programme — and the roll-up to the audit committee ([mod-111](../mod-111-compliance-reporting-audit-and-internal-controls/)) is a single line item instead of twenty.
- Boundary note — security-engineering controls sit in the security learning path, internal AI acceptable-use in [mod-108](../mod-108-ai-governance-model-risk-and-responsible-ai/), DPA / contract-vehicle anatomy in [mod-109](../mod-109-commercial-contracts-ip-and-legal-ops/), and international privacy regimes beyond GDPR in [mod-113](../mod-113-international-privacy-and-cross-border-data/). This chapter owns the US state map; the operating baseline it produces feeds every one of those sibling lanes.
