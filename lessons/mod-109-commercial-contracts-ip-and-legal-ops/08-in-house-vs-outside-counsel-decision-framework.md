# 8. The in-house vs. outside-counsel decision framework

> The legal-function budget scales with revenue, but its composition — outside-counsel spend vs. in-house salary vs. tooling — changes at every stage. The question is not whether the corporation will hire in-house lawyers, but when, in which specialties, and how it will manage the outside-counsel relationships that persist forever alongside.

## Motivation

Every founding team's legal function begins the same way. A partner at a startup-focused Big Law firm — Cooley, Wilson Sonsini Goodrich & Rosati (WSGR), Orrick, Fenwick & West, Latham & Watkins, Gunderson Dettmer, Goodwin Procter, or Perkins Coie — sits opposite the founders at incorporation, produces the certificate of incorporation, the initial-board resolutions, the stockholder agreements, the equity plan, and the founder IP-assignment documents (cross-reference [mod-102](../mod-102-founding-team-legal-architecture/)). That partner is functionally the corporation's outsourced general counsel. Every substantive legal question — the commercial contract stack described in [chapter 01](./01-customer-contract-suite-msa-sla-dpa-security-aup.md), the employment agreements in [mod-103](../mod-103-employment-law-and-contract-design/), the equity issuances in [mod-102](../mod-102-founding-team-legal-architecture/), the board minutes in [mod-111](../mod-111-corporate-governance-board-operations-and-officer-duties/), the first customer redlines — routes through the firm.

That arrangement scales further than founders usually expect. A well-run outsourced-GC engagement can carry a corporation through Series-A and sometimes Series-B before the run-rate outside-counsel spend consistently exceeds the fully-loaded cost of an in-house hire. But eventually every corporation crosses that threshold. When it does, the founders face a series of decisions that most have never made before: what to hire in-house first, what to keep outside forever, how to negotiate rate cards and engagement letters with the outside firms, how to build a panel, how to manage the shift in decision-making authority from the outside firm's senior partner to the corporation's newly-hired Head of Legal.

The failure modes here are as damaging as any elsewhere in the operating model. Corporations that keep everything outside too long burn cash on hourly rates for work an in-house counsel could do at a fraction of the cost, and they lose institutional memory to firms that rotate associates on and off matters. Corporations that hire in-house too early bring on a senior lawyer who has too little commercial-contract volume to justify the compensation and who is not equipped to handle the bursty specialist matters (IP prosecution, litigation, tax) the corporation still needs. Corporations that hire the wrong specialty fit — a generalist GC in a healthtech or defense-tech vertical — under-serve the compliance work that the vertical actually requires. Corporations that grow the outside-counsel panel without building the management discipline (rate cards, matter management, e-billing) let the spend spiral without accountability.

This chapter is the framework for making the sequence of decisions cleanly. It walks the outsourced-GC model at pre-seed / seed / early Series-A; the emerging fractional-GC model at ~$5–25M ARR; the first in-house Head of Legal hire at Series-A / B; the buy-vs-build calculus by specialty (commercial, corporate, employment, IP prosecution, litigation, privacy, tax); the outside-counsel management discipline (rate cards, matter management, panel management, billing review) that must be built alongside; the escalation framework for deciding when a matter goes outside even after the in-house function exists; and the graduation to a full legal team at Series-C / pre-IPO. It closes with a concrete example of a corporation's legal-function evolution across five stages.

## The outsourced-GC model at pre-seed / seed / early Series-A

The dominant pattern at the earliest stages is retention of a startup-focused Big Law firm as the corporation's GC of record. The firms in this niche — Cooley, WSGR, Orrick, Fenwick & West, Latham & Watkins, Gunderson Dettmer, Goodwin Procter, Perkins Coie — have practices structured specifically around venture-backed startups. Their partners handle multiple concurrent formation-through-exit engagements; their associates rotate through matters at each stage; their documentation libraries contain the standard-form NVCA-derived venture-financing documents, equity-plan templates, employment-agreement templates, and commercial-contract starting-point templates that the ecosystem uses. Their rate structures, while high on an hourly basis, are calibrated to the fact that a Series-B fundraise may generate low-six-figures of billing over a compressed window and a Series-A closing may generate similar.

Several of these firms — Cooley, WSGR, Gunderson Dettmer, Fenwick historically — offer a deferred-fee arrangement for pre-fundraise startups. The mechanic varies by firm and by engagement, but the pattern is that fees for formation, initial-financing preparation, and certain corporate-housekeeping matters are either capped, deferred until closing, or applied as a credit toward the corporation's future rounds. <!-- needs-research --> The economic effect is that a well-formed pre-seed corporation can obtain formation and pre-financing legal support at low cash outlay in the expectation that the firm will be retained on the priced round and can bill against the deferred credit. Founders should read the engagement letter carefully — the deferral is usually contingent on the firm being retained on the round, and if the corporation switches firms, the deferred fees typically become immediately payable.

The trade-off in the outsourced-GC model is that the corporation is paying Big Law hourly rates for what is functionally general-counsel work. Partner rates at these firms are in the range of $900 to $1,600+ per hour, with senior associates in the $700 to $1,100 range, mid-level associates $500 to $800, junior associates $400 to $600, and paralegals $250 to $450 — but specific rates for a named firm, named partner, and named engagement should never be quoted from memory. <!-- needs-research --> Every hour of general commercial-redline work at a $1,200/hour partner rate costs the corporation more than an entire day of an in-house counsel's time at a fully-loaded cost basis. That mismatch is tolerable at pre-seed volumes; it becomes intolerable once commercial-contract volume rises past a few deals per month.

Practical selection criteria when choosing the outsourced-GC firm at formation:

- **The firm's documentation base.** Investors have preferences. If the corporation's lead investor consistently uses Cooley or WSGR documentation on their board seats, using the same firm's documentation reduces friction on the priced round. Ask the lead investor which firms they prefer and why.
- **The day-to-day partner.** Big Law relationships are individual, not institutional. The corporation is not retaining "Cooley" — it is retaining a specific partner who will personally handle its work and who will delegate to specific associates. Interview the partner in person before signing an engagement letter. Ask how many concurrent startup engagements the partner carries, who the primary day-to-day contact will be, and how responsiveness works when the partner is in trial or in an M&A closing.
- **The firm's startup / venture practice depth.** The startup-focused firms named above have practices tuned to venture-backed startups. Corporate-first Big Law firms without a distinct startup practice may not have the templated infrastructure to support formation and financing at reasonable cost. Do not retain a general-corporate firm for formation if a startup-focused alternative is available.
- **Sector fit.** The corporation's vertical matters. A healthtech corporation should retain a firm with a serious healthcare regulatory practice (HIPAA, FDA, state-DOH); a fintech corporation should retain a firm with a bank-regulatory practice (GLBA, state money-transmitter, OCC / FDIC / NYDFS); a defense-tech corporation should retain a firm with an ITAR / EAR / DFARS practice. General-startup firms without vertical depth will refer the specialty work out, which multiplies the coordination burden.
- **The firm's post-fundraise plan.** Some firms discount pre-fundraise work aggressively and expect to make up the margin on the fundraise itself. Others price consistently across stages. Understand which model the firm operates under before committing, because the economics change materially post-Series-A.

## The fractional-GC model

An intermediate arrangement has emerged over the last five to seven years for corporations at roughly $5–25M ARR: the fractional GC. A fractional GC is a senior lawyer (typically an ex-in-house GC or a senior Big Law associate) who serves as GC-of-record for the corporation on a monthly-retainer basis, covering somewhere between 15 and 40 hours per month of general-counsel-level attention. Monthly retainers run in the range of $8,000 to $25,000 per month with wide variance by seniority, sector, and geography. <!-- needs-research -->

The fractional-GC model addresses a specific gap in the standard progression. A corporation at $5–25M ARR is generating enough commercial contract volume, enough employment-law matter volume, enough investor-relations work, and enough vendor-management activity that a Big Law relationship alone is no longer economic (rate mismatch on high-volume work) but is not yet generating enough total legal load to justify a full-time in-house hire (typically fully-loaded cost of $300–500k at Series-A / B seniority — see [mod-106](../mod-106-compensation-architecture-and-total-rewards/) for the compensation architecture that produces that number). A fractional GC bridges the gap: cheaper than an equivalent volume of Big Law hours, more responsive than an outside firm on high-touch matters, and available at day-to-day pace for the commercial and operational work that Big Law is not well-suited to at cost.

Signals that a fractional GC is the right next step:

- Commercial-contract volume has grown to the point where the outside-firm redline turnaround (see [chapter 02](./02-contract-playbook-and-fallback-position-matrix.md)) is slowing sales cycles noticeably.
- The corporation is having recurring board-level legal conversations — new equity issuances, option-plan refreshes, secondary transactions, side-letter management — that require ongoing engagement rather than transactional touchpoints.
- Employment-law matter volume has risen to the point where the founders no longer feel comfortable making the terminate-or-not-terminate call themselves (cross-reference [mod-107](../mod-107-performance-promotion-and-offboarding/)).
- The Big Law monthly bill is consistently in the $15–40k range, and the corporation is a year or more away from being able to justify a full-time senior-counsel hire.

The fractional GC does not replace the Big Law firm. Corporate financings, IP prosecution, litigation, and specialty tax work continue to route to specialist firms. The fractional GC's role is the day-to-day general-counsel work — commercial redlines within the playbook ([chapter 02](./02-contract-playbook-and-fallback-position-matrix.md)), employment questions ([mod-103](../mod-103-employment-law-and-contract-design/), [mod-107](../mod-107-performance-promotion-and-offboarding/)), vendor and reseller contracts, board-meeting materials in coordination with corporate counsel ([mod-111](../mod-111-corporate-governance-board-operations-and-officer-duties/)), and outside-counsel management on behalf of the corporation.

Two operational cautions with the fractional model:

- **Availability variance.** Fractional GCs typically carry multiple client engagements simultaneously. The corporation is not the fractional GC's only or highest-priority client, and response times can slip when another client has a crisis. Set the availability expectations in the engagement letter and monitor them.
- **Conflicts and confidentiality.** A fractional GC with multiple concurrent clients in the same vertical creates a competitive-intelligence risk. The engagement letter should include an explicit conflict-check obligation and a confidentiality framework. Prefer fractional GCs who agree to hold verticals exclusive during the engagement.

## The first in-house legal hire

The transition to a full-time in-house lawyer typically happens at Series-A or Series-B, at roughly $10–30M ARR, 100–200 employees, and monthly outside-counsel spend that has consistently exceeded the fully-loaded cost of a hire for two or three quarters. The title varies — Head of Legal, Senior Corporate Counsel, VP Legal, sometimes General Counsel from day one, sometimes Head of Legal Operations combined — and the choice of title matters because it shapes both the reporting relationship and the market for talent.

### Timing signal

The clean quantitative signal is the run-rate comparison. Track the trailing twelve months of outside-counsel spend, excluding one-time transactional events (a fundraise closing, an acquisition, a piece of litigation). If the run-rate steady-state spend consistently exceeds the fully-loaded cost of an in-house hire — base salary plus benefits plus equity plus overhead, typically 1.3–1.4x base for a Head of Legal — the arithmetic favours the hire. The qualitative overlay is that at least half of the outside spend should be work an in-house counsel could realistically handle (commercial redlines, employment questions, vendor contracts, corporate housekeeping). If most of the outside spend is on transactional or specialty work an in-house counsel will still send out, the hire will not displace as much spend as the arithmetic suggests.

Additional qualitative signals:

- The founders are spending materially more than one day per week on legal matters (redline reviews, contract approvals, employment-law questions, board minutes).
- The Big Law relationship is showing strain — turnaround times slipping, associates rotating in without context, the day-to-day partner unavailable during critical windows.
- The corporation is entering a stage transition (Series-B to Series-C, first international market, first regulated-industry customer) that will materially increase legal load.
- Sales is complaining publicly about legal cycle time (cross-reference [chapter 02](./02-contract-playbook-and-fallback-position-matrix.md)).

### Profile of the first hire

The typical first in-house legal hire is a generalist commercial-corporate hybrid. The two dominant pipelines:

- **The senior Big Law associate transitioning in-house.** A 5–10 year associate at a startup-focused firm who has spent significant time on the corporation's or a similar corporation's matters and who is looking for the in-house transition. This candidate knows the corporation's paper, knows the venture-financing playbook, and has trained in a rigorous documentation-and-drafting environment. The gap in this profile is commercial-negotiation experience — Big Law associates typically draft and negotiate corporate transactions, not high-volume commercial contracts.
- **The second-in-house-move Head of Legal.** A lawyer who has already made the first in-house move at a prior similar-stage corporation and is now moving to a second (usually with an incremental title bump — from Senior Corporate Counsel at Corporation A to Head of Legal at Corporation B). This candidate knows what a first-legal-hire scope actually looks like, has already built a playbook and a vendor stack once, and knows the operational realities of scaling a legal function.

Both profiles are legitimate; the choice depends on the corporation's specific gaps. If the outstanding legal load is heavily commercial (customer contracts, vendor contracts, sales-cycle support), the second-in-house-move profile usually wins. If the outstanding load is heavily corporate (financings, secondary transactions, cap-table management, board work), the Big Law senior-associate profile can be preferable.

**Vertical specialisation matters.** A healthtech corporation should hire a Head of Legal with HIPAA depth and, ideally, prior in-house healthtech experience — the counsel who has never negotiated a business-associate agreement or a hospital-system MSA will spend six months learning the vertical while sales is waiting. A fintech corporation needs GLBA depth, state-money-transmitter licensing familiarity, NYDFS Part 500 exposure, and ideally prior bank-regulatory experience. A defense-tech or dual-use corporation needs ITAR / EAR / DFARS familiarity, CMMC awareness, and comfort with government-contracting flowdowns. A privacy-heavy consumer corporation needs GDPR / CCPA / state-consumer-privacy experience (cross-reference [mod-110](../mod-110-privacy-data-governance-and-sector-compliance/)).

Do not compromise on vertical fit for the first hire. The generalist Head of Legal who "will learn the vertical on the job" is a common pattern and a common failure — the vertical work is often the highest-stakes work in the portfolio, and the ramp on regulatory expertise takes longer than the corporation can afford.

### Compensation

Compensation for a Series-A / B Head of Legal in the SF / NYC market typically runs base $200–350k plus equity (a typical mid-single-digit-basis-point-to-low-double-digit-basis-point grant depending on stage and profile) plus performance bonus (10–25% of base). <!-- needs-research --> Cross-reference [mod-106](../mod-106-compensation-architecture-and-total-rewards/) for the comp architecture — banding, benchmark sources, offer construction, geographic differentials — that governs how the specific offer is constructed. Do not benchmark against public-company Deputy GC compensation; do not benchmark against Big Law senior-associate compensation. The correct comparators are Head of Legal at similar-stage venture-backed corporations, drawn from a comp survey (Radford, Option Impact, Advanced-HR) that includes venture-backed private companies.

Equity is often the deciding factor. A candidate leaving a Big Law senior-associate role is stepping down in cash compensation and is being compensated in equity for the risk. Structure the equity grant with the standard four-year vest, one-year cliff (see [mod-102](../mod-102-founding-team-legal-architecture/) for the vesting mechanics), single-trigger acceleration on termination-without-cause after a change of control, and standard early-exercise / 83(b) mechanics.

### Day-one scope

The first in-house legal hire owns, on day one:

- **The commercial-contract suite** — the MSA, SLA, DPA, security exhibit, and AUP ([chapter 01](./01-customer-contract-suite-msa-sla-dpa-security-aup.md)) and the playbook ([chapter 02](./02-contract-playbook-and-fallback-position-matrix.md)).
- **The vendor-contract stack** — inbound vendor MSAs, DPAs, and SaaS agreements the corporation signs as a buyer, including AI-vendor terms (cross-reference [chapter 06](./06-ai-vendor-contracts-and-ai-dpa-pattern.md)).
- **The CLM implementation** ([chapter 07](./07-clm-stack-and-legal-ops-graduation.md)) — selecting, procuring, and rolling out the contract-lifecycle-management platform, integrating it with the corporation's Salesforce and finance stack, and building the intake / triage / obligation-tracking workflows.
- **Primary IP-strategy oversight** — coordination with outside IP prosecution counsel on the patent portfolio, trademark portfolio, and open-source-licensing hygiene. The in-house counsel is not filing prosecutions; the in-house counsel is managing the outside firm doing the prosecutions.
- **Corporate-secretary duties in coordination with outside counsel** — board meeting materials, minute-book maintenance, state-filing management, D&O-insurance coordination (cross-reference [mod-111](../mod-111-corporate-governance-board-operations-and-officer-duties/) and [mod-112](../mod-112-enterprise-risk-insurance-and-compliance/)).
- **Outside-counsel management** — the rate cards, engagement letters, panel construction, and billing review that the rest of this chapter describes.

The first hire is emphatically not a specialist. Do not hire an IP-prosecution attorney, a litigation attorney, or a pure privacy attorney as the first in-house lawyer unless the corporation's business model demands it (a pure IP-licensing corporation might legitimately hire an IP attorney first; a privacy-heavy consumer corporation with an active regulator relationship might legitimately hire a privacy attorney first). The generalist commercial-corporate hybrid is the standard, and it is standard because it optimises for the volume-weighted mix of matters an early-stage corporation actually generates.

## The buy-vs-build decision by specialty

The in-house function does not grow uniformly. Different specialties graduate from outside to in-house at different stages, and some specialties never graduate at all. The framework below sequences the specialties by typical in-house-first stage.

### Commercial

Commercial (customer contracts, vendor contracts, reseller and partner contracts) is the first specialty to move in-house because it is the highest-volume and most-repetitive matter type in the portfolio. Once the corporation is closing more than a handful of deals per month, the marginal cost of an outside firm reviewing each redline is uneconomic relative to a trained in-house counsel using the playbook ([chapter 02](./02-contract-playbook-and-fallback-position-matrix.md)) and the CLM ([chapter 07](./07-clm-stack-and-legal-ops-graduation.md)).

The commercial specialty stays in-house permanently. As the corporation scales, additional commercial counsel is added — a Deputy GC Commercial at Series-C+, senior commercial counsel and commercial counsel at Series-D+, dedicated deal-desk analysts and contract managers alongside — but the function is in-house from the first legal hire onward. Outside counsel is engaged only on bespoke deals (large uncapped liability, novel data-residency, government-contracting flowdowns) and on specific specialties (privacy on regulated deals, IP where the counterparty is proposing joint-development structures).

### Corporate and M&A

Corporate work — financings, secondary transactions, equity plan management, board-resolution drafting, state-filing compliance — is the specialty most commonly kept outside longest. The reasons are structural. Financings are event-driven and bursty; the corporation may go 12–18 months between priced rounds and then have three months of intense activity around a closing. Documentation is highly standardised (NVCA forms) and the marginal value of in-house corporate counsel over outside corporate counsel on a fundraise is modest — the outside firm has the templated base, has done thousands of rounds, and has the investor relationships.

Typical progression: outside at Series-A / B (retain the outsourced-GC firm for financing and corporate housekeeping); some in-house corporate work handled by the generalist Head of Legal at Series-C; dedicated Corporate Counsel or Deputy GC Corporate at pre-IPO. The M&A specialty (as the acquirer, in the acquisition curriculum) similarly stays outside through Series-C; a corporation running a serial-acquisition strategy may hire dedicated M&A counsel in-house earlier.

The pre-IPO transition intensifies. IPO preparation requires a Corporate Counsel or Deputy GC Corporate with SEC-registration experience, coordination with underwriter counsel and audit counsel, S-1 drafting, and 34-Act compliance readiness. Most corporations bring on IPO-specific corporate counsel 12–18 months ahead of the target IPO window and retain the outside firm (typically the same startup-focused firm that has been the outsourced GC, escalated to its capital-markets practice) as the lead-counsel-of-record.

### Employment

Employment law is the specialty where the buy-vs-build decision is most sensitive to headcount rather than to revenue. At small headcount (below ~100 employees), employment-law matter volume is low enough that outside employment specialists — Ogletree Deakins, Littler Mendelson, Fisher Phillips, Seyfarth Shaw, Jackson Lewis — can handle the load transactionally. As headcount rises past 100 and starts building toward 500, the frequency of terminations, protected-category concerns (cross-reference [mod-103 chapter 07](../mod-103-employment-law-and-contract-design/07-eeoc-protected-category-framework.md)), performance-improvement plans (cross-reference [mod-107](../mod-107-performance-promotion-and-offboarding/)), leave-of-absence questions, wage-and-hour classification questions, and multi-state employment-law variance (cross-reference [mod-103 chapter 08](../mod-103-employment-law-and-contract-design/08-state-law-variance.md)) makes the outside-only model expensive.

Typical progression: outside employment counsel through Series-A / B; the generalist Head of Legal handles employment-law questions in coordination with the outside employment firm and with the People / HR function ([mod-104](../mod-104-hiring-onboarding-and-hr-operations/)); a fractional employment counsel arrangement at Series-C is sometimes used as an intermediate step; a full-time Head of Employment Law or Employment Counsel at 500+ employees. The full-time employment hire coincides roughly with the corporation's People function maturing into a full HR-business-partner organisation and with the emergence of employee-relations investigations as a distinct workload.

The outside-employment-law firms remain retained even after the in-house hire. Complex terminations of senior executives, EEOC investigations, DOL audits, wage-and-hour class actions, and multi-state litigation continue to route to the outside firms because they have the trial and administrative-agency experience the in-house counsel typically does not.

### IP prosecution (patent and trademark)

IP prosecution is always outside because prosecution is registered-attorney work requiring USPTO admission. In-house counsel who does not hold a USPTO registration cannot file a patent application, respond to an office action, or prosecute a trademark. This is a structural rather than economic constraint.

The outside firms in this space:

- **Patent prosecution** — Fish & Richardson, Kilpatrick Townsend, Sterne Kessler Goldstein & Fox, Knobbe Martens, Finnegan, Wolf Greenfield, and various boutiques by technology area. Selection is by technology sector — a firm with a deep semiconductor practice differs from one with a deep biotech practice.
- **Trademark prosecution** — Kilpatrick Townsend, Fross Zelnick Lehrman & Zissu, and the trademark practices of WSGR, Cooley, Fenwick, and the other startup-focused firms.

The in-house counsel (once hired) does not handle prosecution but does own IP strategy: which inventions to file on, which jurisdictions to pursue, which trademarks to register, budget for the portfolio, coordination between the invention-disclosure pipeline inside the engineering organisation and the outside prosecution counsel, freedom-to-operate analyses (in coordination with outside counsel), and IP-licensing negotiations (which the in-house commercial counsel typically handles or supervises).

Some large corporations at pre-IPO or post-IPO hire in-house Chief IP Counsel who is USPTO-registered and can prosecute in-house. This is uncommon below 500+ employees and typically requires a corporation with a substantial patent portfolio (100+ filed applications) and a demonstrable ROI on bringing prosecution in-house.

### Litigation

Litigation is always outside because litigation demand is bursty, requires specialist trial experience, and requires the courtroom relationships and administrative-agency familiarity that in-house counsel typically cannot maintain. The corporation may go two or three years with no meaningful litigation exposure and then be hit with a class action, a commercial dispute, an IP infringement claim, or a regulatory investigation that requires immediate deep specialist attention.

The outside firms in this space:

- **General commercial litigation and dispute resolution** — Quinn Emanuel Urquhart & Sullivan, WilmerHale, Latham & Watkins, Gibson Dunn & Crutcher, Skadden Arps, Kirkland & Ellis, and various specialist boutiques.
- **IP litigation** — Quinn Emanuel, WilmerHale, Fish & Richardson, Kirkland, Latham, and specialist patent-litigation boutiques (Susman Godfrey, McKool Smith).
- **Employment litigation** — the outside employment firms named above escalate to their litigation practices; specialist plaintiff-side defence boutiques handle wage-and-hour class actions and EEOC / DOL enforcement matters.
- **Regulatory / enforcement** — the same startup-focused firms handle SEC, DOJ, and state-AG investigations through their white-collar / government-enforcement practices; specialist firms (Williams & Connolly, Ropes & Gray, Debevoise) are engaged for the most complex enforcement matters.

The in-house counsel's role in litigation is management and strategy — engaging outside counsel, controlling the budget and scope, preserving privilege, coordinating discovery (in a matter where the corporation is a party, the in-house counsel typically manages the legal-hold process and the document-production coordination), and reporting to the board on the exposure. In-house counsel is rarely the trial attorney and is never the sole attorney of record in a serious litigation matter.

### Privacy

Privacy is the newest specialty in the buy-vs-build framework and the one with the most active graduation activity. Ten years ago, privacy was a corporate afterthought; today it is a mandatory function driven by GDPR, CCPA, HIPAA, state-consumer-privacy statutes (Virginia, Colorado, Connecticut, Utah, Iowa, Indiana, Tennessee, Texas, Oregon, Delaware, and others), the FTC's evolving Section 5 posture on privacy misrepresentations, and the sector-specific frameworks in healthcare (HIPAA), finance (GLBA), education (FERPA), and children's services (COPPA). Cross-reference [mod-110](../mod-110-privacy-data-governance-and-sector-compliance/) for the substantive framework.

Typical progression: outside privacy counsel at Series-A / B (the outsourced-GC firm's privacy practice or a specialist boutique — Hintze Law, Cooley's privacy practice, WSGR's privacy practice, Baker McKenzie for multi-jurisdictional matters); a fractional privacy counsel or Chief Privacy Officer at Series-C; a full-time in-house privacy attorney or CPO at pre-IPO. Corporations in heavily-regulated verticals (healthtech, fintech, edtech, adtech) accelerate the timeline — a healthtech corporation may need a HIPAA-privacy specialist as the second in-house legal hire.

The privacy function frequently splits from the general legal function at scale, becoming its own reporting line under a Chief Privacy Officer with legal authority delegated. The CPO reports to the GC in most corporations; in heavily-regulated verticals, the CPO may report to the CEO or to the audit committee alongside a separate Chief Compliance Officer.

### Tax

Tax is always outside for structural work. Big Law tax partners handle transaction structuring (equity issuances, secondary transactions, financings, M&A tax structuring); Big 4 accounting firms (Deloitte, PwC, KPMG, EY) handle compliance (federal and state income tax returns, sales-and-use tax compliance, employment tax, transfer-pricing documentation for international operations). The reasons are similar to litigation — tax expertise is bursty in application, specialised by sub-area (income tax, sales tax, international tax, transfer pricing, R&D credits, state and local tax), and requires the deep technical training and continuing education that in-house tax counsel is expensive to maintain.

Some corporations at pre-IPO and post-IPO hire in-house tax counsel or tax director positions. These roles typically manage the outside relationships, coordinate transfer-pricing documentation for international operations, and handle the ongoing SALT (state and local tax) compliance work. The specialist transaction and controversy work continues to route to outside counsel and to the Big 4.

## Outside-counsel management discipline

Once the outside-counsel panel exceeds two or three firms, informal management stops working. The corporation needs a discipline for tracking who is engaged on what matter, what the budget looks like against actual spend, and what the rate exposure is. The discipline has four elements: rate cards, matter management, billing review, and panel management.

### Rate cards

A rate card is a negotiated schedule of hourly rates by attorney seniority (partner, senior associate, mid-level associate, junior associate, paralegal, staff attorney) for a specific firm. The corporation should have a signed rate card with every firm on its panel, revised annually. The rate card typically also specifies:

- The base hourly rates by title, with a specific effective date.
- Any negotiated discount off the firm's standard rates (a 5–15% discount is common for a corporation with meaningful projected annual spend at the firm; larger discounts are possible for firms competing for panel placement at high-volume corporations).
- Rate escalation limits — the annual rate increase permitted (typically capped at CPI, CPI+3%, or a fixed percentage such as 5%).
- Named-attorney restrictions — some matters may be restricted to specific named partners, and the corporation may reserve the right to reject associates assigned to its matters.
- Volume tiers — some rate cards include volume-based rebates (e.g., 5% rebate on the fees paid above $250k in a calendar year, escalating to 10% above $500k).

The rate card is a starting point, not a ceiling. AFAs (alternative fee arrangements) — fixed fees per matter type, blended rates, subscription-model retainers — layer on top. But the rate card establishes the base against which AFAs are constructed.

### Matter management

Every engagement of every outside firm should be opened as a numbered matter in the corporation's matter-management system. The matter number becomes the coordinating index for the engagement — the outside firm bills against the matter number, the CLM tracks the associated contract or dispute, the finance system codes the invoice against the matter, and the corporation's reporting rolls up by matter.

The matter should be opened with:

- **A matter name and description.** Specific enough that a reader six months later can tell what the matter is about.
- **A responsible attorney at the outside firm.** The named partner accountable for the matter.
- **A responsible person at the corporation.** The in-house counsel or legal-ops manager owning the matter.
- **A budget.** A dollar estimate or fee cap. Fixed-fee matters have a defined price; hourly matters have a budget the outside firm agrees not to exceed without prior approval.
- **A category / practice area.** Corporate, commercial, employment, IP prosecution, litigation, privacy, tax, other. Enables spend reporting by practice area.
- **A billing frequency and format.** Monthly billing, LEDES (Legal Electronic Data Exchange Standard) format for e-billing tools, task-code-and-activity-code taxonomy per the ABA UTBMS standard.

Matter-management platforms — Legal Tracker (formerly Serengeti), Onit, SimpleLegal, Mitratech TeamConnect, Brightflag, and others (cross-reference [chapter 07](./07-clm-stack-and-legal-ops-graduation.md)) — automate most of this. A corporation with a Big Law relationship should adopt an e-billing platform no later than the first in-house legal hire; a Series-B / C corporation running multiple firms without an e-billing platform is losing 10–20% of its outside-counsel spend to invoice sloppiness that would be caught by the platform's rule engine.

### Billing review

Outside-counsel bills must be reviewed line-by-line. Every invoice is examined against the rate card, the matter budget, the engagement letter's billing rules, and general reasonableness. The review is monthly (or per invoice, if the firm bills more frequently).

Practices to flag on billing review:

- **Block billing.** A single time entry that combines multiple discrete tasks ("reviewed contract, researched issue, called client re: XYZ, drafted response — 4.2h"). Block billing prevents the corporation from evaluating whether time was spent efficiently. Reject block-billed entries; require unbundled time entries at the 0.1-hour increment for each discrete task.
- **First-year-associate research on partner-level questions.** A partner sending a substantive legal question to a first-year associate and then re-reviewing the associate's memo is often duplicative billing. Where the corporation identifies systematic patterns of this, negotiate the pattern out via the engagement letter.
- **Duplicative work across attorneys.** Multiple attorneys billing on the same matter for the same task ("attended conference call" listed by three attorneys at $850, $650, and $450 each). Some duplication is legitimate (a partner and an associate on a client call), but multiple attorneys covering the same ground repeatedly is a spend leak.
- **Vague time entries.** "Attention to matter" or "further work on documents" without specifying what was done. Reject and require more specific entries.
- **Rounding up.** Entries consistently at whole-hour or half-hour increments when the underlying work took a fraction of that time. Enforce the 0.1-hour minimum billing increment.
- **Billing for internal firm training or firm-side turnover.** A new associate being brought up to speed on the matter by reading prior filings should not be billed to the corporation. Enforce this via the engagement letter.
- **Overhead as expense.** Photocopying, faxing, computer research, secretarial overtime, and similar overhead items should not be passed through as expenses on the invoice at cost or with a mark-up. Enforce this via the engagement letter.
- **Travel time.** Travel time billed at full rate is disputable. Standard practice is to bill travel at 50% of the standard rate, or not at all if the attorney is working during the travel.

E-billing platforms automate most of the flagging via rule engines. The in-house counsel or legal-ops manager reviews the flagged entries and either approves, disputes, or writes off the amounts.

### Panel management

A panel is a controlled list of approved outside firms. At Series-A / B, the panel is typically 1–3 firms (the outsourced-GC firm plus perhaps a specialist employment firm and a specialist IP firm). At Series-C+, the panel grows to 3–5 firms per specialty across corporate, commercial, employment, IP, litigation, privacy, and tax.

Panel construction principles:

- **Two firms minimum per critical specialty.** Redundancy prevents lock-in and preserves negotiating leverage.
- **Documented engagement letter with each panel firm.** The engagement letter incorporates the rate card, the billing rules, the matter-management standards, and any AFAs. Cross-reference [chapter 07](./07-clm-stack-and-legal-ops-graduation.md) for CLM tracking of engagement letters.
- **Named partner accountability.** Each panel firm has a named day-to-day partner who is the corporation's primary contact; the corporation does not accept work from associates whose names it does not know.
- **Annual review.** Each panel firm's performance is reviewed annually against a scorecard — cycle time, budget accuracy, quality of work product, responsiveness, cultural fit. Underperforming firms are placed on notice or removed from the panel.
- **Diversity considerations.** Some corporations include diversity metrics in panel management — the demographic composition of the attorneys assigned to the corporation's matters, the firm's own diversity performance, and specific commitments to associate-diversity development. This is corporation-specific and should be built consistent with the corporation's DEI policies (cross-reference [mod-108](../mod-108-culture-employee-experience-and-dei/) for the DEI-programme context).

Panel management does not eliminate the outsourced-GC-of-record relationship. The lead startup-focused firm is typically kept as the corporate-of-record firm even after specialist firms are added to the panel. The lead firm's partner remains the primary institutional relationship, while the specialist firms are engaged for their respective specialties.

## Rate-card and engagement-letter design

The engagement letter is the contract between the corporation and each outside firm. Its structure varies by firm and by corporation, but the elements below are the standard framework.

- **The scope of engagement.** What the firm is being retained to do. May be narrow (a specific matter) or broad (any matter within a specified practice area). The scope should be broad enough to avoid a re-engagement letter for every new matter but narrow enough to constrain the firm's default behaviour.
- **Rate schedule.** The rate card as of the engagement date, with an annual escalation cap. The engagement letter should specify the rate for each attorney title, the applicable discount from standard rates, and the mechanism for annual adjustments.
- **Billing practices.** The specific practices required. Standard clauses include: no block billing; minimum billing increment of 0.1 hours; no billing for internal firm training, associate turnover, or file familiarisation on new associates joining an existing matter; no mark-up on pass-through expenses; travel billed at 50% of the standard rate; monthly billing in LEDES 1998B or equivalent format.
- **Matter management.** Requirements for matter opening, budget approval, and status reporting. Standard clauses include: no work commenced without a matter number and a written budget; budget overages of more than 10% require the corporation's prior written approval; monthly matter status report including hours worked, budget vs. actual, and forecast to completion.
- **AFAs.** Any alternative fee arrangements applicable to specific matter types. Typical AFAs at Series-B / C for a mature outside-counsel programme:
  - Fixed fees per matter type — a fixed fee for M&A due diligence up to a specified deal size; a fixed fee for a standard commercial-contract redline round; a fixed fee for a routine employment termination.
  - Success fees on specific outcomes — a bonus payable to the outside firm on successful defence of a specific type of claim, or on achievement of a specific transaction outcome.
  - Subscription-model retainers — a fixed monthly fee for a defined bucket of general-counsel-type work, typically used with fractional GCs or with outside firms providing high-volume commercial support.
  - Blended rates — a single hourly rate applied across all attorney titles on a matter, in exchange for the firm's discretion to staff appropriately. Reduces the incentive to over-staff with associates.
- **Confidentiality and conflicts.** The firm's obligation to hold the corporation's information confidential and to run conflict checks before accepting adverse engagements. Standard practice is that the firm may not represent an adverse party in any matter without the corporation's prior written consent, subject to state-bar conflict-of-interest rules.
- **Ownership of work product.** The corporation owns the work product (memoranda, contracts, briefs) subject to the firm's retention of its own precedent-and-forms library.
- **Termination.** Either party may terminate the engagement on written notice, subject to the corporation's obligation to pay for services rendered through the termination date. The engagement letter should specify how open matters are transferred on termination.
- **Dispute-resolution mechanic.** Disputes over billing, scope, or performance are typically resolved by escalation to the firm's managing partner and the corporation's GC / CFO before formal proceedings. Some engagement letters include arbitration clauses; some corporations prefer court venue for enforcement flexibility.
- **Specific engagement-letter terms** (retention amounts, credit balances, specific dollar caps on matter budgets) vary by firm and by corporation and should be negotiated matter-by-matter rather than quoted from memory. <!-- needs-research -->

## The escalation-to-outside-counsel decision framework

Once the in-house function exists, the question shifts. Not every matter goes outside; not every matter stays inside. The in-house counsel needs a published framework for deciding when a matter escalates to outside counsel. This framework mirrors and extends the commercial-contract escalation framework in [chapter 02](./02-contract-playbook-and-fallback-position-matrix.md).

Trigger categories for escalation to outside counsel:

- **Deal size above a threshold.** Deals above the corporation's threshold ($250k ARR at Series-A, $500k at Series-B, $1M+ at Series-C) route to outside commercial counsel for redline support. The escalation is not about substantive complexity — it is about the tail-risk exposure and the likelihood that the counterparty is represented by sophisticated outside counsel on their side.
- **Unfamiliar regulated industry.** The corporation's first deal in HIPAA (healthcare), GLBA (financial services), FERPA (education), COPPA (children's services), FISMA / FedRAMP (federal government), state-money-transmitter (money transmission), NYDFS Part 500 (New York financial services), or defense-contracting flowdowns (FAR / DFARS / CMMC / ITAR / EAR) should route to outside counsel with the sector specialty. Subsequent deals in the sector can be handled in-house against a sector-specific playbook supplement.
- **Aggressive IP terms.** Any counterparty claim to ownership of the Service, IP indemnification obligations without the modify / replace / refund escape, joint-development structures where IP allocation matters, or open-source-licensing questions that could infect the corporation's proprietary code. Cross-reference the IP-ownership fallback in [chapter 02](./02-contract-playbook-and-fallback-position-matrix.md).
- **Large uncapped liability.** Any request for uncapped direct-damages liability, uncapped indemnification (beyond the corporation's standard carve-outs for gross negligence, wilful misconduct, and fraud), or contractual commitments that exceed the corporation's insurance tower (cross-reference [mod-112](../mod-112-enterprise-risk-insurance-and-compliance/)).
- **Novel AI terms.** Counterparty demands on training-data usage, model-output ownership, hallucination liability, human-in-the-loop commitments, provenance requirements, or restrictions on the corporation's use of aggregated / de-identified data. Cross-reference [chapter 06](./06-ai-vendor-contracts-and-ai-dpa-pattern.md).
- **Custom data-residency or sovereign-cloud requirements.** Contractual commitments to keep customer data within a specific geography, in a customer-controlled key-management environment, in a sovereign-cloud region, or subject to specific bilateral data-transfer frameworks. These usually require infrastructure changes as well as contractual commitments and require joint engineering / legal / procurement review.
- **Government contracting flowdowns.** Federal, state, or local government deals bring FAR / DFARS flowdowns, Buy American Act, CMMC / FedRAMP security requirements, and audit rights. Always outside counsel for the first several government deals in each jurisdiction; developed sector-specific playbook can bring subsequent deals in-house.
- **Employee-relations matters with litigation potential.** Terminations of executives, whistleblower complaints, EEOC / DOL charges, harassment / discrimination investigations that could result in litigation. Cross-reference [mod-107](../mod-107-performance-promotion-and-offboarding/) for the termination framework and [mod-103](../mod-103-employment-law-and-contract-design/) for the substantive employment-law scaffolding.
- **Regulatory investigations.** Any inbound from a regulator (SEC, DOJ, FTC, state AG, IRS, DOL, EEOC, HHS OCR, FDA, industry-specific regulators). Immediately outside counsel; do not respond substantively without engagement.
- **Litigation threats.** Any threatened or filed litigation. Immediately outside counsel.
- **Novel corporate transactions.** Anything outside the corporation's standard playbook — secondary transactions, tender offers, unusual equity issuances, spin-offs, minority investments, joint ventures. Route to corporate counsel at the outsourced-GC firm.

The framework should be published as a one-page document with a decision tree, referenced in the in-house counsel's playbook and the deal-desk's routing rules. See [chapter 02](./02-contract-playbook-and-fallback-position-matrix.md) for the commercial-specific version.

## Graduation to a full legal function at Series-C / pre-IPO

By Series-C / pre-IPO, the legal function has grown from one Head of Legal to a team of five to twelve people. The typical org shape:

- **General Counsel.** The most senior legal officer. Reports to the CEO. Board attendance in executive session. Owns the enterprise-legal strategy and represents the corporation to major counterparties, regulators, and litigants.
- **Deputy GC Commercial** (or Head of Commercial Legal). Owns the customer-contract stack, the vendor-contract stack, the sales-desk-and-legal-ops function, and the CLM programme. Reports to the GC.
- **Deputy GC Employment** (or Head of Employment Law / Employment Counsel). Owns employment-law policy, employee-relations advice, terminations, EEOC / DOL / state-agency matters, and coordination with the People / HR function. Reports to the GC.
- **Deputy GC Privacy** (or Chief Privacy Officer). Owns privacy compliance across the applicable frameworks (GDPR, CCPA, state-consumer-privacy laws, HIPAA, GLBA, COPPA, FERPA where applicable), coordination with the security function on incident response, and DPA-tier vendor management. Cross-reference [mod-110](../mod-110-privacy-data-governance-and-sector-compliance/). May report to the GC or to a separate reporting line in heavily-regulated verticals.
- **Deputy GC Corporate** (or Corporate Counsel). Owns corporate housekeeping, board-meeting materials, state filings, cap-table management, financings (in coordination with outside counsel), M&A transactions (in coordination with outside counsel), and pre-IPO readiness (in coordination with underwriter counsel). Cross-reference [mod-111](../mod-111-corporate-governance-board-operations-and-officer-duties/).
- **Deputy GC IP** (or Chief IP Counsel). Owns the IP-portfolio strategy, coordination with outside prosecution counsel, freedom-to-operate analyses, IP-licensing negotiations, and open-source-licensing hygiene. Not universally present at pre-IPO — many corporations delegate IP strategy to a Deputy GC Commercial who supervises outside prosecution counsel.
- **Legal Operations Manager / Director.** Owns the CLM, the matter-management platform, the e-billing platform, the outside-counsel-panel management, and the metrics-and-reporting layer for the legal function. Cross-reference [chapter 07](./07-clm-stack-and-legal-ops-graduation.md).
- **Corporate Secretary function.** Owns board-meeting logistics, minute-book maintenance, state-filing compliance, D&O-insurance coordination, and corporate-governance policy. Sometimes housed under the Deputy GC Corporate, sometimes a separate role reporting to the GC or the CFO. Cross-reference [mod-111](../mod-111-corporate-governance-board-operations-and-officer-duties/).
- **Compliance function.** Owns regulatory-compliance programme (see below), policy management, training, and internal audit coordination. Cross-reference [mod-112](../mod-112-enterprise-risk-insurance-and-compliance/).

### The GC / CCO split

In heavily-regulated verticals, the compliance function often separates from the legal function at scale. The Chief Compliance Officer reports independently — typically to the CEO but with a functional dotted line to the audit committee of the board — and holds authority for regulatory-compliance programme design, testing, and reporting. The rationale is independence: the CCO's role is to identify and report compliance failures without being subordinated to the GC, whose role includes defending the corporation against the consequences of those failures.

The GC / CCO split is standard practice in financial-services corporations (banks, broker-dealers, investment advisers, money-services businesses), in healthcare corporations subject to HIPAA / HITECH and Anti-Kickback / Stark laws, and in public companies with SOX-imposed compliance obligations. Cross-reference [mod-112](../mod-112-enterprise-risk-insurance-and-compliance/) for the compliance-programme design.

In corporations without regulated-vertical exposure, the GC often holds the CCO title as well through pre-IPO. Splitting the roles too early creates coordination overhead without corresponding compliance benefit; splitting them too late leaves the CCO conflicted at a moment when the corporation cannot afford the perception of conflict.

## Failure modes

The recurring patterns of failure in this area, in the order they typically manifest across a corporation's lifecycle:

- **Staying with outside counsel too long past the crossover point.** The corporation's monthly Big Law bill has been $30–60k for three consecutive quarters, but the founders have not yet made the in-house hire because "we're not big enough for our own lawyer." The result is cash burn on hourly rates, sales-cycle drag from outside-counsel turnaround times, and loss of institutional memory as associates rotate on and off matters.
- **Hiring the first in-house counsel too early.** A corporation at $2–3M ARR hires a senior Head of Legal at $250k base plus equity. The volume of commercial contracts, corporate work, and employment matters is not sufficient to justify the compensation. The Head of Legal spends time on lower-value work (procurement contracts, generic vendor terms, HR policy drafting) and looks around for a bigger role within twelve months. The corporation loses the hire and repeats the recruitment cycle.
- **Hiring a GC without the right vertical fit.** A healthtech corporation hires a Head of Legal with a strong commercial-and-corporate background but no HIPAA experience. The counsel spends the first year learning HIPAA on the job while the corporation's sales team is quoting business-associate agreements to hospital-system procurement offices without adequate guidance. The gap is filled by outside HIPAA counsel at premium rates, and the corporation is paying twice for the vertical work.
- **Not building the outside-counsel management discipline before growing the panel.** The corporation adds a specialist employment firm, a specialist IP firm, and a specialist litigation firm to its panel at Series-B without introducing an e-billing platform, without negotiating rate cards, and without opening matters properly. The outside-counsel spend rises 40% year-over-year, but the corporation cannot attribute the spend to specific matters or hold the firms accountable for budget accuracy. The problem is not identified until the CFO flags the run-rate at the Series-C fundraise.
- **Collapsing GC and CCO into a single role in a regulated vertical.** A fintech corporation at pre-IPO retains the GC as CCO because it is faster than hiring a separate CCO. When the OCC or NYDFS opens an examination, the regulator raises independence concerns about the corporation's compliance programme, and the audit committee is forced to hire a CCO under time pressure — often at a compensation premium and with less careful vetting than the role deserves.
- **The outsourced-GC firm captures the in-house function.** The corporation hires a Head of Legal from the outsourced-GC firm as its first in-house counsel. On the surface this looks efficient — the counsel already knows the corporation's matters. In practice, the counsel may be reluctant to reduce spend at the former firm or to substitute alternative firms even where the alternatives are cheaper or better. Guard against this via explicit conflict-of-interest disclosure at hire and by requiring the new Head of Legal to run a competitive rate-card negotiation with the former firm within the first six months.
- **Losing the corporate-relationship handshake at first-hire transition.** The outsourced-GC partner's twelve-year relationship with the founder-CEO is a major intangible asset. If the first in-house Head of Legal is territorial and cuts the outside partner out of board-adjacent conversations, the corporation loses a strategic resource. The correct posture is that the in-house counsel manages the outside relationship rather than displaces it.
- **Failing to plan for the litigation-response surge.** The corporation is hit with its first material litigation matter (class-action complaint, IP-infringement suit, regulatory investigation) and does not have a pre-identified outside firm, a pre-negotiated engagement letter, or a legal-hold process. The first two weeks of response are chaotic and expensive. Every corporation should have an "in case of litigation" playbook — the firms to call, the internal escalation path, the legal-hold notice template — established well before the first matter arrives.

## Concrete example

Consider the legal-function evolution of a hypothetical B2B SaaS corporation across five stages. Specific firm assignments are illustrative, not prescriptive; the corporation's actual choices depend on its vertical, its investors' preferences, its geography, and the specific partners available at each firm.

- **Pre-seed.** The founders form the corporation with WSGR as GC of record. The formation partner drafts the certificate of incorporation, the initial-board resolutions, the founder equity issuances (with 83(b) elections filed), the equity incentive plan, and the founder IP-assignment agreements (cross-reference [mod-102](../mod-102-founding-team-legal-architecture/)). The engagement is on WSGR's deferred-fee arrangement; fees are capped until the priced round. Total outside spend to Series-A: low five figures.
- **Seed to Series-A.** WSGR continues as GC of record. The seed and Series-A financings run through WSGR's venture practice. Commercial contracts (customer MSA, DPA, initial vendor contracts) are drafted by a WSGR commercial associate and reviewed by the WSGR partner. Employment-law matters (first ten hires' offer letters and IP-assignment agreements) are handled by WSGR's employment practice on a transactional basis. IP prosecution is referred out to Fish & Richardson for the corporation's initial patent filings on the core technology. Total outside spend at Series-A: mid five to low six figures annually.
- **Series-A / early Series-B (approximately $8M ARR).** The corporation is closing three to five commercial deals per month; WSGR's redline turnaround is stretching to a week; the founder-CEO is spending most of a day per week on legal matters. The corporation brings on a fractional GC on a monthly retainer for approximately 25 hours per month, covering commercial redlines within the playbook, employment-law questions, vendor-contract management, and coordination with WSGR on corporate matters. WSGR remains GC of record for corporate and financing work; Fish & Richardson remains outside prosecution counsel; Ogletree Deakins is added as outside employment counsel for the higher-stakes employment matters. Total outside spend: mid-to-high six figures annually.
- **Series-B (approximately $20M ARR, 150 employees).** The fractional-GC engagement transitions to a full-time first in-house hire. The corporation hires a Head of Legal — a former WSGR senior associate with strong commercial-contract experience and prior in-house exposure at a similar-stage SaaS corporation. The Head of Legal takes ownership of the commercial-contract stack ([chapter 01](./01-customer-contract-suite-msa-sla-dpa-security-aup.md)), the playbook ([chapter 02](./02-contract-playbook-and-fallback-position-matrix.md)), the CLM procurement and roll-out ([chapter 07](./07-clm-stack-and-legal-ops-graduation.md)), the vendor stack, primary IP oversight (in coordination with Fish & Richardson), and outside-counsel management. WSGR remains retained for corporate and financings and adds Cooley as a secondary corporate firm to preserve leverage. Littler Mendelson is added to the panel alongside Ogletree for employment-litigation-tier matters. A litigation firm (Quinn Emanuel) is identified and a general-services engagement letter is signed, on retainer for immediate response if needed. Total outside spend: low seven figures annually; in-house cost adds mid six figures fully-loaded.
- **Series-C (approximately $50M ARR, 400 employees).** The Head of Legal has been promoted to General Counsel and has hired a team of three around them: a Deputy Commercial Counsel handling the day-to-day redline queue, a Legal Operations Manager owning the CLM and matter-management platform (Ironclad + SimpleLegal), and a fractional Chief Privacy Officer engaged for approximately 20 hours per month to cover the corporation's expanding privacy footprint (CCPA, VCDPA, and the corporation's first EU customers requiring GDPR compliance — cross-reference [mod-110](../mod-110-privacy-data-governance-and-sector-compliance/)). The outside panel now includes WSGR (corporate and financings), Cooley (secondary corporate), Fish & Richardson (patent prosecution), Kilpatrick Townsend (trademark prosecution), Littler and Ogletree (employment — one on retainer, one for panel diversity), Quinn Emanuel (litigation on retainer), and Hintze Law (specialist privacy for GDPR-adjacent bespoke matters). Total outside spend: mid seven figures annually; in-house team cost adds seven figures fully-loaded.
- **Pre-IPO (approximately $150M ARR, 1,000 employees).** The full legal function is built out: GC, Deputy GC Commercial, Head of Employment Law (moved from fractional employment counsel to full-time hire at the 500-employee mark), Chief Privacy Officer (moved from fractional to full-time), Corporate Counsel handling the SEC-registration prep in coordination with WSGR's capital-markets practice and the underwriter's counsel, Legal Operations Manager, and a Corporate Secretary function (cross-reference [mod-111](../mod-111-corporate-governance-board-operations-and-officer-duties/)). The compliance function has been split from legal — a Chief Compliance Officer reports to the CEO with a functional dotted line to the audit committee (cross-reference [mod-112](../mod-112-enterprise-risk-insurance-and-compliance/)). Total in-house team cost: low eight figures fully-loaded; outside-counsel spend: high seven figures annually, driven mostly by IPO preparation and by ongoing litigation and regulatory matters.

The specific firm assignments above are illustrative of common patterns in the current market. Any specific corporation's actual choices depend on its investor preferences, its vertical, its geography, the individual partners available, and the historical relationships in place.

## Summary

- Every startup begins with an outsourced-GC arrangement at a startup-focused Big Law firm (Cooley, WSGR, Orrick, Fenwick, Latham, Gunderson, Goodwin, Perkins Coie); the arrangement scales through pre-seed, seed, and early Series-A, often with a deferred-fee mechanism until the priced round.
- The fractional-GC model is an intermediate arrangement at roughly $5–25M ARR — a senior lawyer on monthly retainer for 15–40 hours per month, cheaper than equivalent Big Law hours and more responsive than an outside firm on high-touch matters.
- The first full-time in-house legal hire typically occurs at Series-A or Series-B, at $10–30M ARR and 100–200 employees, when steady-state monthly outside-counsel spend consistently exceeds the fully-loaded cost of a hire.
- The first hire is a generalist commercial-corporate hybrid — usually a senior Big Law associate or a second-in-house-move Head of Legal — with vertical fit (healthtech HIPAA, fintech GLBA, defense-tech ITAR / EAR) prioritised over generalist background where the vertical demands it.
- Specialties graduate to in-house at different stages: commercial first, employment at 500+ employees, corporate at Series-C+, privacy at Series-C in general-purpose corporations, IP prosecution and litigation and tax never (structurally outside).
- Outside-counsel management discipline has four elements: rate cards (negotiated hourly rates by title, annual escalation caps, volume rebates), matter management (numbered matters, budgets, LEDES billing, e-billing platforms), billing review (line-by-line scrutiny for block billing, duplication, vague entries, overhead pass-through), and panel management (controlled list of approved firms per specialty).
- The engagement letter with each panel firm codifies the rate schedule, billing practices, matter-management standards, AFAs (fixed fees per matter type, success fees, blended rates, subscription retainers), conflicts and confidentiality, ownership of work product, termination, and dispute resolution.
- The escalation-to-outside-counsel framework triggers on deal size, unfamiliar regulated industry, aggressive IP terms, large uncapped liability, novel AI terms, custom data-residency, government-contracting flowdowns, employee-relations matters with litigation potential, regulatory investigations, threatened or filed litigation, and novel corporate transactions.
- The Series-C / pre-IPO legal function is a team of five to twelve — GC, Deputy GCs by specialty, Legal Operations, Corporate Secretary, and a Compliance function that may or may not be split from Legal depending on the corporation's regulatory profile.
- Failure modes include staying with outside counsel too long, hiring in-house too early, missing the vertical-fit requirement, growing the panel without the management discipline, collapsing GC / CCO where a split is needed, and failing to plan for the first litigation-response surge before it arrives.
