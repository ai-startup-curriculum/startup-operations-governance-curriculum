# 6. AI vendor contracts and the AI-DPA pattern

> A corporation that ships an AI-powered product is simultaneously a downstream customer of a foundation-model provider and an upstream vendor to enterprise buyers who now demand AI-transparency terms. The corporation's paper on the two sides must be consistent — a customer commitment the corporation cannot satisfy through its vendor stack is a compliance defect waiting for a coincidence with a breach or a regulator.

## Motivation

Every corporation that embeds a foundation model into a customer-facing product occupies two contractual roles at once. On the buy side, it consumes a model-provider API — Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Microsoft, AWS Bedrock, or a specialised inference platform — and inherits that provider's data-handling, IP-indemnity, sub-processor, and model-versioning terms. On the sell side, the same corporation sells its product to enterprise customers whose procurement, privacy, and infosec teams have caught up with the AI wave and now demand AI-transparency addenda, model-provenance disclosure, training-opt-out commitments, human-in-the-loop guarantees, and IP indemnification for AI-generated output. The two sides are the same problem viewed from opposite ends of the data flow, and the corporation's paper has to be consistent across them.

The failure mode this chapter is written against is the corporation that signs an enterprise customer contract with confident AI-transparency language — "we do not train foundation models on your inputs," "we will notify you before switching base models," "we indemnify you against third-party IP claims arising from AI output" — while its own upstream model-provider agreement is the free-tier consumer terms of service, silently training on prompts, silently swapping model versions, and disclaiming any indemnity that would flow through to the customer. The gap between what the corporation is promising downstream and what its vendor stack actually delivers upstream is the compliance defect. Enterprise procurement teams are increasingly sophisticated about spotting this gap on due diligence; regulators under the EU AI Act's downstream-provider obligations will be increasingly sophisticated about enforcing it.

This chapter is the AI-specific extension of the general customer contract suite in [chapter 01](./01-customer-contract-suite-msa-sla-dpa-security-aup.md) and the general vendor contract suite in [chapter 03](./03-vendor-contract-suite-and-onboarding-workflow.md). It treats the two sides — the inbound AI-vendor contract that the corporation signs as a customer, and the outbound AI-transparency addendum that the corporation offers to its own customers — as a single coherent problem. Regulatory depth on GDPR joint-controller analysis, CCPA processor-service-provider mechanics, EU AI Act prohibited-practice and high-risk-system obligations, and sector-specific privacy law is deferred to [mod-110](../mod-110-privacy-data-governance-and-sector-compliance/); the internal-employee AI-usage acceptable-use policy is treated in [mod-108](../mod-108-culture-employee-experience-and-dei/); vendor-risk register and SecReview mechanics belong to [mod-112](../mod-112-enterprise-risk-insurance-and-compliance/). AI-model-risk technical depth — evaluation frameworks, red-teaming methodologies, alignment testing — is out of scope for this curriculum and belongs to the non-role tracks (`chief-ai-officer-learning`, `head-of-ai-governance-learning`, `ai-risk-engineer-learning`).

## The two roles and why they must be consistent

The corporation's data flow through a typical AI feature looks like: enterprise customer -> corporation's application layer -> foundation-model provider API -> model provider's cloud sub-processor (typically a hyperscaler) -> back through the same chain. The customer's data crosses three organisational boundaries and touches four processing entities before it returns to the customer. Each boundary is a contractual interface.

Two structural facts follow.

First, the corporation is the *processor* of the customer's data under GDPR Article 28 and the *service provider* under CCPA / CPRA at Cal. Civ. Code § 1798.140(ag); the model provider is the corporation's *sub-processor*. This characterisation is not optional or negotiable — it is the default legal characterisation of the data flow — and it means the model provider must appear on the corporation's sub-processor list attached to the corporation's customer-facing DPA (see [chapter 01](./01-customer-contract-suite-msa-sla-dpa-security-aup.md)). A corporation that fails to list its model provider as a sub-processor has misrepresented its data-processing architecture to its customers.

Second, every commitment the corporation makes downstream — training-opt-out, model-provenance, breach-notification, IP indemnity, human-in-the-loop, EU-only processing — has to be technically and contractually delivered by the upstream vendor. If the corporation promises the customer that "customer inputs are not used to train the foundation model" but the corporation is using a free-tier or consumer-tier API that reserves the model provider's right to train on inputs, the promise is unenforceable and the corporation has a claim exposure that will surface the first time an enterprise customer's data-governance function reviews the actual API terms.

The remedy is the two-sided AI paper stack this chapter describes: an inbound AI-vendor agreement with training-opt-out and IP-indemnity and sub-processor disclosure and model-provenance and EU-Act GPAI addendum obligations; an outbound AI-transparency addendum that promises what the inbound stack actually delivers, and no more.

## The inbound AI-vendor / AI-DPA pattern

The corporation's contract with its foundation-model provider is a specialised vendor MSA / DPA combination. The general vendor-contract mechanics in [chapter 03](./03-vendor-contract-suite-and-onboarding-workflow.md) apply — MSA, DPA, sub-processor list, insurance, redline tiering, onboarding workflow — but the AI-specific clauses below are additive and non-negotiable for any model provider that will process customer data.

### Input-data confidentiality

The first clause: customer inputs — prompts, embeddings, retrieval-augmented-generation (RAG) context documents, tool-call arguments, system prompts, and anything else the corporation sends to the API — must not leave the model provider's processing environment except for the stated processing purposes (namely, generating the requested inference response). No secondary use for internal analytics, no use for model improvement, no use for training a successor model, no use for evaluating the provider's own competitive products.

The clause is short but load-bearing because it is the foundation on which the training-on-inputs prohibition (below) is built. If the provider has broad "use of Customer Data for any purpose consistent with the Service" rights in its MSA (the same failure pattern discussed on the general vendor side in [chapter 03](./03-vendor-contract-suite-and-onboarding-workflow.md)), the training prohibition is illusory because the provider can reclassify training as "consistent with the Service."

### Output-IP ownership

The corporation should own the outputs it receives — the generated text, code, image, embedding, or structured output — subject to two structural caveats the provider will insist on.

The first caveat is *non-uniqueness*. The provider cannot warrant that the same or similar prompt from a different customer will not yield the same or similar output. Foundation models are deterministic-ish functions of prompt and configuration; two customers who send similar prompts may receive similar outputs; the provider will not indemnify the corporation against another customer of the provider having "the same" output. The clause typically reads that ownership is granted "to the extent the provider has ownership rights to convey," which is honest legal drafting rather than a hidden trap.

The second caveat is that the U.S. Copyright Office has taken the position that outputs generated by artificial intelligence without meaningful human creative contribution are not copyrightable — see the *Copyright Registration Guidance: Works Containing Material Generated by Artificial Intelligence*, 88 Fed. Reg. 16190 (March 16, 2023). The provider cannot convey ownership of something that is not the provider's to convey, and the corporation cannot claim copyright in something the Copyright Office has said is not copyrightable. The practical implication is that AI-generated output owned by the corporation carries the corporation's contractual and confidentiality-based protections but does not necessarily carry a copyright the corporation can assert against a third party who reproduces it. Cross-reference [chapter 04](./04-trademark-copyright-and-patent-basics-for-founders.md) on IP-basics generally.

### Training-on-inputs prohibition

The single most important AI-vendor clause: the provider agrees not to use the corporation's inputs (or the outputs derived from them) to train, fine-tune, evaluate, or otherwise improve the provider's foundation models, its successor models, its content-safety classifiers, or any other product the provider offers. This is the enterprise-tier "zero-data-retention" or "no-training-on-your-data" commitment.

Two structural points that a corporation writing this clause should understand.

The first is that the *consumer / free tier* and the *enterprise / API tier* of most major model providers have different data-handling defaults. Consumer chat products (the individual-user web interfaces of the major model providers) typically *do* train on user inputs by default, subject to a user-side opt-out; the enterprise / commercial API tiers typically *do not* train on inputs by default. <!-- needs-research: verify current Anthropic / OpenAI / Google / Microsoft / AWS enterprise-term detail; the consumer-vs-enterprise data-handling default distinction is well-established in practitioner discussion but each provider's specific commitments and any recent term changes need direct verification against the current published DPA / privacy policy. --> The consequence is that a corporation that lets its engineers use consumer-tier accounts to prototype against production data has silently opted into model training on production data — the failure mode addressed by the internal AI-usage acceptable-use policy in [mod-108](../mod-108-culture-employee-experience-and-dei/).

The second is that the "zero-data-retention" commitment often has caveats — a short retention window for abuse-detection and content-moderation purposes, a right to retain data implicated in a legal hold or law-enforcement request, and a right to retain aggregated / de-identified telemetry. The corporation reviews these caveats against its own customer-facing training commitments to confirm they are compatible.

### Model-provenance disclosure

Which foundation model is actually serving the corporation's traffic. The clause covers three sub-questions.

- **Model identity and version.** The specific model family and version (e.g., Claude Opus 4.5, GPT-4.5, Gemini 2.0 Pro) that is being served in response to the corporation's API calls.
- **Version-change notice.** Whether the provider may deprecate the current version, roll traffic to a successor version, or silently substitute a different model. The enterprise commitment is typically that the provider gives advance notice (60 to 180 days is a typical range) before deprecating a named model version and that the corporation has the ability to pin to a specific version until deprecation.
- **Silent-swap prohibition.** The provider does not silently substitute a different model version behind the same API name without notice. This is genuinely important because model-behaviour differences between versions can invalidate the corporation's own evaluation and QA against a customer-committed baseline.

<!-- needs-research: verify current Anthropic / OpenAI / Google / Microsoft / AWS enterprise-term detail on model-version deprecation notice periods; the 60-180 day range is typical practitioner range but specific vendor commitments vary and change. -->

The corporation needs this clause because its own outbound customer commitments (below) include model-provenance disclosure to the customer. The corporation cannot honestly tell its customer "we use Model X version Y" if the corporation itself does not have contractual clarity from the provider about which model is serving traffic.

### Sub-processor list disclosure

Foundation-model providers themselves depend on cloud hyperscalers to run inference. The dependency map at the time of writing is publicly known — Anthropic's models are served in significant part from AWS infrastructure, OpenAI's from Microsoft Azure, Google's from Google Cloud, and so on — <!-- needs-research: verify current Anthropic / OpenAI / Google / Microsoft / AWS hyperscaler-dependency claims; the specific hyperscaler pairings shift with provider commercial deals and multi-cloud arrangements --> and the sub-processor chain that starts at "the corporation uses Anthropic" ends at "the corporation's customer's data is processed by AWS as a sub-sub-processor."

The chain matters because the corporation's own customer-facing DPA (see [chapter 01](./01-customer-contract-suite-msa-sla-dpa-security-aup.md)) commits the corporation to disclosing its sub-processors on a public list with change-notification obligations. The corporation's list has to include the model provider; the model provider's own sub-processor commitments have to be adequate to support the corporation's customer-facing commitments. If the corporation's customer requires 30-day advance notice of new sub-processors, the corporation's model provider needs to give the corporation at least 30 days' notice of new sub-processors of its own — otherwise the corporation cannot pass the notice through.

### Data-residency and regional processing

Enterprise customers with EU data subjects, healthcare data, financial-services data, or public-sector data increasingly require regional processing commitments — EU-only, US-only, or specified-region endpoints. Some model providers offer regional inference endpoints (an EU-region endpoint that keeps inference traffic within the EU); some do not. <!-- needs-research: verify current Anthropic / OpenAI / Google / Microsoft / AWS regional-endpoint availability; EU, US, UK, Canada, Australia, Japan, and Singapore are the endpoints most commonly available, but availability is provider-specific and shifts. -->

The corporation's inbound contract needs to (i) identify which regions the provider offers, (ii) commit the provider to processing the corporation's traffic in the region(s) selected, and (iii) specify the notice mechanic if the provider proposes to change the region. The corollary is that the corporation's outbound customer contract can only offer regional processing to the extent the model provider actually supports it — an EU-processing commitment on the outbound side that is not supported by an EU-region endpoint on the inbound side is a promise the corporation cannot keep.

### Breach notification aligned to the corporation's own DPA

The model provider's breach-notification commitment must be at least as strict as, and ideally stricter than, the corporation's own commitment to its customers. If the corporation's customer DPA promises 72-hour breach notification (aligned to GDPR Article 33), the model provider needs to commit to notifying the corporation on a timeline that gives the corporation enough runway to notify its customers within the customer-facing window — typically 24 to 48 hours from the provider's discovery.

Cross-reference the DPA anatomy in [chapter 01](./01-customer-contract-suite-msa-sla-dpa-security-aup.md) and the general vendor-DPA-review checklist in [chapter 03](./03-vendor-contract-suite-and-onboarding-workflow.md).

### EU AI Act GPAI-model addendum obligations

The EU Artificial Intelligence Act — Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 13 June 2024 laying down harmonised rules on artificial intelligence, [OJ L 2024/1689](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj) — imposes obligations on providers of general-purpose AI (GPAI) models under Chapter V (Articles 51 through 56). The obligations flow from the model provider to *downstream providers* — corporations like the AI-embedding B2B SaaS the two sides of this chapter contemplate. The corporation's inbound contract with its model provider needs to secure the artefacts that Chapter V requires downstream providers to receive.

The material Chapter V obligations, from the corporation's downstream-provider perspective:

- **Article 53(1)(a) — transparency documentation.** The provider must draw up and keep up to date technical documentation of the model, including training and testing process and evaluation results, containing at minimum the information set out in Annex XI of the Regulation. The corporation, as downstream provider, is entitled to receive this documentation to the extent required to fulfil its own AI Act obligations (particularly if the corporation is building a high-risk AI system under Chapter III).
- **Article 53(1)(b) — downstream provider documentation.** The provider must draw up, keep up to date, and make available specific technical documentation *to providers of AI systems who intend to integrate the general-purpose AI model into their AI systems*. The minimum content is set out in Annex XII. This is the documentation the corporation actually receives — it is designed to enable the downstream provider to understand the capabilities and limitations of the model well enough to comply with the AI Act.
- **Article 53(1)(c) — copyright-compliance policy.** The provider must put in place a policy to comply with Union copyright law, in particular to identify and comply with reservations of rights expressed under Article 4(3) of Directive (EU) 2019/790 (the text-and-data-mining opt-out). The corporation reviews this policy to confirm that the training-data provenance is defensible; a provider whose copyright-compliance policy is thin or absent is exposing the corporation to downstream IP-infringement claims that the provider's own IP indemnity may not fully cover.
- **Article 53(1)(d) — training-data summary.** The provider must draw up and make publicly available a sufficiently detailed summary about the content used for training the general-purpose AI model, according to a template provided by the AI Office.
- **Article 55 — systemic-risk GPAI obligations.** For GPAI models classified as presenting *systemic risk* under Article 51 (a threshold defined in terms of cumulative training compute, currently 10^25 FLOPs, plus qualitative criteria), additional obligations apply: model evaluation including adversarial testing, systemic-risk assessment and mitigation, tracking and reporting of serious incidents, and adequate cybersecurity protection of the model and its physical infrastructure. Downstream providers integrating a systemic-risk GPAI model inherit heightened due-diligence expectations.

<!-- needs-research: confirm current phasing; prohibited-practices from 2 Feb 2025; GPAI-model provisions from 2 August 2025; high-risk-system provisions from 2 August 2026 -->

The corporation's inbound vendor contract needs to (i) require the provider to deliver the Annex XII documentation on request, (ii) require the provider to disclose whether the model is classified as systemic-risk under Article 51 and, if so, provide evidence of Article 55 compliance, (iii) require the provider to disclose its copyright-compliance policy under Article 53(1)(c), and (iv) commit the provider to update this documentation on model-version changes. Without these deliverables, the corporation cannot demonstrate its own AI Act compliance as a downstream provider.

Regulatory depth on the AI Act — prohibited practices under Article 5, high-risk-system obligations under Chapter III, conformity assessment under Chapter III Section 4, and the EU AI Office enforcement mechanic — belongs to [mod-110](../mod-110-privacy-data-governance-and-sector-compliance/).

### AI-vendor IP-indemnification

Third-party copyright and IP-infringement claims arising from AI output are the AI-specific version of the general IP indemnity discussed in [chapter 01](./01-customer-contract-suite-msa-sla-dpa-security-aup.md). The theory of the claim is that the model was trained on copyrighted material without licence, the output reproduces that material in a form that infringes, and the customer using the output is exposed to a direct-infringement claim from the copyright holder.

Major model providers have introduced named IP-indemnity programmes for their enterprise API tiers — commonly branded as "copyright shield" or equivalent commitments — that promise to defend and indemnify enterprise customers against third-party IP-infringement claims arising from model output. <!-- needs-research: verify current Anthropic / OpenAI / Google / Microsoft / AWS IP-indemnity programme names, coverage scope, caps, and carve-outs; the specific programme features shift with provider commercial updates. -->

The clauses to evaluate on a provider's IP-indemnity offering:

- **Trigger.** Does the indemnity respond to any third-party claim that the output infringes IP, or only to claims that are actually reduced to judgement, or only to specific categories of claims (copyright but not patent, for example)?
- **Carve-outs.** Common carve-outs include (i) claims arising from the customer's misuse of the model (using it for a purpose outside the terms of service), (ii) claims arising from jailbreaks or attempts to bypass content-safety filters, (iii) claims arising from the customer's use of "unfiltered" or "raw" model modes if the provider offers them, (iv) claims arising from the customer having modified or fine-tuned the model, (v) claims arising from the customer's unlawful inputs. The scope of these carve-outs is the negotiation surface.
- **Cap.** The dollar amount or formula limiting the provider's exposure. <!-- needs-research: verify current Anthropic / OpenAI / Google / Microsoft / AWS IP-indemnity dollar caps; caps range widely across providers and tiers, and specific figures should be confirmed against the current published enterprise agreement rather than reported from memory. -->
- **Investment-grade evaluation.** The corporation evaluates whether the indemnity is genuinely useful by asking (i) is the provider financially capable of paying out a large IP claim if it materialised, (ii) do the carve-outs swallow the coverage for realistic risk scenarios, (iii) does the indemnity flow through the corporation's use to the corporation's own customers, or does it stop at the corporation.

The critical operational point: the provider's IP indemnity to the corporation typically does *not* flow through to the corporation's own downstream customers. The corporation, as counterparty of record to its own customer, is the party the customer will sue if AI output infringes; the corporation must then look to the provider under its own inbound contract. This creates a mismatch — the corporation's outbound indemnity has different triggers, caps, and carve-outs from the inbound indemnity that would fund the corporation's defence. The corporation manages this mismatch consciously (see the outbound section below) rather than assuming inbound coverage will simply cover outbound exposure.

## AI-vendor tiering in the onboarding workflow

The corporation's general vendor-tier framework in [chapter 03](./03-vendor-contract-suite-and-onboarding-workflow.md) needs an AI-specific overlay. Three AI-vendor tiers, with distinct treatment:

**AI Tier A — general-purpose foundation-model API providers.** The corporation's direct API consumption of a major model provider (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Microsoft, AWS Bedrock or equivalent inference platform). Treatment: full inbound AI-vendor contract per the pattern above, including training-opt-out, enterprise-grade DPA, sub-processor disclosure, model-provenance clause, EU AI Act GPAI addendum documentation, regional-endpoint commitment where required, IP-indemnity evaluation. Full SecReview under [mod-112](../mod-112-enterprise-risk-insurance-and-compliance/). Annual re-review; more frequent review triggered by any provider policy or terms change. This is the primary model-provider layer and the anchor of the corporation's AI stack.

**AI Tier B — vertical AI SaaS embedding a foundation model.** Third-party vendors whose product is itself an AI application built on a foundation model (an AI-powered sales-intelligence tool, an AI-powered legal-research tool, an AI-powered code-analysis tool, an AI-powered marketing-copy tool). The corporation is consuming an AI product rather than raw model inference, but the underlying data flow still passes through a foundation-model provider — one the corporation may or may not have a direct relationship with. Treatment: the corporation's security and legal review of the AI SaaS vendor must include analysis of the vendor's own model-provider commitments and how they flow through to the corporation. Specifically, (i) which model provider is the AI SaaS vendor using, (ii) is the AI SaaS vendor on the enterprise / commercial tier with training-opt-out, (iii) does the AI SaaS vendor's DPA identify the model provider as a sub-processor, (iv) does the AI SaaS vendor pass the model provider's IP indemnity through to the corporation. A vendor that cannot answer these questions clearly is a Tier B vendor to reject or to move to a lower risk classification (no processing of confidential business data).

**AI Tier C — consumer / individual-tier AI tools.** Individual-user AI products (the consumer chat interfaces of the major model providers; AI writing assistants; AI transcription apps on the free tier; browser-plugin AI copilots). These tools' consumer terms of service typically reserve the provider's right to train on user inputs. Treatment: banned for business data by internal policy. The ban is enforced through the internal AI-usage / acceptable-use policy in [mod-108](../mod-108-culture-employee-experience-and-dei/), which prohibits employees from using consumer-tier AI tools with any category of business data (customer data, financial data, source code, HR data, legal data, or any other data classified as confidential or higher).

The AI-tier overlay is applied during vendor onboarding at the same step that the general vendor-tier assignment is made (see [chapter 03](./03-vendor-contract-suite-and-onboarding-workflow.md)); the two dimensions — general vendor tier and AI vendor tier — combine to determine review depth.

## The outbound AI-transparency addendum

The corporation's own customer MSA / DPA now needs an AI-specific addendum because enterprise customers ask for it, because emerging regulation requires disclosures the general customer contract does not cover, and because it is the mirror image of what the corporation is demanding from its own model provider. The addendum is a self-contained document that plugs into the customer contract suite from [chapter 01](./01-customer-contract-suite-msa-sla-dpa-security-aup.md), typically executed alongside the MSA or referenced from the DPA.

The addendum's canonical sections:

### AI-in-the-loop disclosure

A plain-language statement of which product features use AI, which model families and versions serve those features, and whether the AI's output is (i) generated (the AI is creating content the user sees), (ii) ranked or retrieved (the AI is scoring or selecting from a pre-existing set), or (iii) inferred (the AI is producing a classification, prediction, or extraction). The distinction matters because customer risk analysis, downstream regulatory obligations, and human-oversight requirements attach differently to generative and non-generative features.

The disclosure is written for the customer's data-governance and procurement reviewers, not for the customer's end users; the end-user-facing disclosure obligation (e.g., under EU AI Act Article 50 on transparency to natural persons interacting with AI systems) is a separate obligation that the corporation implements in the product UI, not in the contract.

### Human-in-the-loop commitments for regulated verticals

Where the corporation's product is used in regulated verticals — employment, credit, insurance, healthcare, education, public services, immigration, law enforcement — the addendum commits the corporation to human-oversight design where operationally true. Two representative regulatory anchors:

- **NYC Local Law 144 — Automated Employment Decision Tools (AEDT).** New York City's local law and its implementing rules impose bias-audit, notice, and transparency obligations on employers using automated employment decision tools. A corporation selling to enterprise employers whose AEDT use will be covered has to represent that its product enables — or does not impede — the employer's compliance.
- **EU AI Act Article 14 — human oversight.** For AI systems classified as *high-risk* under Chapter III of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 14 requires that "high-risk AI systems shall be designed and developed in such a way, including with appropriate human-machine interface tools, that they can be effectively overseen by natural persons during the period in which they are in use." The corporation, if selling into an AI system that qualifies as high-risk under Annex III (biometrics, critical infrastructure, education and vocational training, employment, essential private and public services, law enforcement, migration and asylum, administration of justice), needs to enable the deployer's Article 14 compliance.

The commitment is specific: for named regulated-use categories, the corporation warrants that no automated adverse decision (rejection, denial, adverse action, high-risk classification) will be made or communicated to the affected data subject without meaningful human review. "Meaningful" is the load-bearing word — a rubber-stamp human review does not satisfy Article 14 or the analogous US-state expectations. The corporation should not offer this commitment for use cases where the operational reality is a fully automated pipeline.

### Model-provenance disclosure to the customer

The customer-facing mirror of the inbound model-provenance clause. The corporation discloses (i) which foundation-model provider is used for which product feature, (ii) which model version tier is currently deployed, and (iii) the notice mechanic on version or provider changes. The notice window is typically shorter on the outbound side (30 days is common) than on the inbound side (60-180 days is typical) — the corporation has a shorter runway to notify customers than the provider has to notify the corporation.

The disclosure is negotiable in scope. Some enterprise customers demand the specific version pin (e.g., "the corporation warrants that the AI feature is served by Anthropic Claude Opus 4.5"); most accept a family and version-tier disclosure (e.g., "the AI feature is served by Anthropic's Claude family, currently version tier Opus"). Over-specific version pins are dangerous because they may be inaccurate the day after signature — the model may have already deprecated the pinned version.

### Sub-processor disclosure including the model provider

The customer-facing DPA's sub-processor list (see [chapter 01](./01-customer-contract-suite-msa-sla-dpa-security-aup.md)) must include the foundation-model provider by name and, ideally, the model provider's own primary cloud sub-processor (as a sub-sub-processor). The customer's sub-processor-review process will look for the model provider on the list; not finding it is a signal to procurement that the corporation has not thought about its AI supply chain carefully.

The change-notification mechanic for the model-provider entry is the same 30-day advance-notice mechanic as for other sub-processors, with the customer's right to object. The corporation should note internally that a customer objection to the model provider itself is very difficult to accommodate — the model provider is often architecturally load-bearing for the feature — and the corporation may need to negotiate a specific carve-out that the customer's objection triggers a right for the customer to terminate the affected feature rather than a right to force the corporation to switch model providers.

### Training-data commitments

The commitment the customer will most closely scrutinise. The corporation commits, with a specific scope:

- Customer inputs (prompts, uploaded documents, user data submitted through the product) are *not* used to train the corporation's own AI models.
- Customer inputs are *not* used to train the underlying foundation model — the corporation passes the model provider's own training-opt-out commitment through to the customer.
- Customer inputs are *not* used to fine-tune models on behalf of other customers or to build shared-tenant model improvements.
- Customer inputs are used only to serve the customer's own inference requests and, where the customer explicitly opts in per SKU or per feature, to train customer-specific fine-tunes for the customer's exclusive benefit.

This is the Series-A / Series-B canonical commitment: no training on customer inputs unless the customer explicitly opts in per SKU or per feature. The commitment is defensible only if the corporation's own upstream vendor contract actually delivers no-training-on-inputs — which is why the inbound and outbound sides of the AI paper stack must be consistent.

### AI-indemnification pattern for customer contracts

The customer-facing mirror of the inbound IP-indemnity provision. Enterprise customers now routinely redline for IP indemnity covering AI-generated outputs; the negotiation is over four parameters.

- **Trigger.** Whether the indemnity responds to any third-party IP-infringement claim arising from the corporation's product output, or whether it is limited to specific categories of claim (third-party copyright claim; not third-party defamation or misinformation claim). The narrower trigger is the corporation's default; enterprise customers frequently push for broader triggers.
- **Exclusions.** The carve-outs the corporation writes to mirror its own inbound carve-outs, plus additional carve-outs for customer-side behaviours the corporation cannot control: (i) customer prompt manipulation or jailbreak attempts, (ii) customer use of the product outside documented modes, (iii) customer combination of AI outputs with other content, (iv) customer's failure to review AI output before external use, (v) customer's own inputs violating third-party IP. The corporation should not offer indemnity for output the customer has actively manipulated the corporation's product to produce.
- **Cap.** Whether the indemnity is uncapped, super-capped (a specific higher cap than the general liability cap), or capped at the general liability cap of the MSA. The canonical customer redline asks for uncapped IP indemnity; the corporation's defensible position is a super-cap that aligns with the corporation's cyber-liability and IP-liability insurance layer, discussed in [mod-112](../mod-112-enterprise-risk-insurance-and-compliance/). <!-- needs-research: verify current market ranges for AI-IP-indemnity super-caps at Series-B; the multiples applied vary widely and specific figures should not be quoted without direct market verification. -->
- **Flow-through from model provider.** Whether the corporation's own model-provider indemnity flows through to the customer. Typically it does not — the corporation is the counterparty of record and receives the model-provider indemnity in the corporation's own name; the customer's claim runs against the corporation, and the corporation's recovery against the model provider is a separate action. The corporation should not represent to the customer that the model provider's indemnity flows through unless it actually does (which is rare).

The AI-indemnity clause is written knowing the corporation's own inbound coverage will fund some but not all of the outbound exposure. The delta between the outbound exposure and the inbound recovery is the corporation's balance-sheet exposure and needs to be aligned with the cyber-liability and IP-liability layer of the insurance stack in [mod-112](../mod-112-enterprise-risk-insurance-and-compliance/).

### Data-processing-role classification

The customer-facing DPA identifies the corporation as processor and the model provider as sub-processor. Cross-reference [chapter 01](./01-customer-contract-suite-msa-sla-dpa-security-aup.md) on the general controller / processor characterisation.

Two AI-specific twists.

First, where the corporation retains any training or model-improvement data derived from customer inputs (even with the customer's opt-in), the corporation is arguably acting as a *controller* for that specific processing purpose, not a processor — because the corporation is determining the purpose of the further processing (its own model improvement) independently of the customer's instruction. This can shift the characterisation to *joint-controller* under GDPR Article 26, which triggers specific transparency and allocation-of-responsibility obligations. The joint-controller analysis is fact-specific and belongs to [mod-110](../mod-110-privacy-data-governance-and-sector-compliance/); the corporation should flag any customer-opt-in-training arrangement for privacy-counsel review rather than defaulting to a processor characterisation.

Second, the model provider's own processing of the corporation's data — for abuse-detection, content-moderation, or safety-monitoring purposes — may exceed the strict processor scope. Some providers arguably act as controllers for their safety-monitoring processing. The corporation's DPA needs to acknowledge this rather than misrepresent the provider as a pure processor.

## AI-specific SLA and warranty design

The general SLA in [chapter 01](./01-customer-contract-suite-msa-sla-dpa-security-aup.md) needs an AI-specific carve-out because some commitments the general SLA supports on non-AI features are not honestly committable on AI features.

### What can be committed

- **Uptime.** The AI feature's availability (the API responds to requests within a defined success-response tolerance) is committable on the same tiered structure (99.5 / 99.9 / 99.95 / 99.99) as the general SLA. The corporation typically offers a lower uptime tier for AI features than for non-AI features because the AI features' dependency on an upstream model provider adds a failure mode the corporation does not fully control. A 99.5% or 99.9% AI-feature uptime is the common Series-B commitment even where the general product SLA is 99.95%.
- **Response-time / latency.** The time between request and response, measured at a defined percentile (p50, p95, p99). This is committable but should be defined against the actual observed distribution — a p95 latency commitment against a foundation-model API is bounded by the model provider's own p95 latency and cannot be lower.
- **Throttling and rate-limit visibility.** The corporation commits to disclosed rate limits and to advance notice of rate-limit changes.

### What cannot be committed at any operationally-meaningful level

- **Accuracy.** The corporation cannot commit to a specific accuracy percentage on model output because accuracy is a property of the model, the input distribution, and the evaluation criteria — none of which are fully within the corporation's control and none of which can be measured reliably at contract-obligation granularity.
- **Hallucination rate.** The rate at which the model produces factually incorrect or fabricated output. Same reasons as accuracy — the corporation cannot measure hallucination rate at a customer-obligation-relevant granularity and cannot commit to a bounded rate.
- **"The AI will produce correct results."** A general warranty of AI output correctness is unsafe at any deal size. Foundation models are stochastic and produce incorrect outputs even in well-scoped domains; a warranty of correctness is a warranty the corporation will breach.
- **Freedom from bias.** The corporation cannot warrant that model outputs are free from bias in any operationally-meaningful sense because bias is a property of the training data, the model architecture, and the deployment context, and because "bias" itself is contested. The corporation can commit to specific fairness-evaluation processes (Article 10 of the AI Act imposes data-governance obligations on high-risk AI providers, which includes bias examination) but not to a bounded absence of bias.

### The AI-specific SLA carve-out

The practical drafting response is an AI-specific SLA section that (i) applies the general uptime and response-time tiers to the AI features at appropriate levels, (ii) explicitly disclaims warranties of accuracy, correctness, hallucination rate, and freedom from bias, (iii) recommends the customer implement its own human-in-the-loop review for high-stakes decisions, and (iv) commits the corporation to defined evaluation and monitoring processes rather than to outcome metrics.

The disclaimer should be in the same conspicuous-and-in-bold form as the general UCC § 2-316-compliant disclaimer of implied warranties in [chapter 01](./01-customer-contract-suite-msa-sla-dpa-security-aup.md), so that a court applying UCC Article 2 or an analogous body of law will honour it.

## ISO/IEC 42001:2023 as the emerging AI-audit framework

ISO/IEC 42001:2023 — Information technology — Artificial intelligence — Management system — is an international standard published in December 2023 that specifies requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving an AI management system within organisations. It is structurally analogous to ISO 27001 (information-security management system) and to SOC 2 (security-and-availability trust-services criteria) but focused specifically on AI-development and AI-deployment governance.

ISO/IEC 42001's scope covers governance structure and accountability, AI policy and objectives, risk assessment and treatment for AI systems, resource management (including data governance), operational planning and control, performance evaluation and monitoring, internal audit, management review, and continual improvement — the same "plan-do-check-act" management-system pattern as other ISO management-system standards. Annex A of the standard contains a set of AI-specific controls (data-quality management, transparency, human oversight, model-lifecycle management, third-party AI-system due diligence, incident response for AI failures) that a certified organisation implements or explicitly excludes.

Enterprise customer expectations around ISO/IEC 42001 are still emerging. As of the current market state, some enterprise procurement questionnaires now ask about ISO/IEC 42001 certification or roadmap; SOC 2 Type II remains the more universally-requested artefact for general security-and-availability posture. The corporations that are pursuing ISO/IEC 42001 certification are typically (i) those selling into EU customers concerned about AI Act compliance evidence, (ii) those selling into regulated verticals with heightened AI-governance expectations (healthcare, financial services, public sector), and (iii) larger AI-first corporations that see the certification as a differentiator.

The certification's relationship to SOC 2 Type II is complementary rather than substitutive: SOC 2 Type II covers security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality, and privacy trust-services criteria as they apply to a service organisation generally; ISO/IEC 42001 covers AI-specific management-system requirements that SOC 2 does not address. A corporation with a mature AI product will likely need both eventually — SOC 2 for the general service-organisation posture, ISO/IEC 42001 for the AI-specific governance. The audit surface overlaps in some places (both address personnel training, third-party risk, and incident response) but the AI-specific content in ISO/IEC 42001 Annex A does not have a SOC 2 equivalent.

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework (NIST AI RMF 1.0, released January 2023, and its Generative AI Profile released July 2024) is a related-but-distinct artefact. NIST AI RMF is a voluntary framework, not an audit standard — it structures AI-risk management around four functions (govern, map, measure, manage) but does not produce a certification. Corporations frequently reference NIST AI RMF alignment in customer-facing security responses as evidence of a structured AI-risk approach, even without a formal certification.

## Common failure modes

- **Accepting a model provider's consumer / free tier for business data.** Engineers or product managers prototype against production customer data using an individual account whose terms of service reserve the provider's right to train on inputs; the corporation has silently opted in to model training on customer data.
- **Missing the training-on-inputs opt-out.** The corporation is on an enterprise-tier API but the terms of service default to training-on-inputs with a customer opt-out that the corporation has not exercised. The corporation discovers on a customer audit that its customer data has been used for model improvement.
- **Committing to AI-based feature parity that the model version defeats.** The corporation writes an outbound customer commitment ("the AI feature will produce output substantially equivalent to the demonstration provided during evaluation") without securing an inbound model-version pin. The model provider deprecates the version underlying the demonstration; the corporation is in breach without any means to remedy short of switching providers.
- **Inheriting inbound IP-indemnity carve-outs into the customer contract.** The corporation offers the customer IP indemnity mirroring the corporation's inbound coverage without checking that the customer's own use pattern is not carved out. A customer whose product usage includes user-facing content generation that the provider would characterise as "unfiltered" has no coverage; the corporation's outbound indemnity to the customer is on the corporation's own balance sheet.
- **Failing to list the model provider as a sub-processor.** The corporation's customer-facing sub-processor list omits the foundation-model provider — either by oversight or on the mistaken theory that "the model provider does not receive personal data because the corporation only sends prompts." Customer inputs are personal data; the model provider is a sub-processor; the omission is a DPA breach.
- **Committing to a hallucination-rate cap or accuracy warranty.** The corporation offers a customer a bounded hallucination rate or accuracy warranty to close a difficult deal. The commitment is un-measurable in practice and un-defensible in a breach analysis; the corporation is on the wrong end of every subsequent output dispute.
- **Not maintaining a consumer-tier ban for employees.** The internal AI-usage policy in [mod-108](../mod-108-culture-employee-experience-and-dei/) is drafted but not enforced; employees continue to use consumer-tier AI tools with business data; the corporation's outbound training-opt-out commitment is unenforceable because the corporation itself is leaking data to consumer-tier tools.
- **Silent model-version swaps not surfaced to the customer.** The model provider silently rolls the API's underlying model to a successor version; the corporation is unaware; the customer's evaluation of the corporation's product against a baseline is invalidated. The corporation's version-notice commitments to the customer are breached without the corporation knowing.
- **EU AI Act GPAI documentation not requested and not on file.** The corporation is a downstream provider under the AI Act, is subject to the AI Office's downstream-provider expectations, and cannot produce the Annex XII documentation that Article 53(1)(b) entitles it to receive because the corporation never requested it from the model provider.

## Concrete example: Acme Analytics ships an AI-powered analytics feature

Acme Analytics, the same Series-B B2B SaaS from the [chapter 01](./01-customer-contract-suite-msa-sla-dpa-security-aup.md) example, now ships an AI-powered analytics-summarisation feature that takes customer data-warehouse query results and produces plain-language summaries. The feature is built on a foundation-model API. The corporation's paper stack on both sides looks like the following.

### Inbound: Acme's contract with its model provider

- **Enterprise-tier API agreement**, negotiated at the corporation level rather than by a business-unit corporate credit card. The corporation has a signed MSA and DPA with the model provider covering all Acme's use of the API. <!-- needs-research: verify current Anthropic / OpenAI / Google / Microsoft / AWS enterprise-agreement structure and typical minimum-commit levels. -->
- **Training-on-inputs prohibition** confirmed — the enterprise-tier commitment is that Acme's inputs and derived outputs are not used to train, fine-tune, or evaluate the provider's foundation models.
- **Model-provenance addendum** — Acme has pinned to a named model family and version tier; the provider commits to a deprecation-notice window before the pinned version is retired. <!-- needs-research: verify current deprecation-notice periods offered by major providers. -->
- **EU-region endpoint** available — Acme's EU customers are served by the provider's EU-region inference endpoint; the corporation's customer-facing regional-processing commitment is technically deliverable.
- **Sub-processor disclosure** — the provider's sub-processor list identifies the hyperscaler(s) supporting inference and Acme has passed this through to its own downstream sub-sub-processor disclosure. <!-- needs-research: verify current Anthropic / OpenAI / Google / Microsoft / AWS hyperscaler dependencies and sub-processor list mechanics. -->
- **EU AI Act GPAI documentation** — Acme has received the provider's Annex XII downstream-provider documentation under Article 53(1)(b) and the provider's copyright-compliance policy under Article 53(1)(c); Acme has confirmed whether the model is classified as systemic-risk under Article 51 and, if so, has evidence of Article 55 compliance.
- **IP-indemnity programme** — Acme is enrolled in the provider's enterprise IP-indemnity programme. <!-- needs-research: verify current programme names, coverage triggers, carve-outs, and caps. --> Acme has evaluated the carve-outs against Acme's own product use pattern and has documented in the risk register (see [mod-112](../mod-112-enterprise-risk-insurance-and-compliance/)) which of Acme's customer-facing use cases fall inside or outside the indemnity.
- **Breach-notification commitment** aligned to 24-48 hours from provider's discovery of a data-security incident, sufficient to support Acme's own 72-hour customer commitment.

### Outbound: Acme's AI-transparency addendum to its customer MSA

- **AI-in-the-loop disclosure** — the customer receives a plain-language description of the analytics-summarisation feature, identifying it as a generative feature (as opposed to a ranking or retrieval feature), naming the model family (without over-specifying the version pin), and describing how the customer's data flows through the feature.
- **Human-in-the-loop guidance** — for customers using the feature in regulated verticals (Acme has customers in healthcare and financial-services adjacencies), Acme recommends and enables human review of AI-generated summaries before external use; the addendum commits Acme to not making any automated adverse decision on behalf of the customer's own end users.
- **Model-provenance disclosure to customer** — Acme identifies the model family, commits to 30 days' advance notice before switching to a different base model, and pins the version tier without pinning the specific point release.
- **Sub-processor list update** — the model provider is added to Acme's public sub-processor list with 30 days' advance change-notice. The customer has a right to object and, for AI-only feature use, a right to terminate the affected feature if Acme cannot accommodate.
- **Training-data commitments** — Acme commits that (i) customer inputs are not used to train Acme's own models, (ii) the model provider has committed to Acme that customer inputs are not used to train the foundation model, and (iii) any customer-specific fine-tuning is opt-in per SKU and produces a fine-tune available only to the opting-in customer.
- **AI-IP-indemnification for customer** — Acme offers IP indemnity for third-party copyright claims arising from AI-generated output, with carve-outs for customer prompt manipulation, jailbreak attempts, use outside documented modes, and customer combination of AI output with the customer's own or third-party content. The cap is a super-cap sized against Acme's cyber-liability and IP-liability insurance layer (see [mod-112](../mod-112-enterprise-risk-insurance-and-compliance/)). <!-- needs-research: verify current market ranges for AI-IP-indemnity super-caps and how they interact with cyber-liability and E&O policy limits at Series-B stage. -->
- **AI-specific SLA carve-out** — 99.5% uptime on the AI-summarisation feature (lower than Acme's 99.9% general SLA), p95 latency of a defined value against the model provider's own p95, explicit disclaimers of accuracy, hallucination-rate, correctness, and bias-freedom warranties in bold.
- **ISO/IEC 42001 roadmap statement** — Acme references its NIST AI RMF alignment and, for European customers, its ISO/IEC 42001 certification roadmap; SOC 2 Type II remains the primary security-assurance artefact.

### Consistency check

Acme's legal ops function runs a quarterly consistency check: does every outbound customer commitment have a corresponding inbound provider commitment? Training-opt-out — yes; provider commits, Acme passes through. Model-provenance — yes; provider gives 60-day pre-deprecation notice, Acme gives 30-day customer notice. Regional processing — yes; provider has EU endpoint, Acme routes EU-customer traffic through it. Breach-notification — yes; provider commits 24-48 hours, Acme commits 72 hours. IP-indemnity — partial; provider covers a defined scope, Acme's outbound scope is broader and the delta is Acme's balance-sheet exposure (documented in the risk register). Sub-processor disclosure — yes; Acme has the current provider list and updates its own list on change.

The consistency check is the operational discipline that converts the two-sided AI paper stack from "two documents signed at different times" into a coherent compliance posture. Where the check finds a gap — an outbound commitment that the inbound stack cannot fulfil — the gap is either closed (renegotiate the outbound commitment, renegotiate the inbound gap) or documented (accept the delta as balance-sheet exposure, size the insurance layer accordingly).

## Ownership boundary reminder

This chapter is the AI-specific extension of the commercial-contract stack and the general vendor-onboarding workflow. It defers deliberately:

- **[mod-110](../mod-110-privacy-data-governance-and-sector-compliance/)** — GDPR joint-controller analysis, CCPA / CPRA service-provider mechanics, EU AI Act depth for prohibited-practice obligations and high-risk-system conformity assessment, and sector-specific privacy rules (HIPAA, GLBA, FERPA, COPPA). Any question about the substantive privacy-law obligations that make an AI vendor's DPA adequate or inadequate belongs there.
- **[mod-108](../mod-108-culture-employee-experience-and-dei/)** — the internal-employee AI-usage / acceptable-use policy that governs employees' use of consumer AI tools and enterprise AI accounts. The Tier-C ban on consumer AI tools for business data is enforced there.
- **[mod-112](../mod-112-enterprise-risk-insurance-and-compliance/)** — the SecReview and VendorReview workflow that assesses AI vendors' security posture before signature, the risk-register mechanic that tracks AI-specific risks including inbound-outbound indemnity delta, and the cyber-liability / IP-liability insurance layer that funds the corporation's balance-sheet exposure.
- **Non-role tracks (`chief-ai-officer-learning`, `head-of-ai-governance-learning`, `ai-risk-engineer-learning`)** — AI-model-risk technical depth, red-team methodology, evaluation frameworks, alignment testing, and the internal AI-governance operating model that a corporation with mature AI products establishes. This chapter treats those disciplines as inputs — the corporation's contract commitments are informed by the AI-risk function's assessment — but does not teach them.

## Summary

- The corporation that ships an AI-powered product is simultaneously a customer of a foundation-model provider and a vendor to enterprise buyers; the two contract sides must be structurally consistent, or the corporation has a compliance defect and a claim exposure.
- The inbound AI-vendor / AI-DPA pattern requires: input-data confidentiality, output-IP ownership subject to non-uniqueness and Copyright-Office limits ([88 Fed. Reg. 16190](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2023/03/16/2023-05321/copyright-registration-guidance-works-containing-material-generated-by-artificial-intelligence)), training-on-inputs prohibition on the enterprise / API tier (consumer tiers typically do train on inputs — see [mod-108](../mod-108-culture-employee-experience-and-dei/) for the internal ban), model-provenance and version-change notice, sub-processor disclosure covering the hyperscaler chain, regional-endpoint availability, breach-notification aligned to the corporation's own DPA in [chapter 01](./01-customer-contract-suite-msa-sla-dpa-security-aup.md), and EU AI Act Chapter V documentation under Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (Articles 53 and 55, Annexes XI and XII).
- The provider's IP-indemnity programme is evaluated on trigger, carve-outs, cap, and whether it flows through to the corporation's own customers (usually not); the delta between inbound coverage and outbound exposure is the corporation's balance-sheet exposure and is sized against the cyber-liability layer in [mod-112](../mod-112-enterprise-risk-insurance-and-compliance/).
- The AI-vendor tiering overlay on the general vendor tiers in [chapter 03](./03-vendor-contract-suite-and-onboarding-workflow.md): Tier A (foundation-model API providers — full AI-DPA), Tier B (vertical AI SaaS embedding a foundation model — flow-through review of the SaaS vendor's own model-provider commitments), Tier C (consumer / individual-tier AI tools — banned for business data by internal policy).
- The outbound AI-transparency addendum covers: AI-in-the-loop disclosure, human-in-the-loop commitments for regulated verticals (NYC Local Law 144 for employment; EU AI Act Article 14 for high-risk systems under Chapter III of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689), model-provenance disclosure to the customer, sub-processor disclosure including the model provider, training-data commitments (no training on customer inputs unless the customer opts in per SKU or per feature), AI-IP-indemnification with defined carve-outs, and data-processing-role classification with a joint-controller flag for training-opt-in arrangements (deferred to [mod-110](../mod-110-privacy-data-governance-and-sector-compliance/)).
- AI-specific SLA design commits uptime, response-time, and rate-limit visibility; disclaims accuracy, hallucination-rate, correctness, and bias-freedom in a conspicuous UCC § 2-316-compliant form; and typically applies a lower uptime tier to AI features than to the general product because of the upstream-provider dependency.
- ISO/IEC 42001:2023 is the emerging AI-management-system audit standard, structurally analogous to ISO 27001 and complementary to SOC 2 Type II, and increasingly asked-about by EU-facing and regulated-vertical customers; NIST AI RMF 1.0 (and its Generative AI Profile) is a related voluntary framework used for customer-facing security-response alignment. SOC 2 Type II remains the more universally-requested artefact.
- Common failure modes: consumer-tier AI use for business data; missing training-opt-out; inbound-outbound indemnity mismatch; unlisted model-provider sub-processor; hallucination-rate warranty; silent model-version swap invalidating customer commitments; missing EU AI Act Annex XII documentation.
- The consistency check between inbound and outbound AI paper is a quarterly legal-ops discipline; each outbound customer commitment must have an inbound provider commitment that supports it, or the corporation documents the gap as balance-sheet exposure and sizes insurance accordingly.
- The chapter defers privacy-law depth to [mod-110](../mod-110-privacy-data-governance-and-sector-compliance/), the internal AI-usage policy to [mod-108](../mod-108-culture-employee-experience-and-dei/), SecReview and insurance mechanics to [mod-112](../mod-112-enterprise-risk-insurance-and-compliance/), and AI-model-risk technical depth to the non-role tracks (`chief-ai-officer-learning`, `head-of-ai-governance-learning`, `ai-risk-engineer-learning`).
