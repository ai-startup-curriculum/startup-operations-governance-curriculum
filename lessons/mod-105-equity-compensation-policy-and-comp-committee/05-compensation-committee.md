# 5. The compensation committee

> The compensation committee is the structural answer to a Delaware problem — the CEO cannot set the CEO's own pay without colouring the entire-fairness ink. A chartered, independent-leaning subset of the board makes the decision, documents the deliberation, and gives the full board a defensible record.

## Motivation

Setting executive compensation is one of the few recurring board acts where the controlling party — the CEO, and often the founder-CEO — would otherwise sit on both sides of the transaction. The CEO's pay package is a transaction between the corporation and the CEO; the CEO proposing it, the CEO voting on it, and the CEO's hand-picked directors ratifying it is not a defensible deliberation under Delaware's duty-of-loyalty jurisprudence. The *process* used to approve exec comp is as important as the amount approved. Delaware's entire-fairness doctrine — reviewed in [mod-111 chapter 05 — fiduciary duties](../mod-111-corporate-governance-board-operations-and-officer-duties/05-fiduciary-duties-of-directors.md) — shifts the burden to the fiduciary whenever a conflicted transaction lacks procedural safeguards. The compensation committee is the dominant procedural safeguard the governance world has adopted to earn back the business-judgment presumption when exec comp is on the table.

The committee also does ordinary operational work — administering the equity plan, approving the quarterly refresh and promotion grant batches, approving benchmarking vendors, authoring the exec-comp disclosures that eventually appear in the S-1 and the first proxy statement. The committee shows up early (sometimes informally at Series-A, formally at Series-B / C) and then rebuilds itself to a three-independent-director body in the twelve-to-eighteen months before an IPO. This chapter walks through why the committee exists, what its charter contains, how it evolves across stages, how it operates on the quarterly cadence, and how it interacts with the other standing committees.

The equity-plan context for most of the committee's purview is covered in [chapter 01 — equity-plan architecture](./01-equity-plan-architecture.md) and [chapter 04 — the option-pool refresh](./04-option-pool-refresh.md). The committee's annual rhythm — merit, equity refresh, promotion grants — is covered in [chapter 06 — annual comp cycle](./06-annual-comp-cycle.md). The disclosure obligations the committee eventually inherits are covered in [chapter 09 — SEC Reg S-K Item 402 disclosure](./09-sec-reg-sk-item-402-disclosure.md). The structural map of who-owns-what across the governance stack is in [chapter 10 — ownership boundary map](./10-ownership-boundary-map.md).

## Why a compensation committee exists

### The Delaware entire-fairness problem

Under Delaware law, a transaction between the corporation and a fiduciary (the CEO is a fiduciary of the corporation; directors are fiduciaries of the corporation) is a conflicted transaction. The default standard of review for a conflicted transaction is *entire fairness* — the fiduciary bears the burden of proving both fair process and fair price (see *Weinberger v. UOP, Inc.*, 457 A.2d 701 (Del. 1983); *Kahn v. M&F Worldwide Corp.*, 88 A.3d 635 (Del. 2014) for the modern safe-harbour framework). Entire-fairness review is slow, expensive, and uncertain; corporations design process so that it does not apply.

The procedural safeguards that restore the business-judgment presumption include (a) approval by a committee of independent, disinterested directors, (b) that committee's informed deliberation, documented through minutes, and (c) in some fact patterns, informed stockholder ratification. For exec compensation specifically, DGCL § 141(c) authorises the board to delegate authority to committees, and the compensation committee is the specific vehicle that produces the independent-director approval record.

Without a comp committee, every exec-comp decision risks being characterised as a self-dealing transaction with the CEO on both sides. The CEO is the person proposing pay, the CEO's reports are producing the benchmarking, the CEO sits in on the board discussion, and the directors who vote are often the directors the CEO recommended. The comp committee breaks the loop — the committee meets without the CEO when discussing CEO comp, retains its own advisor, and produces its own record.

### Delegation from the full board

The comp committee is a creature of delegation. The full board, acting under DGCL § 141(c) (or the equivalent provision of the corporation's state of incorporation), passes a resolution establishing the committee, delegating specified authority to it, and adopting its written charter. The committee acts within the scope of the delegation; actions outside the scope require full-board ratification. The ownership boundary — what the committee decides on its own versus what returns to the full board — is addressed later in this chapter and in [chapter 10](./10-ownership-boundary-map.md).

### Independence posture at pre-IPO

Independence is a continuous property, not a binary. At Series-A the committee (if it exists) is "independent" only in the nominal sense that the directors are not W-2 employees of the corporation. At Series-C the committee has shifted toward investor and independent directors with the CEO as a non-voting attendee. Pre-IPO, the committee reconstitutes itself to three fully independent directors who meet the stock-exchange standard; this transition is sequenced to be complete before the S-1 is filed, and in any case within the cure period allowed by the exchange after listing.

## The committee charter

The charter is a written document — approved by the full board and typically published on the corporation's investor-relations site at IPO — that defines the committee. Charter template structure and content standards are discussed in NACD, AICPA, and bar-association resources <!-- needs-research: cite specific NACD and AICPA compensation-committee charter templates and publication years. -->; the stock-exchange listing standards (NYSE § 303A.05(a) and Nasdaq Rule 5605(d)(1)) require the committee to have a written charter at listing.

### Required contents

A defensible comp-committee charter addresses, at minimum:

- **Composition** — number of members (typically three), independence standard, chair, Secretary (often the General Counsel or Corporate Secretary; see [mod-111 chapter 03](../mod-111-corporate-governance-board-operations-and-officer-duties/03-corporate-secretary-function.md)).
- **Independence standard** — the applicable standard (stock-exchange listing rule, Dodd-Frank § 952, and the SEC Rule 10C-1 factors; see below). Pre-IPO the standard is often "non-employee" plus the board's own judgment; post-IPO it is the listing standard.
- **Cadence** — minimum meeting frequency (typically quarterly, "and as needed"), executive-session norms (meeting without management when discussing CEO comp), and ability to meet by phone or action by unanimous written consent.
- **Authority and purview** — scope of delegated decision-making (exec-comp approval, equity-plan administration, annual benchmarking approval, authoring the Compensation Discussion & Analysis section of the proxy pre-IPO), and the matters reserved to the full board.
- **Reporting to the full board** — standing agenda item at each full-board meeting; written report of committee actions circulated with the full-board materials.
- **Advisor engagement authority** — the committee's authority to retain compensation consultants, outside counsel, and other advisors directly, and the independence-assessment requirement under Dodd-Frank § 952(b) and SEC Rule 10C-1(b)(4).
- **Member retainer and compensation** — directors' retainer for committee service, chair premium, meeting fees if any. (The nominating / governance committee owns director comp; the compensation committee usually advises.)
- **Secretary and minutes retention** — who records minutes, where minutes are stored (corporate-secretary minute book), the retention period, and the approval cadence (minutes approved at the next meeting).
- **Annual self-evaluation** — the committee reviews its own performance annually; required for exchange-listed companies and a defensible pre-IPO discipline.

### Charter sources

The template universe includes the NACD *Director's Handbook* series, the AICPA audit-and-governance resource library, the Society for Corporate Governance committee-charter benchmarks, and the public charters of recently-IPO'd companies in the corporation's peer set. <!-- needs-research: cite the current editions of NACD Director's Handbook on Compensation and the AICPA / Society for Corporate Governance comp-committee template materials. --> Many venture-stage corporations adopt a form charter from outside counsel at the Series-B board meeting that first establishes the committee, then revise toward the stock-exchange-compliant form as part of IPO-readiness work.

## Pre-IPO independence trajectory

### Series-A — no separate committee

At Series-A there is typically no standing compensation committee. The full board acts on all comp matters — initial exec-comp packages, option grants, equity-plan amendments. Common full-board composition is the founder-CEO, one additional founder-director, and one or two lead-investor directors (the Series-Seed investor director sometimes retains a board seat; the Series-A lead takes a new seat). "Independence" at this stage is nominal — no director is an employee, but no director is independent of the investment or founding relationships that produced the board.

Comp matters at Series-A are usually handled at the regular quarterly board meeting. The founder-CEO proposes, the board discusses, the board approves by unanimous written consent or at the meeting. The record is adequate because at Series-A the amounts are small, the counterparty expectations are modest, and no shareholder has standing to sue over an inside director's $220k base salary.

### Series-B / Series-C — committee forms

By Series-B (and certainly by Series-C), the board constitutes a compensation committee. Typical composition: two or three directors, drawn from the lead-investor director(s) and sometimes one of the independent / observer directors added at the Series-B or C round. The CEO occasionally sits on the committee as a member; more commonly, the CEO attends as a non-voting participant and exits for executive sessions on CEO compensation.

The purpose at this stage is partly procedural (building the habit, producing the minutes, adopting the benchmarking discipline) and partly substantive (approving the exec-comp program, administering the increasingly complex equity plan, running the first option-pool refresh and amendments described in [chapter 04](./04-option-pool-refresh.md)). The Series-B / C committee also begins engaging a comp-benchmarking firm — Compensia, Radford, Pave, or Carta — as discussed below.

### Pre-IPO — three independent directors

In the twelve-to-eighteen months before S-1 filing, the committee reconstitutes itself to meet the full exchange and SEC independence standards.

- **NYSE Listed Company Manual § 303A.05** requires listed-company compensation committees composed solely of independent directors, with the independence determination made under § 303A.02 considering the director's relationships with the company and any affiliated-party relationship.
- **Nasdaq Rule 5605(d)** requires the same for Nasdaq-listed companies, with Nasdaq-specific independence factors in Rule 5605(a)(2) and the compensation-committee-specific additional factors in Rule 5605(d)(2)(A).
- **Dodd-Frank § 952 (15 U.S.C. § 78j-3)** directs the SEC to require stock exchanges to adopt enhanced compensation-committee independence standards.
- **SEC Rule 10C-1 (17 C.F.R. § 240.10C-1)** implements § 952 by requiring the exchange standards to consider (i) the source of compensation of the director, including any consulting, advisory, or other fees paid by the company, and (ii) whether the director is affiliated with the company, a subsidiary, or an affiliate of a subsidiary.

The exchanges allow a *cure period* for newly-public companies — a transition window post-IPO (typically up to one year to full three-independent-director composition under NYSE § 303A.00 and Nasdaq Rule 5615(b), with phase-in milestones at listing, 90 days, and 1 year). <!-- needs-research: confirm the current phase-in schedule under NYSE § 303A.00 and Nasdaq Rule 5615(b) for newly-public comp-committee composition. --> Many corporations target full independence at listing anyway to avoid the perception of a weak governance posture at the pricing roadshow.

## Composition specifics

### Who sits on the committee

The modern compensation committee for a pre-IPO or newly-public corporation looks for three director profiles:

- **A chair with prior comp-committee or former-CEO experience.** The chair runs the executive sessions, interacts directly with the comp-consultant, and signs the compensation-committee report in the proxy. Pattern-matching to other boards' comp-committee processes is a specific chair-level skill.
- **A member with public-company exec-comp experience** — ideally a current or former CFO, Chief People Officer, or sitting-board compensation-committee member elsewhere. This member provides the technical floor on tax (Section 162(m) deductibility, Section 409A deferred comp, ISO limits), accounting (ASC 718 expense), and disclosure (Reg S-K Item 402).
- **A member who also serves on the audit committee** — the overlap is standard governance practice. Comp decisions touch financial-statement line items (stock-based comp expense, change-in-control accruals, severance), and having a single director with visibility into both bodies reduces coordination failures. The dual-committee director is often financially literate (a NYSE § 303A.07 or Nasdaq Rule 5605(c)(2) requirement for audit committee).

### Who should not sit on the committee

The CEO; any management employee; any director with a material consulting relationship with the corporation; any director affiliated with a vendor, customer, or significant investor whose own compensation decisions might be affected by the committee's work. Lead-investor directors are a nuanced case — they often sit on comp committees pre-IPO (and this is accepted practice at Series-B and C), but they typically cycle off before the IPO independence transition because they fail the SEC Rule 10C-1 affiliation prong.

## Cadence

### The quarterly minimum

The committee meets at least quarterly. Many pre-IPO committees meet five to six times per year once the annual comp cycle is active. A representative annual rhythm:

- **Q1 — annual-comp-cycle approval and exec bonuses.** The committee approves the merit increases for the full employee population (often reviewing by department and by level rather than individual-by-individual below the executive layer), approves exec base-salary adjustments, approves cash bonus payouts for the prior year against the approved program, and approves the annual exec equity-refresh grants. See [chapter 06 — annual comp cycle](./06-annual-comp-cycle.md) for the full operating model.
- **Q2 — refresh / promotion grants and mid-year comp-trends review.** The committee approves the mid-year promotion-grant batch (if any), reviews comp-trend data from the benchmarking provider for the mid-year refresh, and reviews the equity-pool consumption rate (burn rate) against plan.
- **Q3 — benchmarking refresh and annual-plan preparation.** Compensia / Radford present the fall benchmarking data. The committee reviews the peer-group composition, the comp-philosophy document, and the preliminary parameters for the next year's annual comp cycle.
- **Q4 — next-year budget and equity-pool-forecast review.** The committee approves the exec-comp components of the next year's operating budget (base, target bonus, equity-refresh target as a percentage of base), reviews the equity-pool forecast for the coming year including any required option-pool refresh (see [chapter 04](./04-option-pool-refresh.md)), and approves the preliminary CEO performance review for the full-board annual CEO-evaluation discussion.

### Trigger-driven meetings

The committee also meets off-cycle for specific events:

- **New-executive hire.** Each new C-suite or SVP hire's offer package is approved by the committee (usually by unanimous written consent for speed) rather than waiting for the next quarterly meeting.
- **Change-in-control event planning.** M&A activity, a secondary-tender event, or acceleration-trigger negotiations drive a special-purpose meeting.
- **Option-pool refresh.** When the full board is going to be asked to approve a share-pool increase to the equity plan, the committee pre-approves the recommendation.
- **10b5-1 window planning.** Pre-IPO this is less common; post-IPO, the committee frequently reviews and approves Section 16 officer 10b5-1 plans and coordinates with the insider-trading policy ([mod-111 chapter 07](../mod-111-corporate-governance-board-operations-and-officer-duties/07-insider-trading-policy-and-10b5-1-programme.md)).

## Authority — what the committee approves on its own

The precise delegation is set in the charter and in the board resolutions establishing the committee. A common delegation pattern:

- **Approved by the committee alone** — all IC equity refresh and promotion grants under a specified size threshold (e.g., any grant below the top-executive-tier size); annual merit increases below the executive layer; cash-bonus payouts for non-executive employees against the approved program; routine administrative actions under the equity plan (acceptance of exercise notices, waiver of transfer restrictions, settlement of RSU withholding mechanics); engagement of comp-benchmarking vendors and other advisors; approval of the compensation philosophy document.
- **Recommended by the committee, approved by the full board** — all C-suite and Section 16 officer new-hire packages, including base, target bonus, equity, and severance terms; annual CEO comp (compensation and performance review); any change-in-control-related compensation decisions; the compensation-committee report that will appear in the proxy.
- **Requires shareholder approval** — equity-plan amendments that materially increase the share pool (Rule 701 pre-IPO, and NYSE § 303A.08 / Nasdaq Rule 5635(c) post-IPO); re-pricings (an exchange-listing requirement under Nasdaq Rule 5635(c)(4) and NYSE § 303A.08); the plan itself at adoption (and at subsequent material amendments).

The ownership boundary map in [chapter 10](./10-ownership-boundary-map.md) collects the full table. The practical rule: anything that touches the CEO's own compensation, anything that is material enough to be board-visible disclosure in the eventual proxy, and anything that amends the equity plan structurally returns to the full board (and in the shareholder-approval cases, to the stockholders).

## The practical materials pattern

Every comp-committee meeting runs on a standard materials pack. The specific composition varies by stage, but the structural template is stable.

### Standard materials pack

- **(a) Benchmarking refresh.** Compensia, Radford, Pave, or Carta data on the corporation's peer group — base, target bonus, actual bonus, equity-grant sizing, total direct compensation, by level and by function. Full refresh annually; interim updates quarterly if the market is moving.
- **(b) Exec-comp proposal from CEO + Head of People.** The CEO and the Head of People (or Chief People Officer) jointly author the proposal for exec-comp actions — new-hire packages, refresh grants, promotions, bonus payouts. The CEO's own compensation is proposed by the committee chair with input from the Head of People, after the CEO is excused from the executive session.
- **(c) IC equity-pool forecast.** The equity administrator (Carta, Pulley, Shareworks) produces the pool-consumption model — shares granted, shares forfeited, shares available, projected burn rate for the next twelve months. See [chapter 04](./04-option-pool-refresh.md).
- **(d) Current-year merit and equity-refresh budget tracking.** The Head of People provides the running tally of approved merit and equity-refresh spend against the budget set at the start of the cycle.
- **(e) Proposed resolutions.** Pre-drafted resolutions for the specific actions on the agenda, usually produced by the General Counsel or Corporate Secretary. The committee approves resolutions substantively equivalent to the drafts (sometimes with wording changes made in the meeting), and the Secretary records the approved form in the minutes.

### Timing and retention

Materials are circulated to committee members 5 business days before the meeting (the stock-exchange-governed cadence post-listing; the pre-IPO default should match). Materials pass through a BoardSuite / Diligent / Nasdaq Boardvantage portal rather than email for board-communications-privilege and discovery-posture reasons. Meeting minutes are drafted by the Secretary, approved at the next meeting, and retained in the corporate-secretary minute book per the record-retention policy ([mod-111 chapter 03](../mod-111-corporate-governance-board-operations-and-officer-duties/03-corporate-secretary-function.md)).

## Compensia / Radford advisor engagement

### When the firm is retained

Compensation-benchmarking firms are typically retained starting at Series-B, when exec-comp benchmarking becomes board-visible and the corporation needs defensible third-party data rather than a founder-CEO's or Head of People's internal survey. Compensia is the dominant firm in the venture-stage private-company market; Radford (Aon) is the dominant public-company firm with a growing late-stage private-company presence; Pave and Carta provide self-service benchmarking products that have displaced some of the lower-end traditional-firm engagements. <!-- needs-research: cite recent market-share or usage data for Compensia, Radford, Pave, and Carta in the venture-stage comp-benchmarking market. -->

### Fee structure and scope

The typical engagement model is an **annual retainer plus per-project fees**. The retainer covers the fall benchmarking refresh and the committee's standing advisor relationship; per-project fees cover specific exec-hire benchmarking, CoC-event advisory work, pre-IPO comp-program design, and the authoring of the compensation discussion & analysis section of the S-1. <!-- needs-research: cite typical fee ranges for Compensia and Radford engagements at Series-B through pre-IPO. -->

### Independence assessment

**Dodd-Frank § 952(b) (15 U.S.C. § 78j-3(b))** requires the compensation committee to assess the independence of any compensation consultant, outside legal counsel, or other adviser before retention, and annually thereafter. **SEC Rule 10C-1(b)(4) (17 C.F.R. § 240.10C-1(b)(4))** specifies the six factors the committee must consider:

1. Other services the adviser's firm provides to the issuer;
2. Fees received by the adviser's firm from the issuer as a percentage of the firm's total revenue;
3. The adviser's firm's policies and procedures to prevent conflicts of interest;
4. Any business or personal relationship of the adviser with a member of the compensation committee;
5. Any stock of the issuer owned by the adviser; and
6. Any business or personal relationship of the adviser or the adviser's firm with an executive officer of the issuer.

The committee documents the independence assessment in the meeting minutes. For most pre-IPO engagements the assessment is straightforward (the adviser does only comp work for the committee and has no other relationship with the corporation), but the record must exist.

## Comp-committee-report authoring

Pre-IPO, the committee begins drafting the executive-compensation disclosure that will appear in the S-1 and in each subsequent proxy statement. The committee is the author of record of the Compensation Discussion & Analysis (CD&A) section and the Compensation Committee Report required by Reg S-K Item 407(e)(5). The mechanics of Reg S-K Item 402 — the Summary Compensation Table, the Grants of Plan-Based Awards Table, the Outstanding Equity Awards table, pay ratio, pay-versus-performance — are deferred to [chapter 09 — SEC Reg S-K Item 402 disclosure](./09-sec-reg-sk-item-402-disclosure.md).

Flag here: the comp committee is the *author* of the exec-comp disclosures. The audit committee is the audit-financial-statement authority (and reviews the ASC 718 expense that flows through from the comp decisions, but does not author the comp disclosure). The disclosure process at pre-IPO involves committee review of successive drafts prepared by the Corporate Secretary / General Counsel with outside securities counsel and the comp-consultant; the committee signs off on each draft before S-1 filing.

## Interaction with other committees

### Audit committee — clawback ownership

**Dodd-Frank § 954 (15 U.S.C. § 78j-4)** and **SEC Rule 10D-1 (17 C.F.R. § 240.10D-1)** require listed issuers to adopt a clawback policy covering erroneously-awarded incentive compensation. The listing standards (NYSE § 303A.14 and the Nasdaq equivalent in Rule 5608) require the policy and its administration. The audit committee owns the clawback policy and its application in a restatement scenario — not the compensation committee — because the triggering event is an accounting restatement that falls in audit's purview. The compensation committee coordinates on recovery mechanics (which comp was erroneously awarded, how much, from whom) but does not own the policy.

### Governance / nominating committee — director comp

Director compensation is the responsibility of the nominating / governance committee at most mature corporations (though the compensation committee frequently advises). The governance committee owns the director-pay program design; the compensation committee may advise on benchmarking and sometimes approves specific one-time director grants. For the venture-stage corporation that has not yet constituted a separate nominating committee, the full board (or sometimes the compensation committee by default) handles director comp — a transition the IPO-readiness work explicitly addresses.

### Executive session norms

The committee meets in executive session (without any management participant) when discussing CEO compensation. The CEO may attend other portions of the meeting (presenting the exec-comp proposal, discussing the equity-pool forecast, reviewing the benchmarking data), but exits before any discussion of the CEO's own package. The chair runs the executive session; the Secretary (if a management employee) also exits unless the committee asks the Secretary to remain to take minutes. Pre-IPO this norm is sometimes informal; post-listing it is a hard rule.

## A worked example

Nimbus Routing, Inc. — a fictional Series-B SaaS corporation ~95 employees, $34M Series-B closed six months ago, Delaware C-corp — adopts its first compensation committee at the board meeting following the Series-B close.

**Charter — key terms.** The charter, drafted by outside counsel (Fenwick) off the firm's standard Series-B comp-committee template:

- **Composition.** Three members: an independent chair (a former VP People at a public SaaS corporation, added to the board at Series-B as an independent director); the Series-B lead-investor director (a partner at the lead-investor firm); and one founder-director (the CTO co-founder). The CEO is a non-voting invitee for the open session; executive sessions on CEO compensation exclude the CEO. The charter commits to a transition to three fully independent directors within twelve months (replacing the lead-investor director with a second independent and replacing the founder-director with the founder-CEO only during the open session as an invitee).
- **Cadence.** Quarterly minimum, plus action by unanimous written consent for new-exec hire packages and off-cycle approvals.
- **Delegated authority.** Approval of all IC and manager-level equity grants (refresh and promotion) up to 50,000 shares per grant; approval of merit increases and cash bonuses for non-executive employees; approval of comp-benchmarking vendors; administration of the 2024 Stock Option and Incentive Plan.
- **Full-board matters.** All C-suite and VP-and-above new-hire packages; annual CEO comp; equity-plan amendments; any change-in-control-related comp actions; share-pool refreshes.
- **Advisor.** Authority to retain Compensia for comp benchmarking and advisory services, with the independence assessment per Dodd-Frank § 952(b) documented at engagement and annually.
- **Secretary.** The General Counsel acts as Secretary; minutes are approved at the following meeting and retained in the corporate minute book.

**Operating pattern — first year.** The committee meets five times in year one — Q1 (adopts the 2025 comp philosophy, approves annual merit for all non-executive employees, recommends CEO and C-suite base and bonus to the full board); Q2 (approves the mid-year promotion grant batch); Q3 (reviews the fall Compensia refresh, approves peer-group composition of ~15 peers chosen by Compensia and committee-reviewed); Q4 (approves the 2026 equity-refresh budget and the Series-B pool-refresh recommendation for full-board action); and one off-cycle UWC meeting to approve the SVP of Engineering new-hire package.

**Materials pack.** Standard template — Compensia benchmarking refresh, CEO + Head of People exec-comp proposal, Carta equity-pool forecast, Head of People merit/refresh budget tracker, Fenwick-drafted resolutions. Circulated five business days before each meeting via the Diligent portal.

**Twelve-month trajectory.** The lead-investor director cycles off the committee at the first anniversary; the board adds a second independent director (a sitting-CFO of another public SaaS corporation) in the Series-C round, which becomes the new committee member alongside the independent chair and the founder-director. Twenty-four months later, pre-IPO, the founder-director rotates off and a third independent director (a former public-company Head of People) is added, achieving the three-independent-director composition required by NYSE § 303A.05 at the time of listing.

## Summary

- The compensation committee exists primarily to solve the Delaware entire-fairness problem that would otherwise arise when the CEO sets the CEO's own pay; an independent-director, properly chartered, properly documented committee restores the business-judgment presumption for exec-comp decisions.
- The charter — composition, independence standard, cadence, delegated authority, reporting, advisor engagement, Secretary and minutes retention — is the written definition of the committee. NYSE § 303A.05, Nasdaq Rule 5605(d), Dodd-Frank § 952 (15 U.S.C. § 78j-3), and SEC Rule 10C-1 (17 C.F.R. § 240.10C-1) govern the independence standard for listed-company committees.
- Independence evolves across stages — Series-A has no separate committee (the full board acts); Series-B / C stands up a 2-3 director committee with investor and sometimes founder participation; the twelve-to-eighteen months before S-1 filing reconstitute the committee to three fully independent directors.
- The committee runs on a quarterly cadence with a standard materials pack (benchmarking, exec-comp proposal, equity-pool forecast, budget tracker, proposed resolutions) circulated five business days before each meeting; off-cycle meetings handle new-exec hires, pool refreshes, and change-in-control events.
- Compensia / Radford / Pave / Carta are retained starting at Series-B for comp benchmarking; the committee performs the Dodd-Frank § 952(b) / SEC Rule 10C-1(b)(4) six-factor independence assessment at engagement and annually, documented in the minutes.
- Boundary interactions: the audit committee owns clawback under Dodd-Frank § 954 / SEC Rule 10D-1; the governance / nominating committee owns director comp; the comp committee is the author of record of the executive-compensation disclosures (CD&A and Compensation Committee Report under Reg S-K Item 407(e)(5)) that appear in the S-1 and proxy — covered in [chapter 09](./09-sec-reg-sk-item-402-disclosure.md).
